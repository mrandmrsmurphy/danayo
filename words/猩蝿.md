## Notes
- [猩](../characters/猩.md) "red ape; vivid red" + [蝿](../characters/蝿.md) "fly" — fruit fly; *Drosophila* spp.; the red-eyed fly

The name traces through the Japanese 猩々蠅 (ショウジョウバエ, shōjōbae) — "the fly of the 猩々," a legendary red-faced ape spirit of Chinese origin.  猩蝿 is a Dan'a'yo shortening, so it is not a word attested in the Chinese or Korean dictionaries. The name was given because the vivid red eyes of *Drosophila* recall the scarlet face of the 猩々; the compound 猩蝿 shortens this to two characters, keeping the key reference. Chinese uses 果蝇 (guǒyíng, fruit fly) or 黑腹果蝇 (the common lab species *D. melanogaster*).

*Drosophila melanogaster* is one of the most important model organisms in the history of biology. Thomas Hunt Morgan's laboratory at Columbia University began using it in genetics research around 1908; its short generation time (ten days at room temperature), large chromosomes, and easy breeding made it ideal for the study of heredity. Morgan's group used *Drosophila* to establish that genes are carried on chromosomes — work that earned Morgan the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The *Drosophila* genome was among the first animal genomes fully sequenced (2000). Today it remains central to research in developmental biology, neuroscience, and aging.

The `japanese` field gives the real Japanese name ショウジョウバエ, written in katakana as the biological name, while the Mandarin xīngyíng, Cantonese sing1 jing4 and Korean 성승 are the compositional readings of the two characters, as the word is a coinage.  蝿 is the Japanese form of the character 蠅 "fly", and the traditional spelling 猩蠅 and the simplified 猩蝇 are listed as aliases.  猩's own `stand_in` is 猩猩, so this word is not its legitimizer.  `kwin` is false because the Dan'a'yo 성잉 differs from the Korean 성승.  Vietnamese ruồi tinh is a native phrase, "fly of the tinh (ape)", not a character-by-character reading.
