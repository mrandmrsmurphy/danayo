## Notes
- [鞠](../characters/鞠.md) "raise, bow" + [躬](../characters/躬.md) "(emperor's) body"
- This word is the stand-in for [鞠](../characters/鞠.md), which is too bounded to stand alone in Dan'a'yo.

鞠 is to bend or curl over, and 躬 is the body or person (and "oneself," as in 躬行 "to do in person"), so 鞠躬 is literally to bend the body: the bow of respect from the waist, made to a superior, at a ceremony or on greeting. It is the ordinary Chinese word for the bow (as in 向觀眾鞠躬 "bow to the audience"), and the word has an old literary pedigree: the *Analects* describes Confucius entering the palace gate with a bowed body (鞠躬如也), and 鞠躬盡瘁 "to bend one's body and exhaust oneself," the idiom for devoted service until death, is attached to the statesman Zhuge Liang.

The Society page cites it as a near-synonym of [拝](拝.md) "to worship, bow," and the War page lists it with 拝 among the words for the gesture, not the weapon. It differs from the deep prostrations of [頓首](頓首.md) and [叩頭](叩頭.md), which bring the head to the ground. Dan'a'yo keeps the shared sense, "bow, bow respectfully." The readings are Mandarin jūgōng, Cantonese guk1 gung1, Japanese kikkyū (きっきゅう), Korean 국궁 and Sino-Vietnamese cúc cung.
