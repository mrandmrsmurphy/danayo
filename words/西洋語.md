## Notes
- [西](../characters/西.md) "west" + [洋](../characters/洋.md) "ocean" + [語](../characters/語.md) "language" — "the languages of the Western world," built on [[西洋]] "the West" (literally "western ocean") plus 語. No stand-in relationship applies (西's own `stand_in` is [[西方]], 洋's is [[大洋]], 語's is [[言語]]).

西洋語 is the East Asian cover term for the languages of Europe and the Euro-American world, seen from the Sinosphere and set against the CJKV languages (see [[中日韓越]]). It follows the pattern of [[西洋]] itself: the "western ocean" that, from China, lay across the sea, became a general name for Europe and then the West. In practice the term mostly means the Indo-European languages of Europe and their colonial offshoots (English, French, German, Spanish, Russian and so on), but it is a cultural-geographic label, not a genealogical one, so it is not strictly the same as "Indo-European"; Hungarian or Finnish, for instance, are European but not Indo-European. The vault's names for individual Western languages ([[英語]], [[法語]], [[西班牙語]], [[露斯亜語]], [[羅馬語]]) are the members of this class.

Dan'a'yo's phonology chapter uses the word as the contrast class for Japanese: where 漢語・韓語・越語 are described as organized around aspirated versus unaspirated initials with central vowels, Japanese is said to be "more similar to 西洋語," organized around a voiced versus voiceless contrast and a set of basic vowels. That is the only use the grammar pages make of it, and this entry takes no further position on typology.

Japanese せいようご (seiyōgo) is a standard dictionary term, defined as the languages of the Western countries, and a close relative of 洋語 (yōgo), which can also mean loanwords of Western origin (compare [[外来語]]). The Mandarin, Cantonese and Korean readings here are the compositional readings of the three characters; the compound is transparent in all three, but it is not asserted here to be a frequent term in each language. No attested Vietnamese equivalent was confirmed, so the `vietnamese` field is omitted.

`kwin` is false: Dan'a'yo 세양요 differs from Sino-Korean 서양어 in the first and last syllables. No homophones (注音 ㄙㄝㄧ⼘ㄫ⼄ is unique), and no syllable-boundary dots are needed, as with [[西洋]] and [[大洋]]: the ㄧ before ⼘ never begins a syllable of its own, and ㄫ never begins one at all.
