## Notes
- [物 (char)](../characters/物%20(char).md) "thing, creature" + [種](../characters/種.md) "kind, type, seed, species" — no stand-in relationship applies here (種's own stand-in is [[種類]]; 物's is the bare word [[物]]).

物種 is "species" in the biological sense: a natural kind of living thing, the unit that a taxonomist counts and names. It is the word behind 物種名 "species designation" in the writing-system chapter, where a name in the Dan'a'yo script is assigned to an organism rather than to a person or a place. The literal build is "kind of thing": 物 is "thing" (in biology, "creature, organism"), 種 is "kind, sort, seed".

The word is primarily Chinese. Mandarin wùzhǒng (Cantonese mat6 zung2) is the ordinary scientific term, as in 物種多樣性 "species diversity", 瀕危物種 "endangered species", and the Chinese title of Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, 《物種起源》. Vietnamese has the Sino-Vietnamese *vật chủng* recorded in the Hán-Việt dictionaries as "the lineage or kind of each species", but the living word for a biological species is the native *loài*, and *vật chủng* is learned or literary. Korean and Japanese do not use the compound this way: Japanese says bare 種 (しゅ) or 生物種 (*seibutsushu*), and the Darwin title is 『種の起源』; Korean likewise says bare 종 or 생물종, and the title is 『종의 기원』. A Sino-Korean reading 물종 is the compositional reading of the characters, and I found no evidence that Korean uses it as a word of its own.

**Meaning clash in Japanese — flagged.** The string 物種 already exists in Japanese, but it is a different word. Written 物種 and read *monodane*, it means "the source or root of a thing" (and, more narrowly, "seed"), and it survives chiefly in the proverb 命あっての物種 (*inochi atte no monodane*, "life is what everything else depends on"). A Japanese reader meeting 物種 will therefore think of that proverb, not of biology. The Sino-Japanese reading ぶっしゅ in the `japanese` field is the compositional on'yomi of 物 + 種; it is cited in Japanese sources as a historical (Tang-era Buddhist) term for "kinds of things", but it is not a living word for "species", and the field is given here only to fill the sphere-wide pattern. It does not overrule the Dan'a'yo sense, which is the Chinese one, and it should not be read as evidence that Japanese uses 物種 for "species".

**Dan'a'yo sense and its neighbours.** Dan'a'yo already has [[種階]] "species", the lowest standard rank in the eight-rank taxonomic hierarchy beside [[属階]], [[科階]] and the rest (see [[分類学]]). The two words divide the work: 種階 names the rank, the slot in the chart; 物種 names the thing that occupies it, an actual species of organism. In sentences such as "this species is endangered" or "name the species", 物種 is the right word; in "the rank of species lies below genus", 種階 is. [[種類]] is the general "kind, type, hypernym" and is not restricted to living things.
