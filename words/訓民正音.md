## Notes
- [訓 (char)](../characters/訓%20(char).md) "to instruct" + [民](../characters/民.md) "the people" + [正 (char)](../characters/正%20(char).md) "correct" + [音](../characters/音.md) "sound" — "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people." The last two characters are the existing word [[正音]]. None of the four characters' `stand_in` points here (訓's and 正's are themselves, 民's is [[人民]], 音's is [[音楽]]), so no legitimizer relationship applies. `kwin` is false because Dan'a'yo 훈민징움 differs from Korean 훈민정음 in the third syllable (징 against 정), the same difference seen in [[正音]] and [[朝鮮正音]].

訓民正音 (Hunminjeongeum) is the name of the Korean writing system as King Sejong the Great ([[世宗]], r. 1418–1450) first introduced it, and also the name of the document that announced it. The script itself was presented at court in the twelfth lunar month of 1443, and the text titled 訓民正音 was published in 1446. It opens with a short royal preface, famously beginning with the observation that the speech sounds of Korea differ from those of China and do not fit Chinese characters, and then gives the letters with brief notes. A longer explanatory companion, the 訓民正音解例 (Hunminjeongeum Haerye), describes the design of the letters. It was lost for centuries and was only rediscovered in 1940. UNESCO added the Hunminjeongeum manuscript to its Memory of the World Register in 1997. The name 正音 ("correct sounds") reflects the project's aim of writing the sounds of the language precisely, in an alphabet simple enough to be learned by ordinary people, rather than depending on [[漢字]] used for their meaning. The script was later known as [[諺文]] and, in the modern era, as Hangul.

The title is a Sino-Korean phrase, so all five languages use it as a proper noun. Mandarin Xùnmín Zhèngyīn and Cantonese fan3 man4 zing3 jam1 are orthographic borrowings of the Korean title, read in the local pronunciation, and Japanese くんみんせいおん is the on'yomi reading. Vietnamese Huấn dân chính âm is the compositional Sino-Vietnamese form. The Korean reading 훈민정음 is the same in both the North and the South. Like its parent word [[漢文]], it belongs to the class of names that tie a script to a particular tradition.

For Dan'a'yo this name has a role beyond history. The guiding maxim of Dan'a'yo phonology, [[朝鮮正音]], deliberately echoes it, and the phonology chapter of the grammar cites 訓民正音 as the precedent for choosing a language's sounds and script on purpose. The Dan'a'yo sense is the same as the shared one: a proper noun for the 1446 proclamation and the script it introduced, and it is not used as a general term for the script, for which [[諺文]] remains the word.
