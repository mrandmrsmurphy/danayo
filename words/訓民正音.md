## Notes
- [訓 (char)](../characters/訓%20(char).md) "to instruct" + [民](../characters/民.md) "the people" + [正 (char)](../characters/正%20(char).md) "correct" + [音](../characters/音.md) "sound" — "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people." The last two characters are the existing word [[正音]]. None of the four characters' `stand_in` points here (訓's and 正's are themselves, 民's is [[人民]], 音's is [[音楽]]), so no legitimizer relationship applies.

Wiktionary: Chinese and Japanese, historical, "Hunmin Jeongeum", the document introducing the hangul script for Korean in 1446; Chinese also lists an obsolete sense, an alternative name for 諺文 "hangul"; Korean 훈민정음 and Vietnamese Huấn dân chính âm are the Han forms of the same title.

Wikipedia: Hunminjeongeum, lit. "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People", is a 1446 work that formally introduced the first native Korean alphabet, which was originally also called Hunminjeongeum. The base text was first published around October 1446 and authored by King Sejong the Great ([[世宗]], r. 1418–1450), written in Classical Chinese. The commentary Hunminjeongeum Haerye was lost and forgotten until its rediscovery in 1940, and a copy of the full text entered the UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 1997. The script is now called Hangul, in Dan'a'yo [[諺文]].

For Dan'a'yo, the guiding maxim [[朝鮮正音]] echoes this title, and the last two characters are the existing word [[正音]]. It is a proper noun for the 1446 work and the script it introduced, not a general term for the script, for which 諺文 remains the word.
