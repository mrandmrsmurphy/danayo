## Notes
- [愛 (char)](../characters/愛%20(char).md) "love" + [人 (char)](../characters/人%20(char).md) "person" — neither character's own `stand_in` points here (both are bare self-standing characters). `kwin: false` per the AND-rule: `characters/愛 (char).md` is `kwin: true`, `characters/人 (char).md` is `kwin: false` → compound `false`.
- **A striking cross-linguistic sense divergence**: 
	- in mainland Mandarin (the HSK-standard usage), 爱人 àirén is the neutral, standard word for "spouse" (husband or wife), NOT a dating partner or illicit lover — a common trap for learners (verified via search); 
	- Taiwan/Hong Kong Mandarin instead uses it closer to "lover," sometimes with an illicit connotation. 
	- Korean 애인 and Vietnamese người yêu both mean "boyfriend/girlfriend" (a neutral, unmarried dating partner). 
	- Japanese あいじん, however, specifically and commonly means "mistress; the other party in an affair" (a post-WWII semantic narrowing — before the war it simply meant "lover," per historical sources found via search). Calling someone's spouse 愛人 in Japanese would wrongly imply an affair.
The four languages have genuinely diverged onto different, sometimes contradictory, senses of the same two characters.  We therefore must impose a fiat 'lover' sense, a mechanical glueing together of the components: love + person.  Boyfriend, girlfriend, partner, affair person, etc. are all possible, possibly illicit, probably sexual in today's day and age.

**2026-09-20**: For the specific, gender-marked, quasi-neutral senses "girlfriend" and "boyfriend," see the Dan'a'yo-original coinages [[愛女]] ("love-woman") and [[愛男]] ("love-man") instead — minted by fiat precisely to sidestep this word's cross-linguistic ambiguity.
