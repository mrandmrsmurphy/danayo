## Notes
- [甥 (char)](characters/甥%20(char).md) "nephew" — stand-in for [[甥 (char)|甥]], which cannot appear independently

**Broadened 2026-09-21, user-directed**: 甥 is now Dan'a'yo's general word for "nephew" — either sibling's son — rather than the classical, narrower "sister's child specifically, gender-unmarked" sense. The classical restriction was real: the character is phono-semantic (生 "life, birth," the phonetic component, giving the reading + 男 "male," the semantic component suggesting a male offspring), and classical usage distinguished 甥 (a sister's child, either sex — see below) from 侄/姪 (a brother's child), a careful uterine/agnatic split that English "nephew" doesn't make. But this word's own cross-linguistic notes already documented the evidence for the broader sense before it was adopted: Japanese 甥 (おい, oi) is the standard word for "nephew," and does *not* distinguish a brother's son from a sister's son at all — sex, not lineage, is the relevant distinction, exactly the split Dan'a'yo now follows, paralleling [[姪]]'s own identical broadening on the female side.

Mandarin 甥 (shēng) specifically meant a sister's child in classical usage, but the everyday word is 外甥 (wàisheng) — literally "outside nephew" — the standard colloquial term for a sister's son; 外甥女 (wàishengnǚ) for a sister's daughter. Bare 甥 appears in formal, literary, or compound contexts. Cantonese 甥 (sang1) similarly. Korean uses 생질 (甥姪, saengjil) as the standard term for a sister's child specifically; the bare 생 is the Sino-Korean reading, and Korean 조카 is the real native word for "nephew/niece" generally (already used on [[姪]] for the female side). Vietnamese sinh is the Sino-Vietnamese reading; the everyday Vietnamese word is the native cháu trai.

kwin is false: Dan'a'yo 諺文 상 does not match Korean 생. The Middle Chinese final ɣæŋ (庚二開) gives Dan'a'yo ㄙㄚㄫ (*sang*/상) — preserving the 아 (a) vowel quality — while Sino-Korean yields 생, with an 애 (e) vowel. This a/e split in the 庚 final is a regular phonological correspondence between Dan'a'yo and Sino-Korean. The English gloss "sororal" (from Latin soror, sister) is one of those precision kinship terms that exists because common English "nephew/niece" does not specify the parent's sex — a distinction that classical Chinese encodes by default.
