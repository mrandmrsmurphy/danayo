## Notes
- [甥 (char)](../characters/甥%20(char).md) "nephew" — this word is the stand-in for its own character.

甥 is Dan'a'yo's general word for "nephew" — either sibling's son — rather than the classical, narrower "sister's child specifically, gender-unmarked" sense. The classical restriction was real: the character is phono-semantic (生 "life, birth," the phonetic component, giving the reading + 男 "male," the semantic component suggesting a male offspring), and classical usage distinguished 甥 (a sister's child, either sex) from 侄/姪 (a brother's child), a careful uterine/agnatic split that English "nephew" doesn't make. Japanese 甥 (おい, oi) is the standard word for "nephew" and does *not* distinguish a brother's son from a sister's son at all — sex, not lineage, is the relevant distinction, the split Dan'a'yo follows, paralleling [姪](姪.md)'s identical treatment on the female side.

Mandarin 甥 (shēng) specifically meant a sister's child in classical usage, but the everyday word is 外甥 (wàisheng) — literally "outside nephew" — the standard colloquial term for a sister's son; 外甥女 (wàishengnǚ) for a sister's daughter. Bare 甥 appears in formal, literary, or compound contexts. Cantonese 甥 (sang1) similarly. Korean uses 생질 (甥姪, saengjil) as the standard term for a sister's child specifically; the bare 생 is the Sino-Korean reading, and Korean 조카 is the real native word for "nephew/niece" generally (already used on [姪](姪.md) for the female side). Vietnamese sinh is the Sino-Vietnamese reading; the everyday Vietnamese word is the native cháu trai.

The English gloss "sororal" (from Latin soror, sister) is one of those precision kinship terms that exists because common English "nephew/niece" does not specify the parent's sex — a distinction that classical Chinese encodes by default.
