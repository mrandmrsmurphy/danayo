## Notes
- [牛 (char)](../characters/牛%20(char).md) "cow" — stand-in for [[牛 (char)|牛]], which cannot appear independently

牛 is a 象形 pictograph of an ox viewed frontally, the curving horns forming the character's top strokes — one of the oldest domesticated-animal characters in the corpus, generating both a literal livestock/product family (牛乳 "cow's milk," 水牛 "water buffalo") and a broader "bovine/horned creature" extension applied to unrelated animals by loose resemblance or behavior (犀牛 "rhinoceros," literally "rhinoceros-ox"; 海牛 "manatee/sea cow," a calque shared with English; 蝸牛 "snail," where 牛 evokes the slow, horn-like antennae rather than any bovine trait at all).

The character's most culturally prominent extension is astronomical and legendary: 牛郎星 (Altair) and 牛郎 name the Cowherd of the Weaver Girl myth (織女/牽牛, the Chinese Qixi legend paralleling Japanese Tanabata and Korean Chilseok), where the ox-herding profession lends the star its name across the whole East Asian sky-lore tradition — Vietnamese's own Ngưu Lang Chức Nữ preserves the same pairing, confirming ngưu (rather than the character's other stored variants ngâu/ngõ) as the reading actually alive in that shared cultural reference. Japanese carries a single on'yomi ぎゅう (GYUU, 牛乳 gyūnyū, 牛丼 gyūdon "beef rice bowl" — despite naming a beef dish, not deriving from any "beef" sense of 牛 itself, since Sino-Japanese 牛 covers the whole animal, meat included), while the native 訓読み うし (ushi) is the everyday standalone word for the living animal. `kwin` is false: Dan'a'yo 뉴 (nyu, from Middle Chinese 疑母 ŋ + 尤韻 ɨu) diverges from Sino-Korean 우, which has lost the nasal onset entirely — the same 疑母-onset-loss pattern already seen on this vault's other 疑母 characters. 牛 shares its Dan'a'yo reading with [[柔]] ("tender, soft, weak") — a coincidental phonological convergence, unrelated in meaning.
