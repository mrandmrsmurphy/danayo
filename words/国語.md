## Notes
- [国](../characters/国.md) "nation" + [語](../characters/語.md) "language" — no stand-in relationship applies (国's own is [[国家]]; 語's is [[言語]]). The traditional form 國語 is kept as an alias because 国 is the parent form on its character page.

国語 is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is the point of the page. Literally it is "nation language", and every language that borrowed the word reads it as "the language of the nation" — but which nation, and which language, depends on the speaker. In Japan and Korea it means "our own language" (Japanese and Korean respectively), in Taiwan it means Mandarin, and in Vietnam it names a writing system. The senses are listed below; the first two shade into each other and are often impossible to tell apart.

1. National language; the official or standard language of a state. This is the broad dictionary sense in every language: English Wiktionary glosses Mandarin 國語 as "language specific to or of official usage in a nation", Japanese Wiktionary has "the public language and common language spoken in a country", and Korean Wiktionary has "the speech widely used by the citizens of a country" (with the example that China's 국어 is called 중국어 or 화어). The most concentrated use is Mandarin guóyǔ, the standard Mandarin of Taiwan, in contrast to mainland 普通話 ([[普通話]]). The word is also the pre-1949 name of Mandarin as the standard language of the Republic of China (from memory, not re-checked).
2. A country's own native language, taken as the speakers' default: the school subject and academic field. Japanese 国語 (こくご) is the school subject of Japanese, with 国語辞典 "Japanese dictionary" and 国語学 "Japanese linguistics"; Korean 국어 is the same, with 국어사전 for the Korean dictionary (Korean Wiktionary: Koreans use it for 한국어 [[韓国語]]). In Japanese contexts it contrasts with [[漢文]] (and 古文 [[古文]]), the Classical Chinese that Japanese students also read: 国語 is the vernacular line, 漢文 the Chinese one. English Wiktionary adds that Classical Chinese writers in Japan, Korea and Vietnam used 國語 for their own languages (Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese).
3. Vietnamese quốc ngữ, the modern Vietnamese writing system in the Latin alphabet. Here the "national language" is in practice a script: English and Japanese Wiktionary both give this as the main sense, and the word contrasts with the older [[字南]] (chữ Nôm, characters adapted to write Vietnamese) and with chữ Nho (Classical Chinese in characters). English Wiktionary also lists a historical use of quốc ngữ for chữ Nôm and a literary "national language".

Attestation per language, checked 2026-10-11 against English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean Wiktionary (Vietnamese Wiktionary rate-limited, not read; the Vietnamese entry rests on English Wiktionary):
- Mandarin (Taiwan) guóyǔ: senses 1 and Taiwanese Mandarin; dated or colloquial in mainland China, where 中文 and 普通話 are standard; Hong Kong Cantonese gwok3 jyu5 historically used it for Mandarin; also "Malay" in Malaysia.
- Japanese こくご: senses 1 and 2, plus Mandarin as a Taiwanese borrowing; the dominant sense is the school subject.
- Korean 국어: senses 1 and 2; the ordinary word for Korean as the national language, and for the school subject.
- Vietnamese quốc ngữ: sense 3, with senses 1 (literary) and chữ Nôm (historical).

A separate proper noun, the *Guoyu* ("Discourses of the States"), is a Spring and Autumn period history attributed to 左丘明; English, Japanese and Chinese Wiktionary list it under its own heading as a proper noun, and it is not included as a sense here (see [[春秋時代]]).

Dan'a'yo keeps the shared reading and lets the context choose. For "foreign language" use [[外国語]], for "international language" [[国際語]], for "Japanese" [[日本語]], for "Vietnamese" [[越南語]], for "Chinese (language)" [[中国語]] or [[中文]]; for an unambiguous "language" use [[言語]]. Japanese こくご is a real native word, so the Sino-Xenic and native readings coincide. No homophones (注音 ㄍㄛㄎ·⼄ unique across both words and characters). The dot marks a genuine fork: ㄍㄛㄎ|⼄ could be re-read as ㄍㄛ|ㄎ⼄, as on [[中国語]] and [[外国語]]. The Mandarin, Japanese and Cantonese senses come from the dictionaries above; the Korean account is limited to Korean Wiktionary and is otherwise from memory, not re-checked.
