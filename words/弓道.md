## Notes
- [弓 (char)](../characters/弓%20(char).md) "bow" + [道 (char)](../characters/道%20(char).md) "the Way; discipline" — same 道-suffix pattern as [[書道]], [[柔道]], [[剣道]], [[空手道]]

**弓道** (*gōngdào*, Japanese *kyūdō*) is the Japanese art of archery — the discipline of the bow framed as a path of self-cultivation rather than a purely martial or competitive skill. The 道 suffix (Japanese *dō*) marks it as one of several modern reframings of older craft or warrior traditions into lifelong practices of inner development: alongside [[柔道]] (*jūdō*), [[剣道]] (*kendō*), [[空手道]] (*karate-dō*), and [[書道]] (*shodō*), it belongs to the Japanese cultural category of *dō* disciplines in which physical mastery and moral-spiritual formation are understood as inseparable.

The older term is **弓術** (*kyūjutsu*, "bow technique"), the martial archery transmitted through Japan's medieval warrior class. 弓術 was war-practical: speed, power, mounted accuracy. The transition to 弓道 as the standard term came in the early twentieth century: the name was officially changed from 弓術 to 弓道 in 1919, according to English Wikipedia. The modern art is usually understood as a reformulation of the warrior arts after the Meiji Restoration into vehicles for self- and national-character development, though I did not check that account against a source. The All Nippon Kyudo Federation (全日本弓道連盟), through a committee in 1953, issued the standard Kyudo Manual.

The bow of 弓道 is the **和弓** (*wakyū*), a long asymmetric bow of more than two metres — by far the longest bow in active use among world archery traditions. Its grip sits well below the geometric centre, with roughly two-thirds of the bow above the hand and one-third below. This asymmetry distinguishes it from every other major bow tradition and is generally attributed to the practical constraints of nocking and drawing while mounted. Traditional construction laminates bamboo, wood, and leather through a complex multi-year process; fibreglass and carbon composites are now standard for practice. The prescribed draw method places the arrow on the right side of the bow for right-handed archers — again inverting the convention of most other traditions — and the release uses a gloved thumb-draw (the *yugake*) rather than a finger release.

Eugen Herrigel's *Zen in der Kunst des Bogenschießens* (1948; English: *Zen in the Art of Archery*) introduced 弓道 to a wide Western readership, framing it as a vehicle for Zen enlightenment. Herrigel studied under Awa Kenzō (阿波研造). Yamada Shōji's 2001 essay "The Myth of Zen in the Art of Archery" is cited by English Wikipedia for the argument that the book's Zen framing misrepresents how the art was taught in Japan; I did not check the details of that essay. The book nonetheless fixed the association of 弓道 with Zen in Western popular culture, and that association has in turn influenced how the art is packaged and explained to foreign students.

Korean **국궁** (*gukgung*, 國弓, "national bow") is the cognate traditional Korean archery, using a shorter composite reflex bow of horn, bamboo, and sinew — far more powerful for its size than the *wakyū* and optimised for longer distances. The Sino-Korean compound **궁도** (*gungdo*), the Korean reading of 弓道 itself, is used in Korean for the art form, but 국궁 designates the Korean tradition specifically, which is distinct from Japanese 弓道 in equipment, technique, and competitive format. Vietnamese **cung đạo** is the Sino-Vietnamese rendering; Vietnamese traditional archery is not a living competitive tradition in the same sense, and *cung đạo* is used primarily when referring to the Japanese discipline.

In Dan'a'yo 弓道 names the Japanese discipline of archery as a way of self-cultivation, a specific cultural art, and not archery in general; it sits on the Art lexipedia page with the other martial arts.
