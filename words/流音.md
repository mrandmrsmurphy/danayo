## Notes
- [流](../characters/流.md) "flow" + [音](../characters/音.md) "sound" — no stand-in relationship applies (流's own stand-in is [[流動]]; 音's is [[音楽]]).

流音 is the "liquid consonant": the "flowing sound", the class of consonants, chiefly l and r, whose articulation lets the airstream pass smoothly rather than stopping or rubbing it. It is a standard phonetic term in all four source languages, which is why Dan'a'yo coins it as a unit and does not split it: Mandarin liúyīn, Cantonese lau4 jam1, Japanese りゅうおん (ryūon) and Korean 류음, all glossed "liquid consonant".

In Dan'a'yo the liquid is ㄌ, written /l ~ ɾ/ — a lateral that may be realized as a tap — and it is the one consonant in the inventory that most obviously belongs to this class. The page on phonology uses 流音 where it lists the onsets that may not combine with the w glide: ㄋ, ㄌ, ㄅ, ㄆ and ㄇ, that is, the nasals, the liquid and the bilabials. It sits beside the other manner terms the grammar needs: [[鼻音]] "nasal", [[塞音]] "stop", [[擦音]] "fricative", and the flap 弾指 + 子音 and the trill [[振動音]], which describe how ㄌ's tap realization differs from a repeated trill.

Korean has two forms of the same word. South Korean 유음 drops the initial ㄹ under the 두음법칙 (initial-sound rule), while North Korean 류음 keeps it, and the `korean` field here keeps the North Korean form, 류음, as the vault does everywhere; Dan'a'yo's own 류움 differs from it, so `kwin` is false. Wiktionary and hvdic have no Vietnamese entry for the compound, so the field is omitted. No homophones (注音 ㄌ⼜ㄨㄇ unique across both words and characters).
