## Notes
- [参](../characters/参.md) "three (anti-forgery form)" + [拾](../characters/拾.md) "ten (anti-forgery form)" — the formal, fraud-resistant way of writing the numeral [[三十]] ("thirty").

Ordinary numerals 三, 十, 百, 千, etc. are visually simple and easy to alter with an extra stroke — a single brushstroke can turn 三 into 二 or 五, or 十 into 千. To prevent tampering on financial documents, checks, contracts, and receipts, the Sinosphere developed a parallel set of "anti-forgery" or "formal" numeral characters (大寫數字), of which [[参]]/[[拾]] are the most common substitutes for 三/十. **参拾** is therefore not a separate word so much as a formal orthographic variant of [[三十]], used wherever a number must be written in a way that resists alteration — most prominently on banknote amounts, legal documents, and accounting ledgers.

The practice and its vocabulary are shared across Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, and Korean financial and legal writing, though the degree to which formal numerals are still used in everyday life varies — they remain standard on checks and in many legal and financial instruments, while the everyday numerals are used elsewhere.

Within the vault the word is the formal twin of [三十](三十.md), written the way [肆拾](肆拾.md) is for [四十](四十.md), as the Numbers page records ("the formal register used on checks, contracts, and financial documents to prevent alteration, alongside the everyday 三十/四十"). It does not read exactly as 三十 does: its 注音 is ㄙㄚㄇㄙㄜㄆ (諺文 삼습), using 拾's own reading ㄙㄜㄆ, where 三十 is ㄙㄚㄇㄙㄧㄆ (삼십), so the two differ only in the vowel of the last syllable. The Korean field, 삼십, is the ordinary Sino-Korean reading of the numeral thirty, which is why `kwin` is false for this word, and the Japanese さんじゅう is likewise the reading of 三十. Related is the numeral [壱](壱.md), another formal-numeral form (for one). Dan'a'yo keeps the sense "thirty, in the anti-forgery numeral form." The aliases 參拾 and 叁拾 are the traditional and variant spellings, and the Vietnamese form is tam thập.
