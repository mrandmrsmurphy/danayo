## Notes
- [意](../characters/意.md) "meaning, intent" + [義](../characters/義.md) "meaning, principle" — this word is the stand-in that legitimizes the bound character [義](../characters/義.md), which cannot appear independently.

Korean and Japanese both draw a sharp register/sense line between this compound and its sibling 意味/意味 (imi/의미), which is not shared with the same clarity by Mandarin. 뜻 (the plain native word) and 意味/의미 cover ordinary "meaning" — the meaning of a word, a sentence, a gesture — while 意義/의의 leans specifically toward "significance, importance, value": "이번 회의의 의의" (the significance of this meeting), 意義深い (significant, meaningful in a weighty sense), rather than a word's dictionary definition. Vietnamese ý nghĩa doesn't split the same way — it covers both "meaning" and "significance" without a separate everyday word doing the plainer job, closer to how English "meaning" itself stretches to cover both senses.

English Wiktionary gives the Chinese noun two senses, "meaning; sense" (比喻意義, "figurative meaning") and "significance; importance; value" (生命的意義, "the meaning of life"; 很有意義, "very meaningful"; 意義重大, "crucial"), with [[意思]] as the synonym for the plain sense and 意義深長 as a derived term. Unlike the paragraph above, which says the Korean and Japanese split is not shared by Mandarin, this suggests Mandarin does cover both senses in one word; the Notes' comparison is not from Wiktionary and I have not re-checked it. Japanese 意義 (いぎ) is listed only as "significance", Korean 의의 as "meaning, significance, importance", and Vietnamese ý nghĩa as "meaning".

Mandarin yìyì and Cantonese ji3 ji6 are as at English Wiktionary.

No other word shares the 注音 ㄜㄜㄧ (checked 2026-10-12).
