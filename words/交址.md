## Notes
- [交 (char)](../characters/交%20(char).md) "mix; exchange" + [址](../characters/址.md) "site; location" — no stand-in relationship applies (交's own is the single-character word [[交]]; 址 is a 名専字, a character used only in names, and this is a name). The characters are literally "crossing site", but the word is a place name and the characters are chosen for sound and tradition, not for that reading.

The spelling. The usual writing is 交趾, with 趾 "toe", but 趾 has no page in this vault, so the vault's word is spelled 交址 with 址. This is an attested variant, not an invention: English Wiktionary's entry for 交趾 lists 交阯 and 交址 as its alternative forms, and Chinese Wikipedia's disambiguation page is headed "交趾（又作交阯或交址）". The user spelling 交趾 and the variant 交阯 are both kept as aliases. English Wiktionary has a page for 交阯 ("alternative form of 交趾") but none for 交址, and Vietnamese Wiktionary shows 交址 only on a stray entry for An Nam (see [[安南]]), so 交址 is attested as a variant by Wiktionary's variant list and by Chinese Wikipedia, not by an entry of its own. The three characters 趾, 阯 and 址 each put a different radical (foot, mound, earth) beside the same phonetic 止 and are close in sound and sense, which is how they come to be interchangeable here (from memory, not re-checked; 止 itself originally depicted a foot). Sino-Xenic readings: 址 and 趾 share Mandarin zhǐ and Korean 지, and 址's 注音 ㄐㄧ is the same as 止's.

This name is a Chinese historical place name that is also the Vietnamese one, so the Chinese senses come first.

1. Jiaozhi, the Han commandery (交趾郡), the ancient Chinese commandery in what is now northern Vietnam, first established under the Han dynasty (English Wiktionary). Japanese Wikipedia dates the commandery from the Former Han to the Tang. The Han conquered the region in the 2nd century BC (from memory, not re-checked) and the commandery was part of the province Jiaozhou (交州), which English Wiktionary lists as a related term.
2. Jiaozhi as a generic term for the lands south of the Five Ridges (五嶺: Dayu, Qitian, Dupang, Mengzhu and Yuecheng) in Liangguang, essentially northern Vietnam (English Wiktionary). The oldest quotation there is from the Han Feizi, c. 2nd century BC, which says the land of the sage-king Yao reached south to Jiaozhi; another, in the Mozi, uses the variant 交阯. The name is therefore much older than the Han commandery, and hvdic's dictionary of classical citations calls it a name of Yao's time. English Wiktionary adds that an early form of the Vietnamese name Giao Chỉ seems to have been used by the native states of the Red River delta before the Chinese conquest.
3. Giao Chỉ, the Vietnamese form of the same name, the region in Vietnamese history. Hvdic gives Hán-Việt giao chỉ with the two Chinese senses above, and Nguyễn Quốc Hùng's dictionary there adds that it was also the name of a tribe in northern Vietnam in the time of the Hùng kings. The `vietnamese` field holds Giao Chỉ.

Etymology. English Wiktionary quotes the Book of Rites (the Liji, 禮記: see [[礼記]]), which says in the English Wiktionary rendering that the southern peoples were called Man, tattooed their foreheads and had their feet turned in towards each other (交趾 here being the "crossed toes"), and records the hypothesis of Ferlus (2009) that the first syllable 交 (Old Chinese *kreːw) is not "cross" but an old ethnonym, from the etymon *k.raːw, which also gave 獠 (lǎo, a general term for ethnic groups of southwest China). Which of the two explanations is right is not settled; the "crossed toes" reading is the traditional Chinese folk etymology.

Derived terms (not created in this vault): 交趾支那 (Jiāozhǐ zhīnà; Japanese こうちしな), Cochinchina, the name Europeans and then the French gave to the south of Vietnam and kept for the colony of Cochinchina (English Wiktionary; Japanese Wikipedia names it as the French-period term for southern Vietnam, see also [[安南]]), and 交趾陶 (Chinese) or 交趾焼 (Japanese kōchiyaki), "Cochin ware", a glazed pottery of Chinese, Vietnamese or Southeast Asian origin (Wiktionary; the provenance is from memory, not re-checked).

Readings. Mandarin Jiāozhǐ and Cantonese gaau1 zi2 are as given at English Wiktionary for 交趾. Japanese こうち is its first reading (the reading こうし, kanon, is also given); Japanese Wiktionary lists the word as a Japanese proper noun with both. The `korean` value 교지 is built from the character pages. Korean Wiktionary has no entry.

Attestation, checked 2026-10-11. English Wiktionary: 交趾 (Chinese senses 1 and 2, Japanese こうち, Vietnamese chữ Hán form of Giao Chỉ); 交阯 (Chinese variant and Vietnamese). Chinese Wiktionary: 交趾 as a CC-CEDICT stub (Jiāo zhǐ). Vietnamese Wiktionary: 交趾 (Vietnamese Han form of Giao Chỉ, with a Japanese section) and 交阯 (Chinese historical variant). Chinese Wikipedia: disambiguation page naming 交阯 and 交址 as variants. hvdic.thivien.net: 交趾 giao chỉ. Japanese and Korean Wiktionaries have no entry. No homophones (注音 ㄍ⼄ㄨㄐㄧ unique across both words and characters).
