## Notes
- [諸 (char)](../characters/諸%20(char).md) "various" + [国](../characters/国.md) "nation" — "various countries; the nations." 諸 is legitimized by [[諸]] itself and 国 by [[国家]]; neither stand-in points here, so this is not a legitimizer word. `kwin: false` per the AND-rule: both characters are individually `kwin: false`.

諸国 is the plural-collective of 国: an unspecified but plural set of countries, "the various countries," as in "written in multiple ways across 諸国." Japanese しょこく and Korean 제국 are attested on Wiktionary ("various countries"). Mandarin zhūguó and Cantonese zyu1 gwok3 are compositional readings (諸's zhū / zyu1 plus 国's guó / gwok3), not separately verified against a dictionary entry; the Vietnamese field is omitted because neither Wiktionary nor hvdic has a compound entry.

The pattern follows [[諸語]] ("the various languages") and the older 諸子 ("the various masters"): 諸 + noun forms a loose plurality, not an exhaustive "all." It contrasts with 各国 ("each country," taken one at a time) and with [[他国]] ("another country," defined by exclusion). In Japanese the word also has a historical sense, "all the provinces," since 国 once meant the old provinces of Japan, and the phrase 諸国漫遊 ("wandering through the provinces") preserves it; in classical Chinese it names the several feudal states of a period, as in the Warring States.

One real clash is on the Korean side: 諸國 is read 제국, which is identical to the everyday 帝國 제국 ("empire"). The two are unrelated in meaning and are written differently in hanja, and Dan'a'yo keeps them distinct (자곡 here against ㄊㄝㄧㄍㄛㄎ for [[帝国]]), so no Dan'a'yo homophone arises. Korean-speaking readers should expect the ambiguity in Korean-only contexts.
