## Notes
- [蠢](../characters/蠢.md) "squirm; wiggle; foolish" + [動](../characters/動%20(char).md) "move" — stand-in for [蠢](../characters/蠢.md), which cannot appear independently
- 蠢動 starts with the lowliest kind of motion — the blind wriggling of worms and insects, captured in 蠢's radical 虫 ("bug, worm") and its core sense of squirming, witless movement — and lifts that image into a pointed metaphor for human misbehavior. To say that troublemakers, bandits, or enemy forces are "蠢動-ing" is to liken their stirrings to vermin working their way through the dark: aimless from a distance, but ominous up close, the first restless signs of trouble before it fully breaks into the open. The compound carries this same derogatory shading everywhere it appears — Mandarin chǔndòng and Cantonese ceon2 dung6, Sino-Korean 준동 (jundong, often paired with descriptions of bandits or enemy infiltrators "준동하다"), Sino-Japanese しゅんどう (shundō), and Sino-Vietnamese xuẩn động all describe not mere movement but unwelcome, unsettling movement — the twitch in the grass that tells you something is there before you can see what it is.

The word has two layers that the English glosses keep apart. The literal one is the squirming of small creatures (as in the classical image of insects stirring in spring, from which 蠢 takes its 春 "spring" element), and the figurative one is "to stir up trouble, to act restlessly," said of rebels, bandits and malcontents. 動 contributes the plain notion of movement (see [動](動.md) "to move"). The Efforts page lists it among the words for exertion with the glosses "to wriggle, to stir up trouble, to act restlessly," which is a loose fit for a word whose emphasis is on disquiet and not on effort. Dan'a'yo keeps both layers, the literal wriggle and the figurative stirring of trouble, together as one word, in line with the shared sense. The character 蠢 also means "foolish" (as in the Chinese 愚蠢 "stupid"), a separate sense of the bound character that this compound does not use.
