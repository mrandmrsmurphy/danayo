## Notes
- [玉 (char)](../characters/玉%20(char).md) "jade" — stand-in for [[玉 (char)|玉]], which cannot appear independently

玉 is 象形, depicting either a 琮 (a ritual jade cylinder-and-cube totem used in ancient sacrificial ceremony) or, by an alternate traditional reading, three pieces of jade strung together on a cord — either way, the character's origin is inseparable from jade's ceremonial and aristocratic status in early Chinese culture, not just its status as a decorative stone. That elevated status carries directly into the character's figurative use: beyond literal jade objects (瓊玉 "fine jade," 玉璽 "the imperial seal," made of jade specifically to mark supreme authority), 玉 became a byword for anything precious, flawless, or beautiful — a semantic generalization visible in 金科玉律 ("golden rules and jade laws," i.e. unbreakable rules) pairing 玉 with 金 as the two most prestige-laden materials in the classical idiom vocabulary. The character's more surprising modern extension is 玉葱 ("onion") — an opaque compound where 玉's "precious/lustrous" association was extended to the pearly sheen of an onion's layers, unrelated to any of the stone senses.

Japanese carries two on'yomi, ぎょく (GYOKU, the default — 玉座 gyokuza "the throne," 玉葱 tamanegi actually uses the native reading instead) and rarer ごく, alongside no native 訓読み listed on the character's own page at all (`japanese_native: ø`) despite だま/たま (tama) being an extremely common and productive native reading in ordinary Japanese (玉葱 tamanegi, 目玉 medama "eyeball") — a real character-page data gap, flagged here rather than corrected, matching the same `ø`-despite-clear-native-life pattern already found on [[手]] earlier in this sweep. Vietnamese ngọc is thoroughly alive in the modern language, both in Sino-Vietnamese compounds (ngọc bích "jade, jasper") and as a common given name element, unlike several other stones/materials in this vault whose Vietnamese readings survive only in learned registers. `kwin` is false: Dan'a'yo 욕 ('yog, from Middle Chinese 疑母 ŋ + 燭韻 ɨok) and Sino-Korean 옥 both preserve the same MC checked coda (written -g/-k in each system respectively), but diverge in the vowel — Dan'a'yo keeps a y-glide before the vowel (yo) that Sino-Korean lacks (o), the one point of mismatch between otherwise near-identical readings.
