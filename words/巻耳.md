## Notes
- [巻 (char)](../characters/巻%20(char).md) "roll, roll up" + [耳 (char)](../characters/耳%20(char).md) "ear"

巻耳 (also written 卷耳, 菤耳) is the title of the third ode of the Shijing (詩經), in the 周南 section — one of the most-discussed plant names in Classical Chinese philology, because its identity has been disputed since antiquity. The Mao commentary tradition and most modern reference works equate it with 苍耳/蒼耳 (Xanthium strumarium, cocklebur), a coarse, tall plant with spiny burr-fruit; this is also the identification followed by later Japanese and Korean commentarial traditions. A significant strand of modern textual-botanical scholarship dissents, however, arguing this is a millennium-old conflation caused by the shared character 耳 ("ear," referring to the ear-shaped fruit) in both plant names — the Erya's description ("shaped like rat's ears, growing in clusters like a plate") and Lu Ji's Jin-dynasty gloss (white flowers, thin vining stems, edible as a bland vegetable) match Cerastium (mouse-ear chickweed, Caryophyllaceae) far better than the coarse, white-flowerless cocklebur (Asteraceae) — and note that Xanthium may not even have been present in pre-Qin China. This vault's English gloss ("field chickweed; field mouse-ear") follows the Cerastium identification; the aliases 苍耳/菤耳 preserve the traditional cocklebur-identified name-forms as attested variants rather than asserting they denote the same plant with certainty.

Mandarin juǎn'ěr (note the polyphonic ㄐㄩㄢˇ juǎn reading of 卷/巻 here, distinct from its usual "roll, volume" reading juàn) and Cantonese gyun2 ji5 read the title directly. Japanese けんじ (kenji) is the classical kundoku on'yomi reading of the poem title (attested in kanbun editions of the Shijing), not a word in ordinary modern use — Japanese instead uses オナモミ (onamomi) as the everyday name for cocklebur itself. Korean 권이 (gwon-i) parallels the Sino-xenic reading in Korean Shijing scholarship, alongside the native plant name 도꼬마리 (dokkomari, "cocklebur"). Vietnamese quyển nhĩ is the standard Hán Việt title used in Vietnamese Kinh Thi (Shijing) scholarship; the cocklebur-identified plant itself is more commonly called ké đầu ngựa natively, with thương nhĩ (蒼耳) and thương nhĩ tử (蒼耳子, the seed/fruit) as the parallel Sino-Vietnamese herbal-medicine terms.
