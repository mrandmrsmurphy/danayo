## Notes
- [罔](../characters/罔.md) "accuse" + [両 (char)](../characters/両%20(char).md) "both" — 借代字, a phonetic-loan spelling: these two characters carry none of their own literal meaning here, standing in purely for their sounds in place of the far rarer, more complex 魍魎 (itself already one of a dozen attested variant spellings of this same word, listed as aliases). Stand-in for [[罔]], which cannot appear independently outside this compound.

罔両/魍魎 (mōryō, wǎngliǎng) names a class of nature-spirit or demon in classical East Asian folklore, specifically associated with mountains, rivers, wood, and stone — and, in the fuller Japanese yōkai tradition the word was borrowed into, described with a distinctive traditional appearance: shaped like a three-year-old child, dark reddish-black, red-eyed, long-eared, and said to feed on the flesh of corpses, robbing graves and disrupting funerals; oak trees and tigers were traditionally believed to ward it off (hence tiger statues historically placed along roads to graveyards). The term appears already in early Chinese sources (including a famous passage in the *Zuo Zhuan* cataloguing the various river- and mountain-spirits that "the people of the Xia" could recognize by depicting them on bronze vessels) well before its adoption into Japanese folklore.

Mandarin wǎngliǎng, Cantonese mong5 loeng5, Japanese もうりょう (mōryō), Korean 망량 (mangnyang), and Vietnamese võng lượng are all standard, attested readings for this word specifically (not merely derived compositionally from 罔/両's own individual readings, since both characters are pure sound-loans here). `kwin` is true: Dan'a'yo 망량 matches Sino-Korean 망량 exactly. No Dan'a'yo homophones (注音 ㄇㄚㄫㄌ⼘ㄫ unique among both words and characters).
