## Notes
- [高 (char)](../characters/高%20(char).md) "high" + [低](../characters/低.md) "low" — no stand-in relationship applies (高 is its own stand-in; 低's is [[低下]]).

高低 is "high and low": the pair taken together as a single quantity, so height, level, and the difference between the top and the bottom. It extends in the usual ways, to pitch (how high or low a note or syllable is), to superiority and inferiority, and, in Mandarin, to an adverb meaning "anyhow, in the end". On the phonology page it is the word for pitch height, in the **high-low principle** (高低原則), the set of rules for which syllable of a phrase carries the high pitch.

The word is a useful route for the "low" half. The bare character 低 is bound in Dan'a'yo and has no word of its own: the stand-alone adjective is [[低下]], opposite of [[高]]. 高低 puts the bound 低 beside 高 in the way the source languages do, as a fixed pair, so the compound needs nothing but the characters themselves.

Mandarin gāodī and Cantonese gou1 dai1 carry all of these senses, and Japanese こうてい (kōtei) is the on'yomi reading, glossed "high and low; rise and fall" (the native kun'yomi たかひく is also recorded). Korean 고저 is the compositional Sino-Korean reading of the same characters and the usual term for pitch height; it was not separately verified in a Korean dictionary. Wiktionary and hvdic have no Vietnamese entry, so that field is omitted, and Vietnamese says cao thấp with native words. No homophones (注音 ㄍㄚㄨㄉㄝㄧ unique across both words and characters).
