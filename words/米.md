## Notes
- [米 (char)](../characters/米%20(char).md) "rice (uncooked)" — stand-in for [[米 (char)|米]], which cannot appear independently

米 is 象形 of scattered grains of husked rice, the four dots/strokes around a central cross representing individual kernels. Beyond the literal grain (米飯 "cooked rice," 玄米 "brown rice," 米粟 "grains, rice and millet"), the character serves this vault's own periodic-table naming convention for fermium via [[費米金]] (a transliteration-based coinage, unlike the descriptive [[火紅素]]/[[石素]]), and — independently of any grain sense at all — as the metric-unit character for "meter" in East Asian scientific/everyday usage (千米 "kilometer," 毫米 "millimeter"), a phonetic borrowing riding on the same syllable with no semantic connection to rice whatsoever.

Japanese carries an unusually wide three-reading split: まい (MAI, used here — 玄米 genmai, 白米 hakumai "polished white rice") is the reading tied to the rice sense, while べい (BEI) covers both the metric-unit sense (メートル-derived compounds) and, entirely independently, serves as the standard abbreviation character for "America" in 米国 (beikoku, "the USA") — a phonetic transliteration unrelated to either the rice or meter senses. め (ME) is the rarest of the three, confined to a small set of classical/technical compounds. Vietnamese distinguishes mễ (used here, the reading for the grain sense, as in mễ cốc "cereals/grains") from mè, stored on the character's own page but actually the modern Vietnamese word for "sesame" — an unrelated homophone rather than a genuine variant reading of 米 itself. `kwin` is false: Dan'a'yo 메 (mei, from Middle Chinese 明母 m + 齊開韻 ei) diverges from Sino-Korean 미 in the vowel. 米 shares its Dan'a'yo reading with two other standalone words, [[迷]] ("be lost, be confused") — already listed among 米's own Derived Characters, a direct phonetic descendant — and [[謎]] ("riddle, mystery, enigma"), itself built on 迷 as its own phonetic component one step further removed, so all three readings trace back to the same phonetic family rather than converging by pure coincidence.
