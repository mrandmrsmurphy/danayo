## Notes
- [広 (char)](../characters/広%20(char).md) "wide" + [東](../characters/東.md) "east" + [語](../characters/語.md) "language" — "Guangdong language," i.e. Cantonese. No stand-in relationship applies (広's own `stand_in` is itself, 東's is [[東方]], 語's is [[言語]]); [[広東]] "Guangdong" is the place name this compound is built on.

広東語 is the language-name compound formed from the place name [[広東]] (Guangdong, "Canton") plus 語 ("language"), the same country/region + 語 pattern as [[中国語]] and [[日本語]]. It names Cantonese, the prestige Yue variety of Guangzhou and Hong Kong. It is closely related to, but not quite the same as, [[粤語]] ("Yue language"): 粤語 names the Yue language group, while 広東語 names the Guangdong-based variety most people mean by "Cantonese" (see the note on that page). In Dan'a'yo's phonology chapter the word is used as the name of the variety that inserts a back close vowel in certain palatalized contexts (`文法 - 02音韻論`, section on the alveolar sibilant /s/).

Cross-linguistically, the compound is attested in Japanese かんとんご (kantongo, built on the Japanese place-name reading Kanton rather than a compositional on'yomi reading, exactly as on [[広東]]) and Korean 광동어 (gwangdong'eo); Wiktionary records 廣東語 as the kyūjitai spelling of 広東語 and notes that under Japanese rule in Taiwan it could also refer to Hakka. In Chinese, the everyday name is 廣東話/广东话 ("Guangdong speech"; Cantonese gwong2 dung1 waa2), with 粵語/粤语 as the formal or academic term; 廣東語/广东语 (guǎngdōngyǔ) exists but is the less common of the three. Vietnamese most often says tiếng Quảng Đông, with the native word tiếng ("language") in front of the Sino-Vietnamese place name, and uses Việt ngữ (粵語) for the Yue name, which can be confused with tiếng Việt, "Vietnamese." The Cantonese, Mandarin and Vietnamese readings given here are the compositional readings of the three characters, and the Vietnamese form is the standard native name rather than a Hán-Việt reading of 廣東語.

`kwin` is false: Dan'a'yo 광동요 differs from Sino-Korean 광동어 in the last syllable (語 reads 요 in Dan'a'yo, 어 in Sino-Korean), the same regular correspondence seen on [[中国語]] and [[韓国語]]. No homophones (注音 ㄍ⺢ㄫㄉㄛㄫ⼄ is unique among words, chengyu and characters), and no syllable-boundary dot is needed: the string admits only one legal partition.
