## Notes
- [書](../characters/書.md) "book; document" + [経 (char)](../characters/経%20(char).md) "classic" — the Classic of Documents, one of the [[五経]]
- Also called the [[尚書]], which is the older name for the same text.
- It's a complete book, so see [[translation/書経 (book)]]

**書経** (*Shūjīng*, "Classic of Documents"; Japanese *Shokyō*, Korean *Seogyeong*, Vietnamese *Thư Kinh*) is the oldest Chinese collection of prose, a compilation of speeches, proclamations, and records attributed to the sage-kings and early dynasties. It is one of the [[五経]] and was studied by every educated person in the classical curriculum. "書" here has its older sense of "writings; documents" rather than "book"; the text is also called [[尚書]] (*Shàngshū*, "Venerable Documents"), the older name, and the later 経 marks its canonical status.

The contents are grouped under four dynastic divisions — 虞書 (Yu), 夏書 (Xia), 商書 (Shang), and 周書 (Zhou) — opening with the 尭典 (Canon of Yao), which the vault's translation begins with. The speeches are the classical source of the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven and of the model of the benevolent ruler.

The transmission is famously contested. The **今文** (New Text) version, reconstructed in the early Han from the memory of the scholar Fu Sheng (伏生), has 28 or 29 chapters; the [[古文]] (Old Text) version, said to have been found in the wall of Confucius's former house ([[孔子]]), had more. The Old Text chapters in the standard edition, presented in the 4th century AD, were shown to be a forgery by Yan Ruoju (閻若璩) in the Qing, which is why the text's authority has been argued over for centuries.

In Dan'a'yo the word is simply the title of this one text, not a generic term for a "classic of documents".
