## Notes
- [孫](../characters/孫.md) "grandchild" + [子](../characters/子.md) "child; (as an honorific) master" — stand-in for [[孫]], which cannot appear independently: the character's meaning (grandchild; descendant) is identical, but 孫 alone is too bounded to stand as a Dan'a'yo word, so 孫子 is its `stand_in` compound

This word has two senses, unrelated beyond the spelling:

1. **grandson** — a son's (or daughter's) son, the sense on which the Kinship placement rests; the feminine counterpart is [[孫女]], and the plural/generic "descendants" is [[子孫]]. Here 子 is the ordinary "child" suffix and 孫 the generational term. Mandarin writes this 孙子 (*sūnzi*, with neutral-tone 子), distinct from the name below; Korean 손자 (*sonja*) is the everyday word for it, while in Japanese the grandchild is native 孫 (*mago*) and そんし is not used for it.
2. **Sun Tzu** — the proper-noun sense below.

As a proper noun, **孫子** (*Sūnzǐ*, "Master Sun") refers both to Sun Wu (孫武, c. 544–496 BC), a military general from the state of Wu during the Spring and Autumn period, and to the strategic treatise attributed to him. The name follows the classical convention of appending 子 ("master") to a thinker's surname as an honorific — the same suffix that marks Confucius (孔子 *Kǒngzǐ*), Mencius (孟子 *Mèngzǐ*), and Laozi (老子 *Lǎozǐ*).

The *孫子兵法* (Art of War) is among the oldest surviving military treatises, organized in thirteen chapters covering terrain, deception, intelligence, and the psychology of command. Its influence stretches far beyond military affairs: it has been central to East Asian statecraft, martial arts traditions, and competitive theory in business and diplomacy alike.

For the text, see [[translation/孫子兵法]].
