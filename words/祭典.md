## Notes
- [祭](../characters/祭.md) "sacrifice; offering; ritual" + [典](../characters/典.md) "canon; ceremony; statute" — stand-in for [祭](../characters/祭.md), which cannot appear independently
- Originally denoting the formal rites by which a community honored its ancestors, deities, or the dead — 祭 the offering itself, 典 the codified form it must take — the compound has widened in modern usage from "sacrificial ceremony" to any grand, ceremonious public gathering: a cultural, artistic, or sporting festival. This semantic drift toward "festival" is most visible in Japanese さいてん (saiten), which today evokes school and town festivals as readily as religious observance, while Mandarin jìdiǎn and Sino-Korean 제전 (jejeon) keep a stronger connection to formal commemorative rites and large-scale public celebrations alike.

It sits in a small family of words about ritual and festival: [祭祀](祭祀.md) is the act of sacrificing and worshipping (the ritual itself), [祭日](祭日.md) is a festival day or holy day in the calendar, and [儀礼](儀礼.md) is ceremonial form and etiquette in general, while 祭典 is the ceremony or festival as an event, with the codified form that 典 implies. Because it spans three domains, it is cited on [Events](../lexipedia/Events.md) (its primary home), [Religion](../lexipedia/Religion.md) (for the sacrificial sense) and [Trade](../lexipedia/Trade.md), where the Rosenfelder "festival" gloss lands. Dan'a'yo keeps both the religious and the public-festival senses together, as the glosses "festival, sacrificial ceremony" record.
