## Notes
- [心 (char)](../characters/心%20(char).md) "heart" — stand-in for [[心 (char)|心]], which cannot appear independently

心 is a 象形 pictograph of a heart, now highly stylized to the point of no longer resembling the organ at a glance — one of the corpus's most productive characters, anchoring an enormous compound family that spans the literal organ (心臓, disambiguating the abstract sense from the physical one), the seat of thought and emotion (心理 "mentality/psychology," 内心 "inner thought/intention," 二心 "duplicity, a divided heart"), and grammaticalized uses in idiom and technical vocabulary alike (離心率 "eccentricity" in mathematics, 点心 "snack/dim sum," 灯心 "lamp wick"). Alone, as a bare word, it names the heart as the seat of mind, feeling, and intention — the abstract/figurative register — in deliberate contrast to [[心臓]], which is reserved for the physical organ.

Across the sphere the mind/organ split is handled differently by each language. Japanese keeps both readings alive with a clean division of labor: しん (SHIN) in Sino-Japanese compounds (心理 shinri, 安心 anshin) versus the native こころ (kokoro), an entire register of its own — poetic, introspective, carrying connotations the borrowed しん compounds don't (心 as the seat of one's authentic feeling, as in 心を込めて "with all one's heart"). Vietnamese tâm likewise belongs to the abstract/spiritual register (tâm lý "psychology," tâm hồn "soul"), while the physical organ is named by the native word tim (trái tim, "the heart" as a body part) — the character's own stored `vietnamese` field lists tim among its readings, but that reading belongs to the organ-sense compound, not this bare abstract word. `kwin` is true: Dan'a'yo 심 (sim, from Middle Chinese 心母 s + 侵A韻 iɪm) coincides exactly with Sino-Korean 심, which itself splits the same way — 심 for the abstract/psychological sense (심리학 "psychology"), 마음 (a native word) for everyday "heart, mind" in casual speech. 心 shares its Dan'a'yo reading with [[尋]] ("inquire for, seek"), an unrelated character whose homophone status is purely coincidental phonology (Middle Chinese 邪母/侵韻 converging on the same Dan'a'yo syllable via a different initial).
