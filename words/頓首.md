## Notes
- [頓](../characters/頓.md) "kowtow" + [首](../characters/首.md) "head" — stand-in for [頓](../characters/頓.md), which cannot appear independently

頓 is to stop abruptly or strike against, and 首 is the head, so 頓首 is to bring the head down to the ground and touch it, a deep bow of the old court and ritual etiquette. It is one of the traditional named bows (a brief touch of the forehead, in contrast to the prolonged prostration of the fullest forms), and so is closely related to [叩頭](叩頭.md) "to kowtow," which can be said of the same gesture and is the more general word.

The word has a second, very common life as an epistolary formula. At the end of a formal classical letter 頓首 (often 頓首再拝) is the writer's closing flourish, "I bow my head to the ground," placed after the signature to show deference, the way "respectfully yours" does in English. That sense is preserved in Chinese and Japanese letter-writing manuals. The Body page lists it as the vault's one concrete gesture, a body motion that has no general word of its own. The readings are Mandarin dùnshǒu, Cantonese deon6 sau2, Japanese tonshu (とんしゅ), Korean 돈수 and Sino-Vietnamese đốn thủ. Dan'a'yo keeps the sense, "kowtow, bow deeply."
