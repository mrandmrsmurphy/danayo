## Notes
- [従 (char)](../characters/従%20(char).md) "obey, follow, collateral" + [兄 (char)](../characters/兄%20(char).md) "older brother" — literally "a collateral older-brother [relative]."

從/従 as a kinship prefix marks a *collateral* relative — one reached by branching off the direct ancestral line, rather than a descendant or ancestor in the direct line itself. 從兄 in its classical, narrow Chinese sense names specifically a father's brother's son older than oneself (a patrilineal parallel cousin) — the same relation modern Mandarin usually reaches for with 堂哥 instead. Modern Japanese has generalized the term past that narrow classical restriction: 従兄 (じゅうけい, jūkei) is simply "an older male cousin," regardless of which side of the family, and in casual speech Japanese collapses the whole cousin system down to the single word いとこ (itoko) anyway, with 従兄/従弟/従姉/従妹 reserved for formal or written registers. Dan'a'yo follows the living, generalized Japanese sense here rather than the narrower classical Chinese one, filling the vault's own documented "no word for cousin" gap on [[Kinship]].

Mandarin cóngxiōng and Cantonese cung4 hing1 are both real, if now largely classical/literary, Sino compounds — attested on Wiktionary, but displaced by 堂哥/表哥 in everyday modern speech. Korean 종형 (jonghyeong) is real but secondary to the far more common native-feeling 사촌형 (built on 四寸, "cousin," rather than 從); Vietnamese tòng huynh is likewise a real, attested Sino-Vietnamese term (anh họ con nhà bác) alongside the more common đường huynh (堂兄).

This word is unambiguous where [[哥哥]] is not: 哥哥 covers a fictive "bro" *or* a real older male relative of the same generation, disambiguated only by context; 従兄 names the real-kinship relation specifically and only.
