## Notes
- [皇](../characters/皇.md) "emperor" + [帝](../characters/帝.md) "emperor" — 皇's own stand-in is this exact compound, but 帝's own is [[帝王]]; transitivity fails, so `#cranberry` does not apply, though 皇 is legitimized as an independent Dan'a'yo entry specifically by this word.

皇帝 was coined by Qin Shi Huang in 221 BC upon unifying China, fusing 皇 (originally associated with mythic sage-rulers like the Three Sovereigns, 三皇) and 帝 (associated with the Five Emperors, 五帝, and with 上帝, the high god) into a single title meant to surpass both — establishing "emperor" as a rank above "king" (王), the highest title used by pre-imperial Chinese rulers. Every subsequent Chinese dynasty adopted 皇帝 as the imperial title, down through the Qing dynasty's fall in 1912.

Mandarin huángdì, Cantonese wong4 dai3, Japanese こうてい (kōtei), Korean 황제 (hwangje), and Vietnamese hoàng đế are all fully compositional and were each historically applied to their own line of monarchs claiming the highest rank — the Japanese 天皇 (tennō) is literally "heavenly emperor," and both Korea (briefly, as the Korean Empire, 1897–1910) and Vietnam (intermittently, asserting parity with China) used 皇帝 for their own sovereigns at various points, a politically loaded claim given China's traditional insistence that only its own ruler held the title.
