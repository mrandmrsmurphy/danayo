## Notes
- [足 (char)](../characters/足%20(char).md) "foot" — stand-in for [[足 (char)|足]], which cannot appear independently

足 is 会意: 口 (here a knee/joint shape, not "mouth") over [[止]] ("stop; foot") — the whole leg from knee to foot, not the foot alone as the modern gloss might suggest. Beyond the plain anatomical sense (手足 "limbs," 足指 "toe," 足裏 "sole," 足球 "football/soccer"), the character generalizes to "adequate, sufficient" — 充足 "adequate," 満足 "satisfied, contented," 自足/自給自足 "self-sufficient" — the same foot-to-sufficiency metaphor recurring across the sphere (compare English "well-footed" idioms of stability and completeness). 画蛇添足 ("to gild the lily," literally "draw a snake and add feet") uses the anatomical sense directly as the idiom's punchline — a snake doesn't need feet, so adding them is the paradigm case of a pointless addition — while this vault's own 破頭傷足 draws on the same body-part vocabulary for its Genesis 3:15 protoevangelium translation.

Japanese carries an unusually wide three-reading set: そく (SOKU, used here — 満足 manzoku, 不足 fusoku "insufficient") dominates ordinary vocabulary, while しょく and すう survive only in rare specialized compounds; the native 訓読み あし (ashi) is the everyday word for the physical foot/leg. Vietnamese túc is the character's sole stored reading, alive in compounds like satisfaction-register words (mãn túc-type formal usage) though the everyday word for "foot" itself is the native chân. `kwin` is true: Dan'a'yo 족 (jog, from Middle Chinese 精母 t͡s + 燭韻 ɨok) coincides exactly with Sino-Korean 족. No homophones (注音 ㄐㄛㄎ shared only by characters — 擢, 属, 族, 嘱 — none with a self-pointing `stand_in`).
