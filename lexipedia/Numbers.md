# Numbers

Number vocabulary spans the full range from everyday counting to specialized mathematical register. Dan'a'yo inherits the Sinitic decimal-with-myriad-grouping cardinal system (counting by powers of ten up through 萬/万 "ten thousand," rather than English's thousand-grouping), so learners moving from a European L1 need to relearn how large numbers are chunked, not just memorize new words. Because Dan'a'yo is also a functioning auxiliary language rather than only a naturalistic conlang, this domain additionally needed a systematic, transparent vocabulary for higher mathematics — arithmetic operations, number types, trigonometry, and calculus — coined deliberately rather than left to develop ad hoc, and cross-checked line by line against real Mandarin/Cantonese/Japanese/Korean/Vietnamese usage so that the coined layer stays recognizable to speakers of every source language.

### Large Numbers

Above a hundred, Dan'a'yo groups by powers of ten up through 萬/万 ("myriad," 10,000) rather than by powers of a thousand — the same 萬-based grouping used throughout the Sinosphere, distinct from the English thousand/million/billion chain.

## Semantic Range Notes

Auditing this domain surfaced several places where Dan'a'yo has to make a deliberate choice among genuinely diverging CJKV conventions, rather than simply concatenating characters.

Most strikingly, **the four arithmetic operations are named twice**, once as a "method" (X法: 加法/減法/乗法/除法, "addition/subtraction/multiplication/division method") and once as a "calculation" verb (X算: 加算/減算/乗算/除算, "to add/subtract/multiply/divide"). This mirrors a genuine register split across the source languages — Mandarin and Cantonese lean on the 法 forms, Japanese and Korean on the 算 forms — and Dan'a'yo keeps both rather than picking a winner, since both are independently standard somewhere in the sphere.

Several number-type words required resolving a **real disagreement among the daughter languages about which root to use**, rather than simple compositional concatenation. [[負数]] ("negative number") composes cleanly in Mandarin/Cantonese/Japanese, but Korean does not follow a direct 負數 calque at all (that would collide with 부수, "Kangxi radical") — Korean instead uses 음수 (陰數, "yin number"), and Vietnamese independently reaches for the same yin/陰-based logic (số âm) rather than a 負-based one. Both are filled with the real attested terms rather than a compositional guess, per this vault's cross-linguistic-field convention.

[[複素数]] ("complex number") is the clearest case of Dan'a'yo needing to pick a side. Mandarin/Cantonese genuinely use 複數/复数 for *both* "plural" (grammar) and "complex number" (math), disambiguated only by context — but Dan'a'yo's own [[複数]] already claims the grammatical sense, so reusing it for math would create a real Dan'a'yo-internal collision the source languages don't actually have to worry about. Japanese and Korean independently solved the identical problem by inserting 素 ("element") — 複素数/복소수 — and Dan'a'yo follows their solution for the same underlying reason they adopted it.

Finally, several items simply have no living Sino-Vietnamese equivalent at all: [[対数]] ("logarithm") is a French loanword in Vietnamese (lôgarit), not a Sino-Vietnamese compound — and the compositional reading that would otherwise look plausible, đối số, is a real Vietnamese math term, but means "argument" (the input to a logarithm), a different concept entirely. [[一打]] ("dozen") is itself a **triple-loan word**: Mandarin/Cantonese phonetically transliterate English "dozen" as 打 (unrelated to 打's ordinary "hit" sense), while Japanese and Korean instead borrow the English word directly (ダース/다스) rather than following the Chinese loan.
