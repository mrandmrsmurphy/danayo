# Metals

Metal vocabulary in Dan'a'yo runs on two parallel naming systems that usually, but not always, agree. Most common metals reuse this vault's own coined periodic-table element names directly as their everyday word — [[黄金]] ("gold") and [[鉄]] ("iron") are simultaneously the ordinary noun and the Periodic Table's own systematic element name, the same double duty found throughout this domain. A handful of metals instead keep a separate, older or more colloquial word distinct from their periodic-table name, most notably aluminum (see the Semantic Range Notes below) — a genuine divergence worth knowing about before assuming every metal word here doubles as its periodic-table entry.

## Semantic Range Notes

The dominant pattern in this domain is that a metal's Dan'a'yo word is simultaneously its everyday noun *and* its Periodic Table symbol-bearing element name — [[黄金]], [[鉄]], [[銀]], [[白金]], [[魔銅]], and [[狼金]] all do double duty this way, so there is normally no separate "colloquial" word to learn alongside the systematic one.

**Aluminum is the one clear exception.** Dan'a'yo's periodic-table entry for element 13 is [[礬素]] (built on the systematic X素 suffix used for main-group elements), but the word actually used for the everyday noun here is instead [[軽銀]], literally "light silver" — a deliberate colloquial coinage naming the metal by its two most immediately apparent properties (silvery lustre, strikingly low density) rather than a transliteration or a specially invented character. The logic mirrors how aluminum foil is universally called "tin foil" in English despite containing no tin: a descriptive folk name displacing the systematic one in everyday use. Dan'a'yo deliberately avoids the Mandarin-coined character 鋁/铝 here too, since it has no traditional CJKV pedigree and is easily confused with 鑢 ("metal file") and 礪/砺 ("whetstone").

The metalworking vocabulary preserves a **register distinction the two closest synonyms might otherwise obscure**: [[鍛冶]] and [[鍛錬]] share their leading character (鍛, "to forge") and look near-identical at a glance, but name genuinely different things. 鍛冶 stays anchored to the literal *craft* of metalworking — the trade, and by extension the blacksmith who practices it. 鍛錬 names the specific *act* of forging by repeated striking, but has drifted so far toward a figurative sense of disciplined training or self-cultivation in living Japanese and Mandarin usage ("tempering" one's body or character) that the literal forging sense is now the minority reading. A learner reaching for "to forge" in the literal metalworking sense should generally prefer 鍛冶 in casual contexts, reserving 鍛錬 for either technical/historical register or the figurative training sense.
