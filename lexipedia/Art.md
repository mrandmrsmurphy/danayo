# Art

Art vocabulary spans the whole range of aesthetic and cultural production: performance (theatre, acting), language arts (writing, literature, poetry, and the classical canon), the visual arts (painting, sculpture, calligraphy), music, and the traditionally adjacent domains of martial arts and craft. Because Dan'a'yo is itself a written, character-based language, this domain's Language Arts vocabulary — writing, reading, books, literature — carries unusual weight: a Dan'a'yo learner reaches for it constantly, not only when discussing art as a subject.

## Semantic Range Notes

**図画 vs. 絵画 — a general/technical term vs. fine art specifically**: [[図画]] ("diagram" + "picture") is the general, slightly technical word for a drawing or picture — the kind of word one meets on a school-subject list (図画工作, "arts and crafts" as a subject). [[絵画]] ("draw, sketch" + "draw") is fine-art painting specifically, both the verb "to paint" and the noun "a painting," and is itself a `#cranberry` case: both constituent characters are legitimized as independent Dan'a'yo entries by this exact compound.

**文学 vs. 文献 — English "literature" is genuinely two concepts here**: [[文学]] is literature as a body of creative written work and an academic discipline (novels, poetry, literary criticism) — a clean, directly parallel compound across the whole CJKV sphere with no sense-divergence. [[文献]] is "literature" in the unrelated sense of scholarly/historical documents and references (a bibliography's "literature review"). English collapses both senses onto one word; Dan'a'yo, like its source languages, correctly keeps them separate.

**詩歌, 詩作, 詩篇 — three genuinely different "poem" words, not synonyms**: [[詩歌]] is the general, unmarked term for poem/poetry. [[詩作]] specifically foregrounds the poem as a *made thing* — the compositional act and craft dimension (詩作を残す, "to leave behind poems"; 詩作活動, "the ongoing practice of composing poems") — and in Korean is a genuine homophone of 始作 ("beginning"), a resonance poets and critics occasionally exploit: every poem is a beginning as much as a making. [[詩篇]] names poems collectively and, capitalized, is the standard CJK Bible name for the Book of Psalms specifically.

**戯曲 vs. 劇 vs. 劇詩 — opera/script, the art form, and a specific poetic mode**: [[戯曲]] names Chinese opera broadly (a sung dramatic tradition combining music, dance, and stylized performance) and, by extension, a play or dramatic script. [[劇]] is drama/theatre as an art form in general. [[劇詩]] narrows further to dramatic poetry specifically — verse written for or about the stage. The three overlap in English translation but stay distinct in Dan'a'yo, matching the source languages.

**刻印 vs. 彫刻 — flat engraving vs. sculptural carving**: [[刻印]] names precise, two-dimensional, knife-based incising of marks, text, or seals, used both literally (engraving a seal or inscription) and figuratively (an impression left indelibly on memory or character). [[彫刻]] is the broader three-dimensional, sculptural sense of carving. The two are not interchangeable despite both glossing to English "carve/engrave."

**頁 preserves a fossil of its own etymology**: 頁 originally depicted a head — still visible as the semantic radical in 頭, 顔, 額 — and only acquired "page, leaf" via an unrelated phonetic loan from [[葉 (char)|葉]] ("leaf"). Korean actually splits the two senses onto two distinct hanja readings (혈 for the old "head" sense, 엽 for "page," the one used here) rather than reusing one reading across both meanings — a real split this vault's own `korean` field follows. Modern spoken Japanese has also largely abandoned 頁's on'yomi in favor of the English loanword ページ (pēji), leaving 頁 confined to literary and technical/compound contexts (頁岩, "shale").

**閲読 is narrower than plain "to read"**: especially in Japanese, 閲読 specifically implies reading while examining or investigating a text's content for a purpose (古文書を閲読する, "to read and examine old documents"), distinct from casual reading (読む) and from the related 閲覧 ("to view/browse while inspecting") and 校閲 ("proofreading/editing").

**鳳笙 names two different things depending on the language**: in Japanese court gagaku, ほうしょう (鳳笙) names a specific 17-pipe free-reed mouth organ. In Chinese, 鳳笙 is mainly a poetic epithet rather than the name of one specific instrument — a genuine cross-linguistic narrowing, not a translation mismatch.
