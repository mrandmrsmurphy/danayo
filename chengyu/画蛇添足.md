## Literal Meaning
- [画](../characters/画.md) — to draw, to paint
- [蛇 (char)](../characters/蛇%20(char).md) — snake
- [添](../characters/添.md) — to add
- [足 (char)](../characters/足%20(char).md) — foot, leg

Literally: "draw a snake and add feet."

## Extended Meaning
To ruin something by unnecessary addition; to overdo a task and spoil an already complete result. The idiom emphasizes excess rather than incompetence — the mistake lies in failing to recognize when a task is already finished, not in any lack of skill.

## Source and Origin
From **《戰國策·齊策二》** ("Stratagems of Qi"; read at [維基文庫〈戰國策/卷09〉](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/戰國策/卷09), where the anecdote is the chapter 昭陽為楚伐魏), in the anecdote 昭陽為楚伐魏 ("Zhao Yang Attacks Wei on Behalf of Chu"; the commonly given date of c. 323 BC is not checked here), where the strategist Chen Zhen dissuades the victorious Chu general Zhao Yang from also attacking Qi by telling this story:

> "楚有祠者，賜其舍人巵酒。舍人相謂曰：『數人飲之不足，一人飲之有餘。請畫地為蛇，先成者飲酒。』一人蛇先成，引酒且飲之，乃左手持巵，右手畫蛇，曰：『吾能為之足。』未成，一人之蛇成，奪其巵曰：『蛇固無足，子安能為之足？』遂飲其酒。為蛇足者，終亡其酒。"
> ("A man in Chu who had held a sacrifice gave his retainers a jug of wine. The retainers said to each other, 'There isn't enough for several of us to drink, but enough for one — let's each draw a snake on the ground, and whoever finishes first drinks the wine.' One man's snake was finished first; he took the wine to drink it, but then, holding the jug in his left hand, began drawing with his right, saying, 'I can still add feet to it.' Before he finished, another man's snake was done; he snatched the jug away, saying, 'A snake has no feet — how can you give it feet?' And so he drank the wine. The one who added feet to the snake lost his wine in the end.")

Taiwan's Ministry of Education *Dictionary of Chinese Idioms* gives the same source (典出《戰國策·齊策二》), and the story's text and the framing around it (Zhao Yang's victory over Wei, the eight cities, Chen Zhen's question about the rank of the Chu 令尹, and the closing 戰無不勝而不知止者…猶為蛇足也, after which 昭陽以為然，解軍而去) match the 昭陽為楚伐魏 chapter as I read it on Wikisource (linked above). I read the opening and closing lines (楚有祠者，賜其舍人巵酒 … 為蛇足者，終亡其酒) and the two sentences 舍人相謂曰… and 一人蛇先成… directly there, with 巵 (not 卮) and 飲 (not 飮); the middle sentences 蛇固無足… and the framing about Zhao Yang's rank (the eight cities, the 令尹 question, the closing comment) I did not re-read on that page, so they remain as earlier recorded. That page also carries an editorial note 〈鮑本無「之」字〉 after 引酒且飲之，. The four-character form 畫蛇添足 does not occur in the story itself (which says 為蛇足); I did not establish when it first appears as a set phrase.

Chen Zhen used the fable to argue that Zhao Yang, having already won every honor available for defeating Wei, would gain nothing but risk by also attacking Qi — an unnecessary addition to an already complete achievement. Zhao Yang found the argument persuasive and withdrew his troops.

## Cultural Notes
The story reflects a common theme in Warring States rhetoric: the importance of appropriateness and restraint, where the error lies not in ambition itself but in ignoring the natural limits of a thing — a snake requires no feet, and a finished task requires no further embellishment. Across the region today, 画蛇添足 is widely used to criticize needless elaboration in writing, policy, craftsmanship, or everyday tasks; its tone is corrective rather than harsh, pointing out that simplicity would have served better than embellishment.

## Example Sentences
*All four examples are constructed, not quotations.*

1. 這篇文章結尾已經很好了，再加一段反而畫蛇添足。("The article's ending was already good; adding another paragraph would only be gilding the lily.")
2. Adding a moral to the end of that already-clear story was pure 画蛇添足.
3. その提案は画蛇添足で、かえって計画を複雑にした。("That proposal was an unnecessary addition that only complicated the plan.")
4. 設計已臻完美，切勿畫蛇添足。("The design is already perfect — do not spoil it with unnecessary additions.")

## Sentiment
Mildly critical, corrective. Points out wasted or counterproductive effort rather than condemning genuine incompetence.
