## Literal Meaning
- [万](../characters/万.md) — ten thousand, myriad
- [物](../characters/物%20(char).md) — thing
- [生](../characters/生.md) — to grow, to be born
- [長](../characters/長%20(char).md) — to grow, to lengthen

Literally: "the ten thousand things grow."

## Extended Meaning
All living things flourish through the vital power of Heaven and Earth; the natural order of life's growth and renewal. Still used today, especially in seasonal prose or philosophical writing, for the growth and renewal of nature, society, or human potential more broadly.

## Source and Origin
The collocation appears repeatedly in pre-Qin and early Han cosmological texts, tied explicitly to the rhythm of the seasons:

- **《荀子·王制》**:
  > "春耕夏耘，秋收冬藏，萬物生長之時也。"
  > (In spring plowing, in summer weeding, in autumn harvesting, in winter storing up — these are the seasons when the myriad things are born and grow.)
- **《淮南子·天文訓》**:
  > "春分而後，天地氣交，萬物生長。"
  > (After the spring equinox, heaven and earth's vital energies intermingle, and all things grow.)
- **《禮記·月令》** has parallel phrasing describing seasonal ordinances, where 生长 is applied to the myriad things in spring and summer.

The underlying imagery is older still, running throughout the Confucian, Daoist, and poetic canons even where the exact four-character string doesn't appear:

- **《詩經·小雅·四月》**: "四月維夏，六月徂暑。萬物方盛，天道乃定。" ("In the fourth month comes summer, in the sixth the heat; all things flourish, Heaven's order is settled.")
- **《孟子·滕文公上》**: "萬物並育而不相害，道並行而不相悖。" ("All things grow together without harming one another; their ways move in harmony without conflict.")
- **《易經·繫辭傳》**: "天地絪縕，萬物化醇。" ("Heaven and Earth interweave, and the myriad things take form.")

By the Han and onward, 万物生长 became a set phrase in cosmological and poetic writing, though not always counted as a formal 成語. Tang poetry often echoes it in spring descriptions, and it stayed alive all the way into modern political poetry — Mao Zedong's 《七律·长征》 includes the line 万物生长靠太阳 ("all things grow relying on the sun").

## Standard Form
**萬物生長** (traditional) / **万物生长** (simplified) — both current, no meaningful difference in usage; Dan'a'yo standardizes on the mixed-script 万物生長.

## Cultural Notes
The phrase carries different philosophical freight depending on tradition:
- **Confucian:** expresses the benevolence of Heaven's order — Heaven nourishes all things impartially (天道無親).
- **Daoist:** reflects the spontaneous self-generation of the Dao — "the Dao gives birth to one, one to two, two to three, three to the ten thousand things" (道生一，一生二，二生三，三生萬物).
- **Buddhist (later usage):** the universal interdependence and transformation of life.
- **Modern:** the growth and renewal of nature, society, or human potential generally.

It also has a cross-cultural afterlife: Christian missionaries in China cited 萬物生長 as a natural expression of divine creation and providence (compare Genesis 1:11–12, "let the earth bring forth grass..."), and Korean and Japanese Neo-Confucians adopted it as shorthand for natural moral order — the principle that life itself is proof of cosmic harmony. Related but distinct expressions in the same semantic field include 萬物化生 ("all things are transformed and generated"), 萬物並育 ("all things grow together"), and 生生不息 ("life begets life without ceasing") — these are separate idioms, not variant spellings of this one.

## Example Sentences
1. 春來萬物生長，草木萌發。("With spring's arrival, all things grow and plants sprout anew.")
2. 봄이 되면 만물생장한다. ("When spring comes, all things grow.")
3. When spring arrives, all things flourish — *vạn vật sinh trưởng*.

## Sentiment
Serene and life-affirming; expresses the cosmic pulse of renewal. Where idioms like 鏡花水月 or 空中樓閣 speak of illusion, 万物生長 turns to reality and vitality — the eternal spring of creation.
