## Literal Meaning
- [五](../characters/五%20(char).md) — five
- [風](../characters/風%20(char).md) — wind
- [十](../characters/十%20(char).md) — ten
- [雨](../characters/雨%20(char).md) — rain

Literally: "Every five days a wind, every ten days a rain."

## Extended Meaning
A time of gentle weather and harmonious order — mild, timely winds and rains that neither drought nor flood, neither storm nor stagnation, sustaining a fertile land. Used figuratively for peace and prosperity under Heaven: a stable, well-governed realm where everything runs smoothly, without ever referring to actual weather in modern usage. Always positive; almost always paired with an implicit claim about good governance.

## Source and Origin
The page previously cited the 《淮南子·氾論訓》. I read that chapter on wikisource and the phrase is not in it, so that attribution is withdrawn.

The earliest source I could verify is **王充《論衡·是應篇》** (Wang Chong, 1st century CE). Wang Chong reports what Confucian scholars say about the signs of a perfect age, and then criticises it:

> 儒者論太平瑞應，…又言山出車，澤出舟，男女異路，市無二價，耕者讓畔，行者讓路，頒白不提挈，關梁不閉，道無虜掠，風不鳴條，雨不破塊，五日一風，十日一雨，其盛茂者，致黃龍、騏、鳳皇。夫儒者之言，有溢美過實。
> "The Confucians, discussing the auspicious signs of a great peace, say that…the wind does not rustle the branches and the rain does not break the clods, that there is a wind every five days and a rain every ten, and that the most flourishing times bring yellow dragons, qilin and phoenixes. But the Confucians' words overpraise and exceed the facts."

Wang Chong goes on to say that wind and rain should be timely and moderate, but that to give the exact count, a wind every five days and a rain every ten, is praise, since weather cannot keep to such a schedule (「風雨雖適，不能五日、十日，正如其數」). So the formula 五日一風，十日一雨 is a Han Confucian commonplace for the weather of a sage-king's age, and the text that preserves it also treats it as rhetorical exaggeration.

The four-character 五風十雨 is attested in later poetry and official prose as a sign of peace and good government, for example in a Tang memorial asking that officials review prisoners so that 「五風十雨，永致昌期」, in Song and Yuan verse, and in Qing imperial edicts.

## Standard Form
**五風十雨** (traditional) / **五风十雨** (simplified) — both forms current; no meaningful distinction beyond the general Mainland-simplified vs. Taiwan/Japan-traditional split.

Sometimes confused with (but distinct from) near-synonym idioms built on the same "favorable weather → good governance" image: 風調雨順 ("favorable winds and timely rain"), 國泰民安 ("the country prospers, the people live in peace"), and 天時地利 ("the harmony of Heaven's time and Earth's advantage"). These are related in theme, not variant spellings of this idiom.

## Cultural Notes
五風十雨 expresses the ancient conviction that cosmic harmony and moral virtue are intertwined. In Confucian cosmology, the state of the heavens mirrors the state of the realm — when a ruler governs with virtue (德治), the *qi* of Heaven and Earth flows smoothly, and gentle winds and timely rains bless the people; the phrase became a meteorological signature of a sage-king's reign. In Daoist reading it instead illustrates 自然之道, nature's self-regulation — neither excess nor deficiency, cyclical renewal, abundance tempered by restraint.

Usage varies little in register across the sphere — in every tradition this stays a learned, literary idiom rather than everyday speech. Modern Chinese uses it figuratively for peace, stability, and prosperity, often paired with claims of national flourishing. Japanese ごふうじゅうう is a learned literary term. Korean 오풍십우 appears in classical writing to describe periods of peace or a ruler's benevolent reign. Vietnamese *ngũ phong thập vũ* appears in poetic and historical prose as a metaphor for good governance.

## Example Sentences
1. 君行仁政，則五風十雨，年穀豐登。
   "When the ruler governs with benevolence, the winds and rains come in their time, and the harvest is rich."
2. 五風十雨の世、政治も安らかなり。
   "In an age of 'five winds and ten rains,' the polity too is tranquil."
3. 오풍십우라, 태평성세라 할 만하다.
   "It may well be called a time of five winds and ten rains — an age of peace and plenty."
4. Dưới triều vua hiền, ngũ phong thập vũ, dân an vật thịnh.
   "Under a virtuous king, there were five winds and ten rains — the people secure, the land prosperous."

## Sentiment
Strongly positive, auspicious, and literary. Invokes an idealized image of good governance rather than actual weather; almost always used to praise a ruler, an era, or a state of affairs as harmonious and well-ordered.
