## Literal Meaning
- [呉](../characters/呉.md) — Wu (the ancient southern state)
- [牛 (char)](../characters/牛%20(char).md) — ox
- [喘](../characters/喘.md) — pant, gasp
- [月 (char)](../characters/月%20(char).md) — moon

Literally: "The Wu ox pants at the moon."

## Extended Meaning
Describes someone so conditioned by a past hardship that they overreact with fear to something only superficially similar and actually harmless — the fear is disproportionate because it is driven by memory, not by the present danger. It carries a reflective, mildly sympathetic tone rather than mockery: the person is not being called foolish so much as understandably scarred. A secondary, less common extended use applies it to describe stifling, oppressive heat in general (by association with the ox's suffering under the summer sun).

## Source and Origin
**《世說新語·言語》** (Liu Yiqing 劉義慶, compiled 5th century):
> 滿奮畏風，在武帝坐，北窗作琉璃扇屏風，實密似疏，奮有難色。帝笑之，奮答曰：「臣猶吳牛，見月而喘。」
> (Man Fen was afraid of drafts. Seated in attendance on [Emperor Wu of Jin], near a north-facing window fitted with a glazed screen that was in fact sealed tight but looked as if it let the wind through, he showed visible distress. The emperor laughed at him. Man Fen replied: "Your servant is like a Wu ox — seeing the moon, it pants.")

Man Fen (滿奮) was a Western Jin official serving under Emperor Wu of Jin (晉武帝, Sima Yan), founder of the Jin dynasty. Ordered to sit near a window that only looked drafty, he panted with dread before any wind actually touched him — comparing himself to the oxen of the southern Wu region, so worn down by the region's fierce summer heat that they would pant in fear at the mere sight of the moon, mistaking its pale light for the returning sun.

The fixed four-character form 吳牛喘月 is a later crystallization, not a verbatim quotation: the original text gives 臣猶吳牛，見月而喘 ("I am like a Wu ox — seeing the moon, it pants"), split across the comparison (臣猶吳牛) and the action (見月而喘). Later usage condensed the pairing into the compact 呉牛喘月.

## Standard Form
**呉牛喘月** (Japanese shinjitai form, used here as the Dan'a'yo standard)
- 吳牛喘月 — traditional form (呉 → 吳)
- 吴牛喘月 — simplified form (呉 → 吴)
- 呉牛月に喘ぐ — a native Japanese verb-phrase rendering ("the Wu ox pants at the moon"), used alongside the four-character Sino-Japanese form

## Cultural Notes
Most current in Mandarin and Japanese literary/essayistic prose, where it functions as a compact, slightly bookish way to describe trauma-driven overreaction — a register above everyday conversation, closer to English "once bitten, twice shy" but with a more explicitly psychological framing (the fear as a conditioned response, not just caution). It is less commonly encountered in colloquial Cantonese or Korean speech, surviving mainly in written or formal contexts across the sphere. Vietnamese Ngô ngưu suyễn nguyệt is a direct Sino-Vietnamese borrowing of the Chinese idiom, used in literary registers rather than everyday speech.

## Example Sentences
1. 他曾經被那間銀行拒絕貸款，如今一聽到「銀行」二字就緊張，簡直是呉牛喘月。
2. Ever since the accident, he flinches at any loud engine noise — a classic case of _呉牛喘月_.
3. 被開水燙過的孩子，見了溫茶也會躲開，這正是呉牛喘月的道理。
4. Her wariness of every new manager, after one bad boss years ago, is _Ngô ngưu suyễn nguyệt_ in miniature.

## Sentiment
Mildly negative to neutral, and notably sympathetic rather than critical — it frames the overreaction as an understandable scar from real past suffering, not a character flaw. Register is literary/reflective; not used as an insult.
