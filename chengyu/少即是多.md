## Literal Meaning
- [少 (char)](../characters/少%20(char).md) — less
- [即 (char)](../characters/即%20(char).md) — namely, in other words
- [是 (char)](../characters/是%20(char).md) — is
- [多 (char)](../characters/多%20(char).md) — more

Literally: "less is more."

## Extended Meaning
Simplicity contains abundance; restraint reveals fullness. Used both literally (in art, architecture, and design) and metaphorically (in ethics, relationships, or leadership) to praise the clarity and power gained by removing excess rather than adding to it.

## Source and Origin
Though not an ancient chengyu, 少即是多 fuses a modern Western aesthetic maxim with deep resonance in traditional East Asian philosophy. The English phrase "less is more" was popularized by the German-American modernist architect **Ludwig Mies van der Rohe** (1886–1969), who distilled architectural beauty into essential, unornamented form. Rendered into Chinese as 少即是多, the phrase struck a chord with pre-existing Daoist, Confucian, and Zen ideals that simplicity reveals essence.

Its most direct classical echo is **《道德經》** chapter 48:

> "為學日益，為道日損。損之又損，以至於無為。"
> ("In the pursuit of learning, one acquires daily; in the pursuit of the Dao, one diminishes daily — until one arrives at non-action.")

The popular modern maxim 大道至簡 ("the great Way is supremely simple") is often loosely attributed to the *Dao De Jing* itself, but this specific four-character phrase does not actually appear anywhere in that text — it is better described as a later folk distillation of Daoist thought rather than a direct quotation. Similarly, the principle "以少勝多" ("the few overcoming the many") is commonly associated with Sunzi's *Art of War* and with historical battles won against numerically superior forces, but is not a verbatim line from the text itself. 少即是多 thus deliberately bridges a genuinely ancient philosophical current with a specifically modern, named Western maxim, rather than claiming ancient textual origin for the four-character form itself.

## Cultural Notes
少即是多 embodies the paradox of emptiness and fullness, a recurring theme across Chinese philosophy. In Daoism, "less" means removing clutter, desire, and excess so the Dao can flow unhindered, as expressed in the *Dao De Jing* passage above. In Confucian ethics, moderation (中庸, *zhongyong*) makes virtue enduring — true refinement lies in proportion and balance rather than ornament. In Zen, simplicity is itself a form of enlightenment: when the mind is emptied of attachment, reality shines through more clearly, an idea expressed in the Zen garden, the single brushstroke, or the silent poem. In Japanese aesthetic philosophy the same paradox appears in 渋い (*shibui*, quiet beauty) and 侘寂 (*wabi-sabi*, graceful imperfection), and Japanese speakers often render the same idea in native phrasing as 少ないことは多いこと (*sukunai koto wa ooi koto*), especially in architecture, Zen art, and tea culture. Korean usage favors 소즉시다 or the native 적을수록 많다 ("the fewer, the greater") in minimalist design and life-philosophy contexts. Vietnamese thiểu tức thị đa appears in modern essays on art and spirituality, often tied to the Buddhist ideal of moderation, 少欲知足 (*thiểu dục tri túc*, "few desires, knowing sufficiency").

## Example Sentences
1. 設計不在多，而在精；少即是多。("Design is not about more, but about refinement — less is more.")
2. 茶の湯の美は、少即是多の精神にある。("The beauty of tea ceremony lies in the spirit of 'less is more.'")
3. 소즉시다, 단순함이 진리를 드러낸다。("Less is more — simplicity reveals truth.")
4. Thiểu tức thị đa – càng giản đơn, càng sâu sắc。("Less is more — the simpler, the deeper.")

## Sentiment
Calm, reflective, aesthetically and philosophically affirming. Praises restraint and simplicity as sources of clarity and depth rather than lack.
