## Literal Meaning
- [呵呵](../words/呵呵.md) — "ha-ha," the sound of laughter (reduplicated onomatopoeia)
- [大 (char)](../characters/大%20(char).md) — great, loudly
- [笑 (char)](../characters/笑%20(char).md) — laugh

Literally: "'Ha-ha,' laughing greatly" — an onomatopoeic laugh-sound combined with a verb intensifying it.

## Extended Meaning
Describes laughing loudly, openly, and heartily — a full, unrestrained laugh rather than a polite chuckle. Neutral to positive in classical and literary usage, though Mandarin 呵呵 by itself has picked up a separate, much more recent internet-era connotation of cold or sarcastic dismissal (see Cultural Notes) that does not carry over into the fixed four-character idiom.

## Source and Origin
**《景德傳燈錄》** (Jingde Chuandeng Lu, "Jingde-era Record of the Transmission of the Lamp"), a Song-dynasty Chan/Zen Buddhist chronicle compiled by the monk Daoyuan (道原) in 1004 CE, records the lineages and teaching-encounters of over 1,700 Chan masters. I searched the CBETA text (T2076): the four characters 呵呵大笑 occur repeatedly, in the encounter records of different masters — for example in 卷八 (「谷山呵呵大笑三聲」, and a master who 「撫掌呵呵大笑」 on awakening), 卷十二 and 卷十四. In these records the laugh is a Chan gesture: a teacher's or student's burst of laughter at an exchange that has reached its point.

An earlier version of this page said the idiom came from Yangshan Huiji's laughter at Sansheng Huiran, filed under 卷八. The exchange itself is real (it is in the 卷十二 entry for Sansheng, 鎮州三聖院慧然禪師: Yangshan asks his name, he answers 「名慧寂」, Yangshan says 「慧寂是我名」, and he replies 「我名慧然」), but the text there says 「仰山大笑而已」 ("Yangshan simply laughed"), not 呵呵大笑, and it is in 卷十二, not 卷八. I have therefore removed that attribution. I did not trace whether 呵呵大笑 is attested before the 景德傳燈錄.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** hē hē dà xiào
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** ho1 ho1 daai6 siu3
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** かかたいしょう
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 가가대소 (ga-ga-dae-so)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** ha ha đại tiếu

Korean notably uses 가, not the more intuitive-looking 하 (matching the onomatopoeic "ha" sound directly) — dictionaries explicitly confirm 가가대소 as the standardized reading, even though 呵 is popularly associated with the "ha" laugh sound. This reflects 呵's separate, older Sino-Korean reading tied to its "scold, exclaim" sense (呵 is glossed 꾸짖을 가, "scold-GA," in traditional Korean character dictionaries), which became the fixed reading for this classical idiom even though the idiom's actual meaning is about laughter, not scolding.

## Cultural Notes
In classical and literary registers across all five languages, 呵呵大笑 simply means hearty, open laughter — entirely positive or neutral. Mandarin has a notable modern exception: bare 呵呵 (without 大笑) has developed a widely-documented internet-era sarcastic sense — a cold, dismissive "oh, sure" rather than genuine amusement, a shift traceable as far back as the Ming/Qing dynasties but sharply intensified by internet culture, to the point that many Mandarin speakers now avoid typing 呵呵 sincerely at all (see [[呵呵]] for more detail). This sarcastic shade does not attach to the full four-character idiom 呵呵大笑, which remains unambiguously about genuine, hearty laughter in all registers. Japanese and Korean usage of the full idiom survives mostly in literary or classical-flavored writing rather than everyday conversation.

## Example Sentences
1. 聽到這個笑話，他不禁呵呵大笑。
2. Hearing the punchline, she broke into _hēhē dàxiào_.
3. 彼はその話を聞いて呵呵大笑した。
4. The old monk's answer left the whole room in _ha ha đại tiếu_.

## Sentiment
Positive to neutral; describes unrestrained, hearty laughter. Unlike bare Mandarin 呵呵 in modern informal writing, the full idiom carries no sarcastic undertone.
