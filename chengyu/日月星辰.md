## Literal Meaning
- [日 (char)](characters/日%20(char).md) — sun, day
- [月 (char)](characters/月%20(char).md) — moon, month
- [星 (char)](characters/星%20(char).md) — star
- [辰](characters/辰.md) — celestial body, constellation

Literally: "Sun, moon, stars, constellations."

## Extended Meaning
日月星辰 refers collectively to the heavenly bodies and, by extension, the vast order of the cosmos. In early Chinese thought the four luminous bodies of heaven established cosmic order, time, and kingship, with the sovereign's virtue measured by harmony with their celestial rhythms. Used poetically across the region to refer to the heavens, the natural order, or eternity — a bridge between heaven's constancy and human transience.

## Source and Origin
The phrase has deep roots in early Chinese cosmology and literature, appearing in the **Book of Documents (《書經·堯典》)**, one of the oldest Chinese classics:

> 「乃命羲和，欽若昊天，曆象日月星辰，敬授人時。」
> "[The Emperor Yao] then charged Xi and He, in reverent accordance with their observation of the wide heavens, to calculate and delineate the movements and appearances of the sun, moon, stars, and constellations, and so to deliver respectfully the seasons to the people."

In early Chinese thought, 日月星辰 collectively referred to the four luminous bodies of heaven, whose movements established cosmic order, time, and kingship — the sovereign's virtue was measured by harmony with these celestial rhythms.

## Cultural Notes
日月星辰 embodies the cosmic hierarchy and moral symbolism that underpins Chinese cosmology. In Confucian thought, these celestial bodies symbolize the Mandate of Heaven (天命): the ruler's virtue should mirror the clarity of the sun, the constancy of the moon, and the orderly procession of the stars, and to "align with Heaven" (順天) meant to govern in harmony with these cycles. In Daoist philosophy, they represent the natural spontaneity (自然, *ziran*) of the universe — the Dao flowing through cosmic motion, unforced and eternal. In Buddhist usage, 日月星辰 often serve as metaphors for the Buddha's radiance and the constancy of truth amid the flux of samsara.

Japanese じつげつせいしん (jitsugesseishin) appears in formal or poetic contexts evoking grandeur or timelessness. Korean 일월성신 (ilwol seongsin) is used in both literature and idiom to signify the universe or divine order. Vietnamese nhật nguyệt tinh thần occurs in Sino-Vietnamese verse and historical chronicles to express celestial harmony.

Closely related expressions include 天地玄黃 ("heaven dark, earth yellow," the primordial order), 日月同輝 ("the sun and moon shine together"), 星羅棋布 ("stars arrayed like a chessboard," scattered far and wide), and 斗轉星移 ("as the Big Dipper turns, the stars shift," time passes).

## Example Sentences
1. 「乃命羲和，欽若昊天，曆象日月星辰，敬授人時。」(《書經·堯典》)
2. 日月星辰照四方，天地萬物共生長。("The sun, moon, and stars shine over the four quarters, nurturing all things under heaven.")
3. 일월성신이 증명하듯, 진리는 변치 않는다. ("As the sun, moon, and stars attest, truth does not change.")
4. Long after the empire itself had crumbled, the 日月星辰 wheeled on overhead, indifferent to the rise and fall of kingdoms.

## Sentiment
Grand, reverent, timeless. The eternal lights — silent witnesses to all beneath Heaven, evoking cosmic order and permanence against human transience.
