## Literal Meaning
- [鰥](characters/鰥.md) — a widower, an old man without a wife
- [寡](characters/寡.md) — a widow, an old woman without a husband
- [孤](characters/孤.md) — an orphan, a child without a father
- [独](characters/独.md) — alone, an old person without a son

Literally: "the widower, the widow, the orphan and the lonely."

## Extended Meaning
鰥寡孤独 names the four classes of people who, in the classical view, have no family to support them: the old man with no wife, the old woman with no husband, the child with no father, and the old person with no son. By extension it means the helpless and destitute in general, the people a good ruler is expected to look after first. It is the standing phrase for the classical idea of social welfare, and it is still used for the vulnerable members of a community who have no one to turn to.

## Source and Origin
The four definitions are in the 《孟子·梁惠王下》, chapter 5, which I checked on Wikisource: 「老而無妻曰鰥，老而無夫曰寡，老而無子曰獨，幼而無父曰孤。此四者，天下之窮民而無告者。」 ("An old man without a wife is called 鰥, an old woman without a husband is called 寡, an old person without a son is called 獨, a child without a father is called 孤. These four are the most destitute of the people of the world, with no one to appeal to.") Mencius says King Wen of Zhou made these four his first care when governing 岐. Note that Mencius lists them in the order 鰥, 寡, 獨, 孤; the set phrase puts 孤 before 獨.

The 《禮記·王制》, which I also checked on Wikisource, gives a parallel definition in the section on caring for the aged, but names the widower 矜 rather than 鰥: 「少而無父者謂之孤，老而無子者謂之獨，老而無妻者謂之矜，老而無夫者謂之寡」. So 矜寡孤獨 is the same idea in a variant spelling, while the Mencius text is the source of the 鰥 form. I did not look for other early uses of the exact four-character order or check how the order 鰥寡孤獨 became fixed.

## Variants
- 鰥寡孤獨 — traditional form
- 鳏寡孤独 — simplified Chinese form
- 矜寡孤獨 — the 《禮記》 spelling, with 矜 for the widower

## Cultural Notes
The four classes are the traditional test of a humane government. In Mencius the point is that benevolent rule starts with those who cannot ask for help, and the idiom has the same ethical weight in later writing. I did not check how common the idiom is in each language. The rare character 鰥 is what makes the idiom hard to read for many learners; in Japanese it is a hyōgai kanji and in Korean it is outside the standard teaching list.

## Example Sentences
1. 「老而無妻曰鰥，老而無夫曰寡，老而無子曰獨，幼而無父曰孤。」(《孟子·梁惠王下》)
2. 政府要先照顧鰥寡孤獨。("The government should first care for the widowed, the childless and the orphaned.")

## Sentiment
Solemn, compassionate; the language of duty toward the helpless.
