## Literal Meaning
- [不 (char)](characters/不%20(char).md) — not
- [可 (char)](characters/可%20(char).md) — able to, permissible
- [思](characters/思.md) — to think
- [議](characters/議.md) — to deliberate, discuss

Literally: "cannot be thought or discussed."

## Extended Meaning
不可思議 negates the capacity of conceptual thought itself — it marks realities that cannot be grasped through discursive reasoning, a boundary of cognition rather than something merely surprising or strange. In formal, philosophical, or religious writing it retains this original sense of transcendence; in everyday speech across the region it has broadened to mean simply astonishing, mysterious, or hard to explain, with register ranging from elevated to neutral depending on context.

## Source and Origin
不可思議 is a Buddhist translation term, a calque of Sanskrit **अचिन्त्य** (*acintya*, "inconceivable") and the related Pali *acinteyya* ("imponderable"). The expression entered Sinitic vocabulary through early translations of Mahāyāna scripture and became standard in medieval doctrinal and devotional literature.

Its most direct canonical grounding is **《維摩詰所說經·不思議品第六》** (the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, tr. Kumārajīva), an entire chapter devoted to the concept, which states:

> "唯，舍利弗！諸佛菩薩，有解脫，名不可思議。"
> ("Indeed, Śāriputra! The buddhas and bodhisattvas possess a liberation called the inconceivable.")

The chapter illustrates this "inconceivable liberation" with paradoxical images — fitting Mount Sumeru inside a mustard seed, pouring the four great oceans into a single pore — without diminishing either the container or the contained, a liberation beyond ordinary causal logic. The term also appears repeatedly throughout the **《妙法蓮華經》** (Lotus Sūtra), describing the inconceivable duration of kalpas and the rare, wondrous events surrounding a Buddha's teaching. As with many Buddhist translation compounds, the phrase fit naturally into the four-character rhythm of literary Chinese and was readily assimilated into broader secular usage, gradually detaching from strictly doctrinal contexts.

## Cultural Notes
In Japanese, ふかしぎ (fukashigi) is in wide everyday use — often written 不思議 alone, dropping 可 — as a common, mildly wondering word for "strange, mysterious, curious" (不思議の国のアリス, "Alice in Wonderland"), largely detached from its Buddhist origin for most speakers. Korean 불가사의 similarly circulates in secular contexts, notably as part of the set phrase 세계 7대 불가사의 ("the Seven Wonders of the World"). Mandarin and Cantonese retain both registers side by side: elevated/philosophical in Buddhist or literary contexts, and merely "amazing, incredible" in casual speech. Vietnamese bất khả tư nghị stays closer to its formal Buddhist register, less commonly used in casual secular speech than its Sinitic counterparts.

## Example Sentences
1. 諸佛菩薩不可思議解脫之力，非凡夫所能測也。("The inconceivable power of liberation of the buddhas and bodhisattvas cannot be fathomed by ordinary people.")
2. 這座橋的工程技術真是不可思議。
3. The engineering behind this bridge is truly *bùkěsīyì* — inconceivable.
4. 彼は不可思議な力を持っていると言われている。("He is said to possess mysterious, inexplicable powers.")

## Sentiment
Positive to awed/neutral, ranging in register from elevated philosophical/religious (describing genuine transcendence of ordinary cognition) to casual everyday amazement (astonishing, hard to believe).
