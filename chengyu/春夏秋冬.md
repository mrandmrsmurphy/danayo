## Literal Meaning
- [春 (char)](characters/春%20(char).md) — spring
- [夏 (char)](characters/夏%20(char).md) — summer
- [秋 (char)](characters/秋%20(char).md) — autumn
- [冬 (char)](characters/冬%20(char).md) — winter

Literally: "Spring, summer, autumn, winter."

## Extended Meaning
春夏秋冬 refers to the four seasons and, by extension, the cyclical rhythm of time and change itself. The phrase encapsulates the Chinese understanding of cyclical time — not linear progress or decay, but rhythmic return. Used literally for the seasons, or metaphorically for the passage of time and life's rhythm, mirroring the rise and fall of empires, the aging of the body, and the renewal of the spirit.

## Source and Origin
The phrase is as ancient as Chinese civilization itself, representing the fourfold rhythm of nature and time. The **Book of Documents (《書經·洪範》)** lays out the Five Elements framework from which later Han-dynasty cosmology derived its correlation of elements, directions, and seasons:

> 「五行：一曰水，二曰火，三曰木，四曰金，五曰土。」
> "The five elements are: water, fire, wood, metal, and earth."

The specific formula of seasonal cycle — birth in spring, growth in summer, harvest in autumn, storage in winter — is stated directly in **《史記·太史公自序》** (Sima Qian's postface to the *Records of the Grand Historian*), in its summary of the Yin-Yang school's teaching:

> 「夫春生夏長，秋收冬藏，此天道之大經也。」
> "Spring brings birth, summer growth, autumn harvest, winter storage — this is the great constant of Heaven's Way."

The **Classic of Poetry (《詩經·小雅·出車》)** also celebrates this seasonal harmony:

> 「春日遲遲，卉木萋萋。」
> "The spring days linger, the grasses grow thick and green."

Together these sources show the sequence 春夏秋冬 to be not merely calendrical but cosmic — the visible manifestation of Heaven's rhythm (天道), the pattern through which qi (氣) flows and life renews. Each season carries moral and emotional symbolism: spring as birth, summer as vigor, autumn as decline, winter as rest.

## Cultural Notes
In Confucian cosmology, 春夏秋冬 represents the order (理, *li*) of Heaven and Earth: the ruler who governs according to the seasons acts in harmony with Heaven's mandate (天命). In Daoist philosophy, it illustrates *wu wei* (無為), the principle of acting in accord with nature — just as spring's growth and winter's stillness follow their own timing, so should human life follow the effortless flow of the Dao. In Buddhist usage, the four seasons are metaphors for impermanence (無常, *anitya*): everything changes, perishes, and is reborn, yet within this flux lies a deeper constancy.

Japanese しゅんかしゅうとう (shunka shūtō) is widely used in poetry, seasonal aesthetics, and even branding to evoke 物の哀れ (*mono no aware*), the gentle sadness of passing beauty. Korean 춘하추동 (chunha chudong) appears in literature and proverbs symbolizing continuity and endurance. Vietnamese xuân hạ thu đông is used in poetry and folk sayings, often to mark the transience of life or love.

Related expressions include 四時之序 ("the order of the four seasons"), 歲月如流 ("time flows like a river"), 一年四季 ("the four seasons of a year"), and 循環不息 ("unceasing cycle").

## Example Sentences
1. 「夫春生夏長，秋收冬藏，此天道之大經也。」(《史記·太史公自序》)
2. 春夏秋冬，寒暑代謝，惟德不變。("Through spring, summer, autumn, and winter — cold and heat succeed one another, but virtue does not change.")
3. 춘하추동이 바뀌어도, 그리움은 여전하다. ("Though the seasons change, my longing remains.")
4. 春夏秋冬の花鳥風月、すべては無常の歌なり。("The flowers, birds, wind, and moon of the four seasons — all sing the song of impermanence.")

## Sentiment
Contemplative, cyclical, timeless. All things bloom, fade, and rest — yet the circle is never broken.
