## Literal Meaning
- [春 (char)](characters/春%20(char).md) — spring
- [夏 (char)](characters/夏%20(char).md) — summer
- [秋 (char)](characters/秋%20(char).md) — autumn
- [冬 (char)](characters/冬%20(char).md) — winter

Literally: "Spring, summer, autumn, winter."

## Extended Meaning
春夏秋冬 refers to the four seasons and, by extension, the cyclical rhythm of time and change itself. The phrase encapsulates the Chinese understanding of cyclical time — not linear progress or decay, but rhythmic return. Used literally for the seasons, or metaphorically for the passage of time and life's rhythm, mirroring the rise and fall of empires, the aging of the body, and the renewal of the spirit.

## Source and Origin
The four characters 春夏秋冬 occur together in the **《管子·四時》** ("The Four Seasons," chapter 40 of the *Guanzi*), checked on Wikisource:

> 「然則春夏秋冬將何行？東方曰星，其時曰春，其氣曰風。」
> ("Then what should be done in spring, summer, autumn and winter? The east is called the Star; its season is called spring, its breath is called wind.")

This is a documented occurrence of the sequence; I did not search for an earlier one. A search summary notes that some early texts give the order differently, as 春秋冬夏 (for example in the 《禮記·孔子閒居》 and 《墨子》), and that early Chinese time reckoning was first in two seasons, spring and autumn, so the settled four-season order is a later development; I did not check those claims against the texts.

The seasonal pattern itself is stated in **《史記·太史公自序》**, in the 論六家要指 attributed to Sima Tan (司馬談, Sima Qian's father), which I checked on Wikisource, in his comment on the Yin-Yang school:

> 「夫春生夏長，秋收冬藏，此天道之大經也。」
> ("Spring brings birth, summer growth, autumn harvest, winter storage — this is the great constant of Heaven's Way.")

The **Classic of Poetry (《詩經·小雅·出車》)** celebrates the same season in 「春日遲遲，卉木萋萋」 ("The spring days linger, the grasses grow thick and green"), a line I did not re-check. Each season carries moral and emotional symbolism: spring as birth, summer as vigor, autumn as decline, winter as rest (an interpretation, not a documented use).

**Corrected.** The earlier version of this page gave the 《書經·洪範》 as the origin, quoting its list of the five elements. I checked the 洪範: it does not contain 春夏秋冬 (it speaks of 歲月日時), and the list of five elements is not about seasons, so that citation has been removed. The earlier line that the phrase "is as ancient as Chinese civilization itself" was also unsupported.

## Cultural Notes
In Confucian cosmology, 春夏秋冬 represents the order (理, *li*) of Heaven and Earth: the ruler who governs according to the seasons acts in harmony with Heaven's mandate (天命). In Daoist philosophy, it illustrates *wu wei* (無為), the principle of acting in accord with nature — just as spring's growth and winter's stillness follow their own timing, so should human life follow the effortless flow of the Dao. In Buddhist usage, the four seasons are metaphors for impermanence (無常, *anitya*): everything changes, perishes, and is reborn, yet within this flux lies a deeper constancy.

Japanese しゅんかしゅうとう (shunka shūtō) can evoke 物の哀れ (*mono no aware*), the gentle sadness of passing beauty. Korean 춘하추동 (chunha chudong) appears in literature and proverbs symbolizing continuity and endurance. Vietnamese xuân hạ thu đông is used in poetry and folk sayings, often to mark the transience of life or love.

Related expressions include 四時之序 ("the order of the four seasons"), 歲月如流 ("time flows like a river"), 一年四季 ("the four seasons of a year"), and 循環不息 ("unceasing cycle").

## Example Sentences
1. 「夫春生夏長，秋收冬藏，此天道之大經也。」(《史記·太史公自序》)
2. 春夏秋冬，寒暑代謝，惟德不變。("Through spring, summer, autumn, and winter — cold and heat succeed one another, but virtue does not change.")
3. 춘하추동이 바뀌어도, 그리움은 여전하다. ("Though the seasons change, my longing remains.")
4. 春夏秋冬の花鳥風月、すべては無常の歌なり。("The flowers, birds, wind, and moon of the four seasons — all sing the song of impermanence.")

## Sentiment
Contemplative, cyclical, timeless. All things bloom, fade, and rest — yet the circle is never broken.
