## Literal Meaning
- [鏡 (char)](characters/鏡%20(char).md) — mirror
- [花](characters/花.md) — flower
- [水 (char)](characters/水%20(char).md) — water
- [月 (char)](characters/月%20(char).md) — moon

Literally: "Flowers in a mirror, the moon in water."

## Extended Meaning
鏡花水月 describes something beautiful but intangible: an illusion, vision, or ideal that can be seen but never grasped. It became a stock metaphor in Chan/Zen Buddhism for the illusory nature of phenomena (空相, *śūnyatā*) — appearances that reflect reality without themselves being substantial or real. Used across the region to describe unreal beauty, false hope, visionary ideals, or anything as fleeting and untouchable as a reflection: love, memory, fame, or dreams.

## Source and Origin
The metaphor of form as insubstantial as "flowers in a mirror, the moon in water" (鏡中花，水中月) is a widespread trope in Chinese Buddhist sutra literature and commentary, used to illustrate the doctrine that visible appearances are real as perception yet empty of independent substance. **《楞嚴經》** (Śūraṅgama Sūtra) is among the major texts associated with this teaching of the illusory nature of perceived phenomena. The related Daoist compendium **《雲笈七籤》**, compiled by **張君房** in the Northern Song dynasty, likewise draws on mirror-and-reflection imagery in its own cosmological and meditative writing, showing the trope's currency across both Buddhist and Daoist traditions of the period.

By the Song dynasty, poets such as Su Shi (蘇軾) and Ouyang Xiu (歐陽修) had taken up the same image for fleeting beauty and unattainable ideals in secular verse, cementing 鏡花水月 as a general-purpose idiom well beyond its original doctrinal context.

## Variants
- 鏡花水月 / 镜花水月 — traditional/simplified, identical meaning
- 水月鏡花 — reversed word order, same sense
- 鏡裡觀花 — "viewing flowers in a mirror"
- 水中撈月 — "fishing for the moon in water," a closely related image of futile grasping at illusion

## Cultural Notes
In Buddhist usage, all appearances are transient and insubstantial — they reflect reality but are not themselves real. In poetic usage, the phrase names beauty that exists only in perception: love, memory, fame, or dreams. Philosophically, it marks the boundary between illusion and truth, the paradox of perception itself.

Japanese 鏡花水月 (kyōka suigetsu) became something close to an aesthetic ideal in Zen-influenced art — beauty seen only in reflection, emphasizing ephemerality and perception. Korean 경화수월 expresses unattainable beauty or ephemeral art. Vietnamese kính hoa thuỷ nguyệt carries the same sense of poetic illusion and dreamlike vision.

Related in sense (though a distinct idiom) is [[空中楼閣]] ("a castle in the air").

## Example Sentences
1. 「榮華富貴，如鏡花水月。」("Glory and wealth are like flowers in a mirror or the moon in water.")
2. 他對那段往事的記憶，如鏡花水月，一觸即散。("His memory of that past was like flowers in a mirror and the moon in water — vanishing at a touch.")
3. 그의 꿈은 경화수월이었다. ("His dream was as unattainable as flowers in a mirror and the moon in water.")
4. Every promise the startup had made turned out to be 鏡花水月 — beautiful on the pitch deck, gone the moment anyone tried to touch it.

## Sentiment
Visible yet untouchable; beautiful yet unreal. A vision that reveals the truth of impermanence — emptiness clothed in beauty.
