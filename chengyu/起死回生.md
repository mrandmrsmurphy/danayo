## Literal Meaning
- [起 (char)](../characters/起%20(char).md) — to raise, to rise up
- [死](../characters/死.md) — dead
- [回 (char)](../characters/回%20(char).md) — to return, to turn back
- [生](../characters/生.md) — life, to live

Literally: "Raise the dead and bring them back to life."

## Extended Meaning
To rescue a situation from apparent ruin; to revive something — a project, a business, a plan, a person's fortunes — that seemed completely hopeless. Applies equally to literal medical recovery from near-death and to any dramatic reversal of an otherwise doomed outcome.

## Source and Origin
**《史記·扁鵲倉公列傳》** (Records of the Grand Historian, "Biographies of Bian Que and Cang Gong"):
> 越人非能生死人也，此自當生者，越人能使之起耳。
> ("I, Yueren [Bian Que's given name], am not able to bring the dead back to life — this is someone who was fated to live regardless, and I have merely helped him rise.")

Bian Que (扁鵲), the legendary physician of the Warring States period, was passing through the state of Guo (虢) when he learned the crown prince had suddenly collapsed and was believed dead. Where others saw a corpse, Bian Que recognized signs of life others had missed — warmth near the groin, faint sounds at the ear — and diagnosed a condition called 尸蹷 ("corpse collapse," a form of blocked-qi suspended animation, not true death). Using acupuncture at key points, followed by medicinal hot compresses and twenty days of further treatment, he fully revived the prince. When the whole realm marveled that he could raise the dead, Bian Que gave the modest reply quoted above — he could not resurrect the truly dead, only recognize and restore life where it still, faintly, remained. The idiom 起死回生 crystallized from this episode as a byword for extraordinary, seemingly miraculous recovery.

## Standard Form
**起死回生**
- Occasionally expanded in classical/literary contexts as **起死人而肉白骨** ("raise the dead and put flesh back on white bones"), a more elaborate variant with the same sense

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** qǐ sǐ huí shēng
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** hei2 sei2 wui4 sang1
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** きしかいせい
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 기사회생 (gi-sa-hoe-saeng)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** khởi tử hồi sinh

All five readings are fully compositional, matching each constituent character's own standard reading.

## Cultural Notes
Used across all five languages both literally (medical recovery from a near-fatal condition) and figuratively (rescuing a failing business, project, team, or plan from what seemed like certain collapse). Mandarin usage leans toward business and project-rescue contexts; Japanese usage is especially common in sports commentary and drama, describing a dramatic last-minute comeback; Korean usage similarly emphasizes escaping near-certain defeat. The idiom always implies the situation looked genuinely hopeless before the turnaround — it is not used for ordinary improvement or routine success.

## Example Sentences
1. 這個新策略讓公司起死回生，扭轉了連年虧損的局面。
2. The last-minute merger was nothing short of _qǐ sǐ huí shēng_ for the failing company.
3. 彼の一打がチームを起死回生させた。
4. Against all odds, the surgery brought about a genuine _khởi tử hồi sinh_.

## Sentiment
Strongly positive; describes a dramatic, near-miraculous reversal of fortune from a situation that looked truly hopeless.
