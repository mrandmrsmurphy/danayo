## Literal Meaning
- [金 (char)](../characters/金%20(char).md) — gold
- [甌](../characters/甌.md) — small bowl, cup, chalice
- [無 (char)](../characters/無%20(char).md) — without, not having
- [缺](../characters/缺.md) — chip, gap, flaw

Literally: "a golden chalice without a chip." The word 金甌 on its own is the literary noun for the golden chalice and, figuratively, for the nation (see [[金甌]]).

## Extended Meaning
A country that is whole and inviolate: its territory unbroken, unconquered and undivided, like a gold vessel with no dent or nick. It is a literary, formal and strongly positive set phrase of the language of patriotism and statecraft, used of national territory or sovereignty, either as a fact ("the realm was still whole") or as an aim ("to keep the land whole"). It can also be turned to praise a ruler or statesman who kept the state intact. In the older, original sense of the story below it is a boast of the kind that fate then punishes.

## Source and Origin
The image comes from a remark of Emperor Wu of Liang (梁武帝, 蕭衍), spoken to himself in 548 (太清二年) while he was undecided whether to accept the defection of the Eastern Wei general Hou Jing (侯景) and the lands of Henan that Hou Jing brought with him. The emperor had dreamed that the northern commanders were surrendering their territory to him, and his favourite minister Zhu Yi (朱異) urged him to accept; he did, Hou Jing rose in rebellion later the same year, and the Liang state was wrecked (the outline of events is from memory, not re-checked; the wording below is what was checked).

Checked 2026-10-11 against zh.Wikisource. The line is in three texts, in slightly different words, and the four-character form 金甌無缺 is in none of them.

**《南史》卷六十二〈朱異傳〉** (列傳第五十二; compiled in the 7th century by Li Yanshou, from memory), the passage to which the idiom is traditionally traced:
> 「太清二年，為中領軍，舍人如故。初，武帝夢中原盡平，舉朝稱慶，甚悅，以語異曰：『吾生平少夢，夢必有實。』異曰：『此宇內方一之征。』及侯景降，敕召群臣廷議，尚書僕射謝舉等以為不可許。武帝欲納之，未決，嘗夙興至武德合口，獨言：『我國家猶若金甌，無一傷缺，承平若此，今便受地，詎是事宜？脫至紛紜，悔無所及。』」
> (In the second year of Taiqing he was made commander of the central army ... Emperor Wu had dreamed that the central plains were wholly pacified and the whole court had congratulated him, and he was glad and told Zhu Yi: "I dream little; when I dream, it comes true." ... When Hou Jing surrendered the court was summoned to debate; Xie Ju and others thought he should not be accepted. The emperor wished to accept him and could not decide; once, rising early, he came to the gate of Wude hall and said to himself: "My state is like a golden chalice, with not one flaw or chip. In such peace as this, to accept land now, is that fitting? If it brings turmoil, regret will be too late.")

**《梁書》卷五十六〈侯景傳〉**, the same scene, with the first words in the other order:
> 「曾夜出視事，至武德閣，獨言：『我家國猶若金甌，無一傷缺，今便受地，詎是事宜，脫致紛紜，非可悔也。』」
> (Once, going out at night to attend to business, he came to Wude hall and said to himself: "My house and state are like a golden chalice, with not one flaw or chip. Now to accept land, is that fitting? If it brings turmoil, it cannot be regretted away.")

**《資治通鑑》卷一百六十〈梁紀十六〉** (placed under the first year of Taiqing, 547; compiled in 1084, from memory):
> 「然意猶未決，嘗獨言：『我國家如金甌，無一傷缺，今忽受景地，詎是事宜？脫致紛紜，悔之何及？』」

So the passage contains the phrase 金甌 and the clause 無一傷缺 ("not one flaw or chip"), but not the four characters together. The 南史 wording 「我國家猶若金甌，無一傷缺」 is the one usually quoted. The earliest text in which I could find the four-character form 金甌無缺 is the 黃氏日抄 (Southern Song, by Huang Zhen) at 卷四十七, in a passage about Huo Guang of Han: 「復廢昏立明舉金甌無缺之天下以畀之」, and the variant 金甌無闕 occurs in the Song 羣書會元截江網 卷四 (「金甌無闕之天下」). Both were found by full-text search of zh.Wikisource (the first page's text could not be loaded for a direct read, so that one is taken from the search excerpt), and the search is not exhaustive, so the idiom may be older. In short: the image was coined by the emperor's remark in 548, and the four-character idiom crystallized later, by about the Song period at the latest.

## Standard Form
**金甌無缺**
- 金瓯无缺 — simplified form (甌 → 瓯, 缺 unchanged); current in mainland China
- 金甌無闕 — with the variant 闕 for 缺, as in some Song-period and later texts
- 金甌無欠 — the Japanese form, with 欠 for 缺, which Japanese Wiktionary's entry for the Chinese idiom gives as its gloss (the Japanese usage itself is from memory, not re-checked)

## Cultural Notes
The idiom belongs to the vocabulary of national feeling: a country thought of as a precious vessel, which is either whole or cracked. In Chinese it is a standard phrase of political and historical prose. English Wiktionary labels it literary and gives two senses, "faultless golden chalice" and, figuratively, "firm, uninvaded nation". Several writers use it of the realm kept intact against foreign loss: the 1887 Chinese gazetteer 日本國志 (卷十) says of Japan that, unlike India, Turkey, Annam and Burma, it had "kept its home and state as before, a golden chalice without a flaw"; and the Joseon annals 朝鮮王朝實錄 (孝宗實錄 元年, 宣祖實錄 三十七年) use it of the Korean state ("a state at the height of its strength, a golden chalice without flaw", and "even were the state a flawless golden chalice"), and the travel diary 熱河日記 (卷十) of the Han dynasty keeping the imperial thread unbroken. In Vietnam the same words are written in the court chronicle 大南寔錄 (正編列傳二集, 卷四), in praise of the empress 儷天英皇后, who is said to have "held the realm, a golden chalice without flaw, and handed it on to her descendants". Vietnamese 金甌, kim âu, thus has the same sense of the whole country, and the Cà Mau province and city have a name written with the same two characters 金瓯 (see [[金甌]]); the Vietnamese reading of the full idiom, and its present-day standing in Vietnam, are from memory, not re-checked. For the ironic side of the story, where the boast precedes the collapse, compare [[金城湯池]], another "golden" fortress idiom.

## Example Sentences
1. 我國家猶若金甌，無一傷缺。(the emperor's remark, as in the 南史)
2. 日本自通商以來雖頗受外侮，而家國如故，金甌無缺。(from the 日本國志, 卷十)
3. 守護國土，使之金甌無缺，是每個國民的責任。(a constructed modern example: "Keeping the national territory whole is every citizen's duty.")
4. After the treaty the new state's borders were declared _jīn'ōu wú quē_, whole and inviolate.

## Sentiment
Strongly positive, solemn and patriotic; literary and formal in register. Its original context carries an ironic warning, since the boast was followed by the invasion.
