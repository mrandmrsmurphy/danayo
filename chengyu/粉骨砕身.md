## Literal Meaning
- [粉 (char)](characters/粉%20(char).md) — to grind to powder
- [骨 (char)](characters/骨%20(char).md) — bone
- [砕 (char)](characters/砕%20(char).md) — to shatter, smash
- [身](characters/身.md) — body

Literally: "To grind one's bones to powder and shatter one's body."

## Extended Meaning
粉骨砕身 describes devoting oneself utterly, to the point of sacrificing one's life, for duty, gratitude, or righteousness. It is a phrase of tragic nobility, conveying the Confucian martyr's unwavering loyalty, the Buddhist bodhisattva's vow of self-sacrifice for others, and — in Christian translation — the sense of laying down one's life for love. In modern Japanese and Korean usage it has also softened into a common way to describe simply working oneself to the bone or giving one's utmost effort, without necessarily implying literal death.

## Source and Origin
The expression descends from classical Chinese poetic and Buddhist diction. Its clearest early source is **于謙's 《石灰吟》** ("Ode to Lime," Ming dynasty):

> 「千錘萬擊出深山，烈火焚燒若等閒。粉骨碎身全不怕，要留清白在人間。」
> "A thousand hammer-blows brought it from the deep mountains; it faces the raging fire as though it were nothing. It does not fear having its bones ground and body broken — it only wishes to leave its purity in the world."

Yu Qian, a loyal Ming minister later executed unjustly, used this line (ostensibly about limestone) to symbolize unyielding integrity even unto death — a reading reinforced by his own biography. The phrase also echoes Buddhist sutra imagery of self-sacrifice for compassion or enlightenment, as in the devotional formula 「願粉骨碎身以報佛恩」 ("I would gladly be ground to dust to repay the Buddha's grace"). Together these sources gave the phrase its enduring sense of total devotion to a cause, whether moral, political, or spiritual.

## Standard Form
**粉骨砕身** (Japanese shinjitai 砕) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 粉骨碎身 — traditional Chinese form (碎)
- 粉身碎骨 — a Mainland-common reordering, identical in sense

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese usage 粉骨碎身 remains a literary or moral idiom denoting selfless loyalty to one's ruler, parents, or moral duty. Japanese 粉骨砕身 is still common and has broadened considerably in modern usage to mean simply "to work oneself to the bone" or "devote oneself wholeheartedly" — 粉骨砕身の努力 ("utmost effort") is common corporate and everyday phrasing with no literal violence implied. Korean 분골쇄신 carries both the classical sacrificial-loyalty sense and this same modern "utmost effort" meaning. Vietnamese phấn cốt toái thân appears mainly in poetry and classical education, retaining its literal, devotional weight ("utter devotion to repay grace").

Related in sense (though not variants of the same phrase) is 肝腦塗地 ("to spill one's liver and brains upon the ground"), another hyperbolic image of loyalty or sacrifice.

## Example Sentences
1. 「粉骨碎身全不怕，要留清白在人間。」(于謙《石灰吟》)
2. 願為國家粉骨碎身，無怨無悔。("I am willing to be crushed to dust for my country, with no regrets.")
3. 나라를 위해 분골쇄신하겠다. ("I will sacrifice myself completely for the nation.")
4. In its modern, softened sense: 彼は粉骨砕身の努力をした ("he worked with wholehearted devotion" — no bones literally at risk).

## Sentiment
Tragic nobility, or, in modern softened usage, simple wholehearted effort. Conveys total devotion — historically unto death, today often just unto exhaustion.
