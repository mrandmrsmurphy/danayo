## Literal Meaning
- [開](../characters/開.md) — open
- [天](../characters/天%20(char).md) — sky, heaven
- [辟](../characters/辟.md) — used here as Dan'a'yo's stand-in grapheme for 闢, "to open, to clear (land)" — not its own primary sense "dispel, refute"
- [地](../characters/地%20(char).md) — land, earth

Literally: "To open up Heaven and cleave the Earth."

## Extended Meaning
1. The beginning of the universe; the creation of Heaven and Earth.
2. Any unprecedented or epoch-making event; the start of a new era.

Used mostly in the second, figurative sense in modern speech — praise for a genuinely first-of-its-kind achievement, invention, or turning point, framed at the scale of cosmic beginnings.

## Source and Origin
The image comes from the Chinese creation myth of Pangu (盤古), as told in the **《三五歷紀》** of Xu Zheng (徐整), a work of the Three Kingdoms state of Wu (3rd century CE). The page previously called it a Han work quoted in the 《後漢書》; it is neither. It survives in quotations, notably in the 《藝文類聚》 and the 《太平御覽》 (juan 2), which give:

> 天地渾沌如雞子，盤古生其中，萬八千歲。天地開闢，陽清為天，陰濁為地，盤古在其中，一日九變，神於天，聖於地。天日高一丈，地日厚一丈，盤古日長一丈。如此萬八千歲，天數極高，地數極深，盤古極長，後乃有三皇。
> "Heaven and earth were mixed in chaos like an egg, and Pangu was born within it, for eighteen thousand years. Then heaven and earth were opened up: the clear yang became heaven, the turbid yin became earth. Pangu was within them, changing nine times a day, divine in heaven, sage on earth. Each day heaven rose ten feet, earth thickened ten feet, and Pangu grew ten feet. So it went for eighteen thousand years; heaven became extremely high, earth extremely deep, Pangu extremely tall. Afterward came the Three Sovereigns."

The page's earlier paraphrase of this passage ("陰陽分判") is not the text. The myth gives the phrase its image, though the four-character 開天闢地 is not in it: the text says 天地開闢, "heaven and earth were opened up".

The four-character 開天辟地 is found in the Tang: the 《武德樂》 hymn 《昭烈舞》 in the 《樂府詩集》 (juan 9) has 「開天辟地，峻嶽夷海」, "he opened heaven and cleared the earth, levelled mountains and the sea", used there of the founding of the dynasty. It is common afterwards in Ming and Qing fiction, usually as 開天闢地 or 開天辟地, for the creation of the world or for anything unprecedented, as in the 《盤古至唐虞傳》 (「盤古氏開天闢地」).

## Standard Form
**開天闢地** (traditional) / **开天辟地** (simplified) — Dan'a'yo's filename uses 辟 as the standard stand-in grapheme for 闢 (see Literal Meaning).

## Cultural Notes
In Daoist cosmology, the phrase names the birth of duality (陰陽) from primal chaos (混沌). In Confucian historiography, it marks the starting point of human civilization or dynastic order. In modern usage generally, it's applied to any first or revolutionary act — "an event that opens Heaven and Earth."

## Example Sentences
1. 自開天闢地以來，未有此事。
   "Since the opening of Heaven and Earth, such a thing has never happened."
2. 開天闢地の偉業。
   "An achievement as great as the creation of Heaven and Earth."
3. Từ khi khai thiên tích địa chưa từng có việc ấy.
   "Since the world was first opened, such a thing has never occurred."

## Sentiment
From the very opening of Heaven and Earth — the dawn of all things. A phrase of cosmic beginnings and world-making power, strongly positive, reserved for genuinely first-of-their-kind events.
