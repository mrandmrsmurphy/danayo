## Literal Meaning
- [蒼 (char)](characters/蒼%20(char).md) — blue-green, vast (of the sea; 滄 is a registered alias used in this sense)
- [海](characters/海.md) — sea
- [桑](characters/桑.md) — mulberry
- [田](characters/田.md) — field

Literally: "The blue sea becomes mulberry fields."

## Extended Meaning
蒼海桑田 names the great transformations of the world: the mutability of all things, drastic change over time, the impermanence of worldly affairs on a cosmic scale where even the most enduring things transform. It expresses awe and melancholy before time's vast transformations, and is used across the region for time, loss, and memory — the transformation of landscapes, fortunes, and human lives alike.

## Source and Origin
The phrase originates in Daoist and Buddhist-adjacent writings, with an early and celebrated reference in **《神仙傳》** (Biographies of Immortals), attributed to Ge Hong (葛洪), from the Eastern Jin (4th century CE):

> 麻姑自說云：「接待以來，已見東海三為桑田。向到蓬萊，又水淺於往日會時略半耳，豈將復為陵陸乎？」方平笑曰：「聖人皆言，東海行復揚塵也。」
> (Magu said of herself: "Since I was received [into the immortals], I have seen the Eastern Sea turn into mulberry fields three times. When I recently went to Penglai, the water was again about half as shallow as when we last met — will it become dry land again?" Fang Ping laughed: "The sages all say that the Eastern Sea will once more raise dust.")

The wording is as given in several secondary pages that quote the 《神仙傳》 (the chapter on Magu and Wang Yuan); I could not load a primary text, so I have not read the passage in an edition. The earlier version of this page gave the line as 「麻姑謂王方平曰：『自吾得道以來，已見東海三為桑田。』」, which differs from the wording above in both the speaker's introduction and the phrase 自吾得道, so it has been replaced.

In Chinese mythology the immortal Magu (麻姑), with her vast lifespan, had witnessed the coastline shift over eons — a poetic way to express how even vast seas can dry up and farmland can flood again. The compressed four-character form 滄海桑田 does not occur in the passage, which says 東海三為桑田; I did not establish when it first appears as a set phrase. The earlier statements that Bai Juyi and Su Shi used the phrase in poetry, and that it "appeared frequently" in Tang and Song poetry, were not verified and are removed.

## Standard Form
**蒼海桑田** is the Dan'a'yo standard form, with 滄 (and its own simplified form 沧) treated as a registered alias in this vault.
- 滄海桑田 / 沧海桑田 — traditional/simplified forms using 滄/沧
- 桑田滄海 / 桑田沧海 — reversed word order, same sense ("mulberry fields turn into seas")

## Cultural Notes
In Daoist thought, 蒼海桑田 reflects the natural cycles of transformation — everything changes according to the Dao's ceaseless flow. In Buddhist usage it illustrates impermanence (無常), the illusory stability of worldly forms. In Confucian moral reading it serves as a warning not to trust in wealth or power, since fortune shifts like the sea. In literary usage more broadly it is a sigh of nostalgia for vanished worlds, dynasties, and loves.

Related in sense (though distinct idioms) are 世事無常 ("worldly affairs are impermanent"), 滄桑變化 ("the changes of sea and mulberry field," i.e. great changes), 白雲蒼狗 ("white clouds turn into blue dogs," unpredictable change), 物換星移 ("things change, stars move"), and 盛衰榮辱 ("prosperity and decline, honor and shame").

## Example Sentences
1. 「接待以來，已見東海三為桑田。」(《神仙傳·麻姑》)
2. 世事如夢，滄海桑田。("Worldly affairs are like dreams — the blue sea becomes mulberry fields.")
3. 세상은 창해상전이라, 모든 것이 변한다. ("The world is ever-changing — the blue sea turns to fields.")
4. Looking at old photographs of the neighborhood, she felt the full weight of 蒼海桑田 — nothing left standing that she remembered.

## Sentiment
Awe, melancholy, reflective. Expresses wonder before time's vast transformations — Daoist in its detachment, Buddhist in its compassion, and Confucian in its sober warning against trusting in permanence.
