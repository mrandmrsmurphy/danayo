## Literal Meaning
- [諸 (char)](../characters/諸%20(char).md) — various, all
- [行 (char)](../characters/行%20(char).md) — conditioned phenomena, formations (Buddhist technical sense; not "to go")
- [無 (char)](../characters/無%20(char).md) — without, lacking
- [常](../characters/常.md) — permanence, constancy

Literally: "All conditioned things are without permanence." 行 here translates Sanskrit *saṃskāra* — the Buddhist technical term for compounded, conditioned phenomena — not its ordinary sense of "going" or "conduct."

## Extended Meaning
Everything that arises through causes and conditions is transient: nothing composed or conditioned lasts forever, and all things inevitably change, decay, and pass away. This is one of the foundational insights of Buddhist doctrine — not a general observation about mood or fortune, but a formal teaching about the nature of existence itself.

## Source and Origin
**《大般涅槃經》** (Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra), fascicle 14 of the Mahāyāna version — the verse known as the "Snow Mountain Verse" (雪山偈) or "Verse of Impermanence" (諸行無常偈):
> 諸行無常，是生滅法。生滅滅已，寂滅為樂。
> (All conditioned things are impermanent; this is the nature of arising and passing away. When arising and passing away have ceased, that cessation itself is bliss.)

This verse translates the Sanskrit teaching *sarva-saṃskārā anityāḥ* ("all conditioned phenomena are impermanent") and is one of the **Three Dharma Seals** (三法印) of Buddhism, together with [[諸法無我]] ("all dharmas are without self") and [[涅盤寂静]] ("nirvana is peace").

The verse carries a famous past-life story of the Buddha: as a young ascetic practicing on a snow-covered mountain (the "Snow Mountain Boy," 雪山童子), he heard the god Indra — disguised as a fearsome rākṣasa demon — recite only the first half of the verse. Overjoyed but desperate to hear the rest, the youth agreed to offer his own body as payment. Upon hearing the complete verse, he inscribed it on the rocks and trees around him, then threw himself from a high place to fulfill his promise — at which point Indra revealed his true form, caught him in mid-air, and foretold his eventual Buddhahood. Japan's classical *iroha* poem (いろは歌), once used to teach the kana syllabary, is traditionally understood as a poetic Japanese paraphrase of this same verse.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** zhū xíng wú cháng
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** zyu1 hang4 mou4 soeng4
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** しょぎょうむじょう
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 제행무상 (je-haeng-mu-sang)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** chư hành vô thường

行 is read ぎょう (GYOU) in Japanese and hành in Vietnamese here — each language's distinct "conduct, practice, formation" reading — rather than the more common こう (KOU) / hàng readings tied to 行's "go, travel, row, merchandise" senses. This is the standard split in Buddhist technical vocabulary specifically (cf. 修行 shugyō/tu hành, "religious practice").

## Cultural Notes
In Japan, 諸行無常 is inseparably associated with the opening line of the *Heike Monogatari* (平家物語): 祇園精舎の鐘の声、諸行無常の響きあり ("The bell of Gion Shōja tolls into every heart to warn that all is vanity and evanescence") — a passage so famous that the phrase now evokes the rise and fall of political power and worldly glory as much as it does formal Buddhist doctrine. In Chinese and Korean Buddhist contexts, it retains its primarily doctrinal and philosophical sense, referring straightforwardly to the impermanence of all conditioned phenomena rather than carrying a specific literary association.

## Example Sentences
1. 佛教常說諸行無常，提醒人世間萬物皆會變化。
2. The monk's sermon returned again and again to _zhū xíng wú cháng_ — nothing that arises can last.
3. 平家物語は諸行無常の思想で知られている。
4. Even her most treasured possessions, she reflected, were subject to _chư hành vô thường_.

## Sentiment
Reflective and solemn rather than negative; a foundational statement of Buddhist doctrine, often evoking both philosophical acceptance and a wistful sense of loss or transience.
