[Biblical Chengyu](chengyu/Biblical%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [豹](characters/豹.md) — leopard
- [斑](characters/斑.md) — spots, markings
- [改 (char)](characters/改%20(char).md) — to change
- [乎 (char)](characters/乎%20(char).md) — rhetorical question particle

Literally: "The leopard's spots change? [rhetorical: they do not]"

## Extended Meaning
豹斑改乎 rhetorically denies that a deeply ingrained nature or habit can be changed by will alone — used to argue that someone habituated to a certain character or behavior (usually negative) cannot simply reform themselves through ordinary effort. It carries a pessimistic view of human self-improvement: real change, in the Biblical framing, requires something beyond the person's own resolve. Used to comment on stubborn character flaws, recidivism, or the futility of expecting a fundamentally unchanged person to act differently.

## Source and Origin
**Jeremiah 13:23**:

> "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil." (Jer 13:23, ESV)

豹斑改乎 isolates the second half of the verse's parallel rhetorical question, rendering it as a compact four-character idiom. The final 乎 preserves the interrogative-rhetorical form of the original Hebrew question (a question expecting the answer "no"), rather than flattening it into a plain declarative statement — a structural choice that keeps the idiom's force as a challenge rather than a mere assertion.

## Variants
- 豹能改斑乎 — a fuller rendering making the modal "can" explicit ("can the leopard change its spots?")
- 豹斑可改 — a declarative (non-rhetorical) statement form ("the leopard's spots can be changed")
- 古実人豹斑改乎 — including the verse's parallel image of the Cushite/Ethiopian changing his skin

## Cultural Notes
豹斑改乎 is used across the CJKV Christian sphere in the same register as the English "a leopard can't change its spots" — pointing out the futility of expecting deep character change without genuine transformation. The leopard-spots metaphor for unchangeable nature is itself cross-culturally intuitive (comparable idioms exist independently in many languages), so the idiom lands easily even for readers unfamiliar with its specific Biblical source, while still functioning as a recognizable scriptural citation in explicitly religious contexts.

## Example Sentences
1. 「古實人豈能改變皮膚呢？豹豈能改變身上的斑點呢？若能，你們這習慣行惡的便能行善了。」(Jer 13:23, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 惡人之性如豹斑改乎，難以自變。("The nature of the wicked is like [asking] can a leopard change its spots — difficult to change by oneself.")
3. 習久成性，豹斑改乎？("When habit becomes nature, can a leopard change its spots?")
4. He'd promised to change a dozen times before, and she'd learned by now: 豹斑改乎 — some things simply don't.

## Sentiment
Rhetorical, pessimistic regarding human self-reform. Used to express the impossibility of changing deeply ingrained character or habits without external (divine) help.
