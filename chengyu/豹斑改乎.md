## Literal Meaning
- [豹](characters/豹.md) — leopard
- [斑](characters/斑.md) — spots, markings
- [改 (char)](characters/改%20(char).md) — to change
- [乎 (char)](characters/乎%20(char).md) — rhetorical question particle

Literally: "The leopard's spots change? [rhetorical: they do not]"

## Extended Meaning
豹斑改乎 rhetorically denies that a deeply ingrained nature or habit can be changed by will alone — used to argue that someone habituated to a certain character or behavior (usually negative) cannot simply reform themselves through ordinary effort. It carries a pessimistic view of human self-improvement: real change, in the Biblical framing, requires something beyond the person's own resolve. Used to comment on stubborn character flaws, recidivism, or the futility of expecting a fundamentally unchanged person to act differently.

## Source and Origin
**Jeremiah 13:23**:

> "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil." (Jer 13:23, ESV)

The Chinese Union Version (和合本) renders the verse 「古實人豈能改變皮膚呢？豹豈能改變斑點呢？若能，你們這習慣行惡的便能行善了。」 (checked on cnbible.com, which shows the same wording in the 現代標點和合本; the 聖經新譯本 reads 「古實人怎能改變他的膚色？花豹怎能改變牠的斑點？你們這些慣作壞事的人，又怎能行善呢？」). The Chinese Bible's own phrase for the leopard clause is therefore 豹豈能改變斑點呢.

I found **no attestation of 豹斑改乎 as a fixed idiom** in Chinese, Japanese or Korean sources. It appears to be a Dan'a'yo four-character coinage that isolates the leopard half of the verse's parallel rhetorical questions. The final 乎 keeps the question form, a rhetorical challenge expecting the answer "no," rather than flattening it into a plain declarative. The earlier description of it as an established form in the Bible-translation tradition was unsupported and has been removed.

## Variants
The attested Chinese rendering is the Chinese Union Version sentence above. Three variants listed here earlier (豹能改斑乎, 豹斑可改, 古実人豹斑改乎) could not be found in any source and have been removed.

## Cultural Notes
豹斑改乎 is used in the same register as the English "a leopard can't change its spots": it points out the futility of expecting deep character change without genuine transformation. The leopard-spots image for an unchangeable nature is cross-culturally intuitive, so it lands easily even for readers unfamiliar with its Biblical source, while remaining a recognizable scriptural citation in religious contexts. Because 豹斑改乎 is not attested as a fixed idiom, no claim is made here about how common it is in any particular country.

## Example Sentences
1. 「古實人豈能改變皮膚呢？豹豈能改變斑點呢？若能，你們這習慣行惡的便能行善了。」(Jer 13:23, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 惡人之性如豹斑改乎，難以自變。("The nature of the wicked is like [asking] can a leopard change its spots — difficult to change by oneself.")
3. 習久成性，豹斑改乎？("When habit becomes nature, can a leopard change its spots?")
4. He'd promised to change a dozen times before, and she'd learned by now: 豹斑改乎 — some things simply don't.

## Sentiment
Rhetorical, pessimistic regarding human self-reform. Used to express the impossibility of changing deeply ingrained character or habits without external (divine) help.
