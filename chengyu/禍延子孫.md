[Biblical Chengyu](chengyu/Biblical%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [禍](../characters/禍.md) — calamity, disaster
- [延](../characters/延.md) — extend, prolong
- [子孫](../words/子孫.md) — descendants ([子](../characters/子.md) + [孫](../characters/孫.md))

Literally: "calamity extends to descendants."

## Extended Meaning
A warning that the consequences of serious, entrenched wrongdoing are not confined to the wrongdoer but extend into the lives of their descendants. Applied biblically, it names the covenantal, conditional principle that persistent sin carries generational weight — without teaching mechanical fate, denying individual responsibility, or overriding the possibility of repentance.

## Source and Origin
禍延子孫 is itself a pre-existing classical-style Chinese expression, attested in Chinese moral and historical discourse describing political downfall, clan punishment, or the long shadow of wrongdoing within family lines, without inherent theological specification. The family maxim collection **《朱柏廬治家格言》** (17th century) contains the line "匿怨而用暗箭，禍延子孫" ("harboring a grudge while striking in secret brings calamity down upon one's descendants"), and a related conceptual pairing appears in **《周易·坤·文言》**: "積善之家，必有餘慶；積不善之家，必有餘殃" ("a household that accumulates good deeds will have surplus blessing; a household that accumulates ill deeds will have surplus calamity") — not the exact four-character formula, but the same older concern that a family's fortune or misfortune extends beyond any one individual's life.

Dan'a'yo repurposes this pre-existing secular idiom for a specifically biblical principle, articulated in **Exodus 20:5**, **Deuteronomy 5:9**, and **Exodus 34:7**:

> "...visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me." (Exodus 20:5, KJV)

Traditional Chinese Bible translations render this as "追討他的罪，自父及子，直到三四代" ("visiting his sin from father to son, to the third and fourth generation"). In biblical theology this generational extension is covenantal and conditional: it applies specifically to those who persist in hatred of God, and is immediately contrasted in the same passage with mercy shown "unto thousands" of those who love him — an asymmetry in which mercy far outweighs judgment. Later prophetic texts further nuance the principle: **Ezekiel 18** emphasizes personal accountability directly, and **Jeremiah 31:29–30** explicitly rejects simplistic generational blame ("the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"), so that in Scripture generational consequence coexists with, rather than overriding, individual moral agency.

## Cultural Notes
Because 禍延子孫 predates its biblical application, its adoption into Dan'a'yo represents a deliberate theological reorientation: in classical discourse it describes inherited misfortune generically, while in biblical application it describes covenantal judgment within a moral framework governed by a just and merciful God. Standing alone, the phrase can sound fatalistic; within the larger biblical arc it is restrained by divine patience, the promise of surpassing mercy, and the possibility of repentance and restoration. Within the Dan'a'yo Christian sphere, the expression serves as a sober warning about the historical weight of sin rather than a doctrine of inherited guilt.

## Example Sentences
1. 若一族長久悖逆，或見禍延子孫，然悔改亦可止其延。("If a household persists in rebellion, calamity may extend to descendants; yet repentance may arrest its course.")
2. 「必追討他的罪，自父及子，直到三、四代。」(出埃及記 20:5，和合本 Chinese Union Version)
3. Ezekiel 18 tempers the older warning of 禍延子孫 by insisting each soul answers for its own sin.
4. 家風敗壞，恐禍延子孫；然神之憐憫，遠勝於怒。("A corrupted family legacy risks calamity extending to descendants; yet God's mercy far exceeds his anger.")

## Sentiment
Sobering, cautionary, historically conscious. Warns that actions reverberate across generations, yet remains bounded by the larger biblical arc of mercy.
