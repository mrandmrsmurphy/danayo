## Literal Meaning
- [呉](characters/呉.md) — Wu (a Spring and Autumn period state)
- [越 (char)](characters/越%20(char).md) — Yue (a Spring and Autumn period state)
- [同](characters/同.md) — same, together
- [舟](characters/舟.md) — boat

Literally: "Wu and Yue in the same boat."

## Extended Meaning
呉越同舟 describes enemies or rivals who, thrown together by shared danger or circumstance, cooperate despite their hostility — a moral of necessity and solidarity, where shared peril dissolves division. The sense has broadened over time from literal survival to a general metaphor for political alliances, teamwork, or temporary cooperation among rivals. Used to describe people or groups who must cooperate despite rivalry, especially in crisis, in political commentary, journalism, and everyday description of "strange bedfellows" or "coalitions of convenience."

## Source and Origin
The idiom originates from the Warring States period text **《孫子兵法·九地篇》** (*The Art of War*, "On the Nine Grounds"), traditionally attributed to **Sunzi (Sun Tzu)**:

> 故吳人與越人相惡也，當其同舟而濟於江，則相救如左右手。
> "Thus, though the men of Wu and Yue hate each other, when they are crossing the river together in the same boat, they will save one another as if their own hands and feet."

This line draws on the historical enmity between the states of Wu (吳) and Yue (越), fierce rivals in the 5th century BC during the Spring and Autumn period. Yet when caught in the same peril — a storm at sea — even bitter enemies instinctively cooperate to survive. Sunzi uses the image to argue that a general can induce the same cohesion in his own troops by placing them in a position where cooperation is the only path to survival.

## Standard Form
**呉越同舟** (Japanese shinjitai 呉) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 吳越同舟 — traditional Chinese form (吳)
- 吴越同舟 — simplified Chinese form (吴)

## Variants
- 吳楚同舟 — a poetic variant substituting Chu (楚) for Yue, used in some Tang-era verse

## Cultural Notes
In Confucian ethics, 呉越同舟 reflects the pragmatic virtue of 仁 (*ren*, humaneness) and 義 (*yi*, righteousness) transcending rivalry when life and duty are at stake. In Daoist terms, it illustrates the fluid balance of opposites — even antagonistic forces harmonizing under pressure, like water finding its level in the same vessel. In Buddhist interpretation, it can be read allegorically: all sentient beings share the same "boat of existence" (生死之舟) and must cooperate on the path to liberation.

Modern Chinese usage describes people or groups who must cooperate despite rivalry, especially in crisis. Japanese ごえつどうしゅう (goetsu dōshū) appears in both historical writing and metaphorical political contexts for uneasy alliances. Korean 오월동주 (owol dongju) appears frequently in journalism and political commentary to describe unlikely cooperation or coalitions of convenience. Vietnamese Ngô Việt đồng chu retains its classical form in literature and proverbs, meaning "enemies joined by fate."

Closely related in sense (though not variants of the same phrase) are 同舟共濟 ("to cross the river together in one boat; to work together in difficulty"), 化敵為友 ("to turn enemies into friends"), 患難與共 ("to share hardship together"), and 共渡難關 ("to overcome adversity together").

## Example Sentences
1. 「故吳人與越人相惡也，當其同舟而濟於江，則相救如左右手。」(《孫子兵法·九地篇》)
2. 雖然他們平時競爭激烈，但在危難之時也能吳越同舟。("Though they usually compete fiercely, in times of trouble they can still unite like Wu and Yue in the same boat.")
3. 여야는 오월동주의 처지에 놓였다. ("The ruling and opposition parties are now like Wu and Yue in the same boat.")
4. The two rival firms, facing the same lawsuit, found themselves in an uneasy 呉越同舟 partnership overnight.

## Sentiment
Pragmatic, uneasy, cooperative. Rivals bound by circumstance rather than affection — the storm makes temporary brothers of foes.
