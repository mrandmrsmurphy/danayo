[Biblical Chengyu](chengyu/Biblical%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [不 (char)](../characters/不%20(char).md) — not
- [打](../characters/打.md) — to strike, to discipline
- [成 (char)](../characters/成%20(char).md) — to become, to form
- [器](../characters/器.md) — vessel, implement, useful instrument

Literally: "without striking, it does not become a vessel." The imagery evokes shaping metal or pottery — raw material requires force and refinement to become useful.

## Extended Meaning
Discipline is necessary for the proper formation of character. Applied to children, it conveys the principle that correction, including corporal discipline, is part of moral training — the English parallel "spare the rod, spoil the child" expresses the same idea.

## Source and Origin
不打不成器 is itself a pre-existing Chinese folk proverb rather than a Dan'a'yo coinage from Scripture directly, closely related to the line "玉不琢，不成器" ("jade uncut does not become a vessel") found in the Song-dynasty primer **《三字經》** (Three Character Classic), which continues with "人不學，不知義" ("a person untaught does not know righteousness"), likening the shaping of jade to the disciplined formation of character through education.

The proverb parallels several passages in the biblical book of **Proverbs** closely enough that Dan'a'yo adopts it as a Bible-tradition chengyu:

> "He that spareth his rod hateth his son: but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes." (Proverbs 13:24, KJV)

> "Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him." (Proverbs 22:15, KJV)

> "Withhold not correction from the child: for if thou beatest him with the rod, he shall not die." (Proverbs 23:13, KJV)

The English proverb "spare the rod, spoil the child" derives historically from these same texts, though it is not a verbatim quotation of Scripture. The Dan'a'yo idiom borrows the pre-existing Chinese proverb intact, since it already renders the same pedagogical principle in classical, memorable form.

## Cultural Notes
In biblical wisdom literature the "rod" symbolizes authority and corrective discipline rather than uncontrolled anger — the goal is formation, not harm. The metaphor of 器 ("vessel") is significant in East Asian thought more broadly: a vessel suggests usefulness, purpose, and moral shape, and Confucian educational ideals (as in 《三字經》) likewise stress disciplined cultivation as the path to a formed, useful character. Modern contexts complicate the reception of this maxim, since contemporary discussion distinguishes carefully between structured discipline and abuse; within Christian theology, discipline is properly framed within covenantal love and parental responsibility, echoing Hebrews 12:6 on divine chastening ("For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth"). Within the Dan'a'yo Christian sphere, the saying emphasizes formation through correction rather than endorsing severity for its own sake.

## Example Sentences
1. 教養之道，在愛與戒並行；不打不成器，然亦不可過度。("The way of upbringing joins love and correction; without discipline one is not formed, yet excess is also wrong.")
2. 「不可不管教孩童，你用杖打他，他必不至於死。」(箴言 23:13，和合本 Chinese Union Version)
3. Parents debating discipline often invoke 不打不成器 as a defense of firm correction, though modern voices caution against taking it too literally.
4. 玉不琢不成器，人不教不知義；不打不成器之訓，正本於此。("Jade uncut does not become a vessel; a person untaught does not know righteousness — the teaching 'without discipline, no formation' is rooted precisely here.")

## Sentiment
Didactic, firm, morally instructive. Conveys seriousness about character formation and parental responsibility.
