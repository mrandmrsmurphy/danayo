## Literal Meaning
- [一](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [石](../characters/石%20(char).md) — stone
- [二](../characters/二%20(char).md) — two
- [鳥](../characters/鳥%20(char).md) — bird

Literally: "one stone, two birds."

## Extended Meaning
Achieving two results with a single action; a single effort producing double benefit. Fully standard and stylistically neutral — it appears in speech, writing, education, business, and media, carrying no literary register and no moral weight beyond efficiency. It simply names an optimization.

## Source and Origin
Unlike most chengyu, 一石二鳥 is **not** a Classical Chinese idiom. It is a calque of the English proverb "to kill two birds with one stone," which Japanese Wikipedia dates to 17th-century Britain, and it states outright that the four-character idiom, though firmly established in Japan, "is not a word from the Chinese classics" because it originated in Britain. English Wiktionary treats it as a Japanese-made Sino-compound (和製漢語): the Japanese entry is the calque of the English phrase, and the Chinese entry is orthographically borrowed from Japanese, not the other way round. From there it also passed into Korean and Vietnamese as part of the shared modern Sino-lexical layer.

**Corrected.** This page previously said the phrase was introduced into written Chinese in the late nineteenth to early twentieth century directly through translation from English. I found no support for that route; the sources above point through Japanese.

**Not verified.** I did not establish when the phrase was first coined in Japanese, or its earliest Chinese use. Reference pages I consulted disagreed or were inconsistent on the Japanese date, so none is stated here. I also did not search Chinese texts exhaustively, so "no premodern Chinese textual source" below means that none was found.

Classical Chinese contains structurally similar ideas, but not this specific image of stone and birds. What is notable is how smoothly the phrase naturalized: its four-character structure fits chengyu norms perfectly, and its causal logic aligns with long-standing pragmatic reasoning in Chinese prose. As a result, it is often mistaken for an ancient idiom despite its modern origin.

## Standard Form
**一石二鳥** (Dan'a'yo, using shinjitai 鳥)
- 一石二鸟 — simplified Chinese form
- Both forms current; the underlying idiom is identical, reflecting only the ordinary traditional/simplified script split rather than any separate history.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** yì shí èr niǎo
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** jat1 sek6 ji6 niu5
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** いっせきにちょう
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 일석이조 (il-seok i-jo)
- **Vietnamese (Sino-Vietnamese):** nhất thạch nhị điểu

## Cultural Notes
Across the region, 一石二鳥 is fully standard and stylistically neutral in all five CJKV languages that use it, appearing across registers with no special literary weight. Its simplified alias 一石二鸟 reflects the same modern-layer status — a calque doesn't accumulate the kind of orthographic history a classical idiom does, so there's little regional variation beyond the ordinary traditional/simplified split.

## Example Sentences
1. 這個方法既省時又省錢，真是一石二鳥。("This method saves both time and money — truly killing two birds with one stone.")
2. By combining the trip with the conference, she managed a 一石二鳥 outcome.
3. To kill two birds with one stone, he studied Japanese while watching anime — 一石二鳥的做法。

## Sentiment
Positive and practical; approving of efficiency rather than emotionally charged. No irony or ambivalence — unlike many classical idioms, it carries no baggage beyond "well done, that was efficient."

## Usage Note
一石二鳥 is a good example of a "false antique." Its form signals classical authority, but its history is modern — useful as a diagnostic case when distinguishing inherited literary idioms from translated conceptual vocabulary.
