## Literal Meaning
- [意](../characters/意.md) — mind, spirit, resolve
- [気](../characters/気%20(char).md) — energy, vital force, morale
- [揚](../characters/揚%20(char).md) — raised, lifted
- [揚](../characters/揚%20(char).md) — raised, lifted (reduplicated for intensity)

Literally: "spirit raised high and on display."

## Extended Meaning
High-spirited, swaggering, flushed with confidence or triumph; openly buoyant morale. 意氣 denotes inner resolve, temper, and morale rather than abstract intention; 揚揚 describes that inner state being lifted and visibly carried. The compound therefore names not mere confidence but confidence *on display* — an outward strut, not just an inward feeling. It is used to describe people who are visibly confident or triumphant, sometimes with an admiring tone and sometimes with a hint of irony; context determines whether the confidence read is justified, premature, or performative.

## Source and Origin
**《史記·項羽本紀》** ("Records of the Grand Historian, Biography of Xiang Yu"), compiled by Sima Qian (司馬遷) c. 91 BCE. The chapter narrates the final days of Xiang Yu (項羽), besieged at Gaixia:

> 項王軍壁垓下，兵少食盡，漢軍及諸侯軍圍之數重。……項王乃夜起，飲帳中，與美人同飲。歌曰：力拔山兮氣蓋世，時不利兮騅不逝。騅不逝兮可奈何？虞兮虞兮奈若何！
> (King Xiang's army was walled in at Gaixia, his soldiers few and his provisions spent; the Han army and the armies of the various lords surrounded him in several rings. … King Xiang rose that night and drank within his tent, together with his beloved lady. He sang: "My strength plucked up mountains, my spirit covered the world / but the times were against me, and Dapple will not go / Dapple will not go — what then can I do? / Yu, oh Yu, what will become of you!")

The four-character phrase itself is not in Sima Qian's own text; it comes from commentators glossing this scene. Pei Yin (裴駰), in his *Shiji* commentary, describes Xiang Yu's earlier demeanor — at the height of his power, before the siege — as **"意氣揚揚"**, a swaggering high spirits that the final song's despair throws into sharp relief. Tang poets later reused the same register: *意氣風發* and *意氣揚揚* both appear describing exuberant scholars or generals, and modern chengyu dictionaries gloss the phrase as 得意的樣子；神采飛揚 ("the appearance of being pleased with oneself; high-spirited and triumphant"). In the Xiang Yu narrative this outward buoyancy is pointedly juxtaposed with an impending reversal, giving the phrase — even when used elsewhere, of other subjects — a faint edge of instability: elevation that invites gravity.

## Standard Form
**意気揚揚** (Dan'a'yo, using Japanese shinjitai 意/気)
- 意氣揚揚 — traditional Chinese form
- 意气扬扬 — simplified Chinese form

All three are the same idiom; Dan'a'yo standardizes on the shinjitai spelling.

## Cultural Notes
Across the Sinosphere, 意気揚揚 describes people who are visibly confident or triumphant — narrative prose, reportage, and evaluative description all use it freely, and it sits close to praise without quite endorsing the judgment. It reports posture and mood, not outcome; in narrative contexts it often precedes contrast, reversal, or correction, an echo of its own origin scene. In Japanese, the phrase carries a slightly more literary flavor than in everyday Mandarin, where it doubles as a common descriptive collocation for confident body language.

## Example Sentences
1. 他意氣揚揚地走進會場，彷彿早已勝券在握。("He strode into the hall triumphant, as if victory were already assured.")
2. After winning the debate, she walked off the stage 意気揚揚, chin up and smiling.
3. His 意気揚揚 manner before the trial made the later verdict sting all the more.

## Sentiment
Positive on the surface — confident, triumphant, buoyant — but carrying an unstable edge inherited from its origin story: a mood of triumph poised right before a fall. Can be read admiringly or ironically depending on context.
