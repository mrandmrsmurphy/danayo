## Literal Meaning
- [意](../characters/意.md) — mind, spirit, resolve
- [気](../characters/気%20(char).md) — energy, vital force, morale
- [揚](../characters/揚%20(char).md) — raised, lifted
- [揚](../characters/揚%20(char).md) — raised, lifted (reduplicated for intensity)

Literally: "spirit raised high and on display."

## Extended Meaning
High-spirited and visibly pleased with oneself; swaggering, flushed with confidence or triumph. 意氣 denotes inner resolve, temper, and morale rather than abstract intention; 揚揚 describes that inner state being lifted and visibly carried. The compound therefore names not mere confidence but confidence *on display* — an outward strut, not just an inward feeling. It is used to describe people who are visibly confident or triumphant, and since the source scene is a rebuke of complacency (see below), it often carries a note of smugness; context determines whether the confidence read is justified, premature, or performative.

## Source and Origin
**《史記·管晏列傳》** ("Records of the Grand Historian, Biographies of Guan Zhong and Yan Ying," chapter 62), by Sima Qian (司馬遷). The phrase 意氣揚揚 itself occurs in the anecdote of the charioteer of Yan Ying (晏子), prime minister of Qi, checked against the Wikisource text:

> "晏子為齊相，出，其御之妻從門閒而闚其夫。其夫為相御，擁大蓋，策駟馬，**意氣揚揚**甚自得也。既而歸，其妻請去。夫問其故。妻曰：『晏子長不滿六尺，身相齊國，名顯諸侯。今者妾觀其出，志念深矣，常有以自下者。今子長八尺，乃為人僕御，然子之意自以為足，妾是以求去也。』其後夫自抑損。晏子怪而問之，御以實對。晏子薦以為大夫。"
> (When Yan Ying was prime minister of Qi, his charioteer's wife peeked through the gate at her husband. He sat beneath a great canopy, whipping the four horses, **swaggering with high spirits** and thoroughly pleased with himself. When he came home, his wife asked to leave him. He asked why. She said: "Yan Ying is under six feet tall, yet he is prime minister of Qi and his name is known among the lords. Today I watched him go out, and his thoughts were deep; he always keeps himself humble. You are eight feet tall, yet you are only someone's driver, and still you are satisfied with yourself. That is why I want to leave." After that her husband restrained himself. Yan Ying wondered at the change and asked, and the charioteer told him the truth; Yan Ying recommended him as a court grandee.)

So the idiom is a four-character crystallization of Sima Qian's own wording, and its original sense is a **smug, self-satisfied** swagger that is rebuked, not an admiring triumph.

**Corrected.** The earlier version of this page claimed the phrase came from 《史記·項羽本紀》 and from a commentary by Pei Yin describing Xiang Yu. I checked the Xiang Yu chapter: the string 意氣揚揚 (and 揚揚) does not occur in it, and I did not find the claimed Pei Yin comment, so the Gaixia scene and the "instability before a fall" reading have been removed. Claims about Tang poets reusing the phrase were not checked and are not repeated.

## Standard Form
**意気揚揚** (Dan'a'yo, using Japanese shinjitai 意/気)
- 意氣揚揚 — traditional Chinese form
- 意气扬扬 — simplified Chinese form

All three are the same idiom; Dan'a'yo standardizes on the shinjitai spelling.

## Cultural Notes
Across the Sinosphere, 意気揚揚 describes people who are visibly confident or triumphant — narrative prose, reportage, and evaluative description all use it freely. It reports posture and mood, not outcome, and because its source anecdote criticizes the charioteer's self-satisfaction, it can carry an undertone of complacency or showing off, though it is also used neutrally for plain high spirits. In Japanese, the phrase carries a slightly more literary flavor than in everyday Mandarin, where it doubles as a common descriptive collocation for confident body language.

## Example Sentences
1. 他意氣揚揚地走進會場，彷彿早已勝券在握。("He strode into the hall triumphant, as if victory were already assured.")
2. After winning the debate, she walked off the stage 意気揚揚, chin up and smiling.
3. His 意気揚揚 manner before the trial made the later verdict sting all the more.

## Sentiment
Mixed. Positive on the surface — confident, triumphant, buoyant — but with a note of smugness inherited from the charioteer anecdote, where the swagger is the thing rebuked. Can be read admiringly, neutrally or ironically depending on context.
