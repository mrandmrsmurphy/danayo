## Literal Meaning
- [貪 (char)](../characters/貪%20(char).md) — greedy
- [官](../characters/官.md) — officer, official
- [汚 (char)](../characters/汚%20(char).md) — dirty, corrupt
- [吏](../characters/吏.md) — clerk, functionary

Literally: "corrupt officials and filthy clerks."

## Extended Meaning
Greedy, dishonest bureaucrats; officials who abuse their power for personal gain. It encapsulates a perennial theme of Chinese political ethics — when virtue fails among those in power, corruption spreads through the whole realm.

## Source and Origin
The earliest attestation of the fixed four-character idiom appears in the Yuan-dynasty drama **《鴛鴦被》** ("The Mandarin Duck Quilt"), Act 4:

> "一應貪官污吏，准許先斬後聞。"
> ("All corrupt officials [of this kind] — permission is granted to execute first and report afterward.")

The two component terms are themselves older: 貪官 ("greedy official," one who enriches himself through bribes and extortion) and 汚吏/污吏 ("filthy clerk," a petty bureaucrat who sullies the administration of justice) circulated separately before being fixed into this compound. The idiom became especially prominent in Ming and Qing vernacular fiction and in memorials submitted by upright censors decrying moral decay in governance.

## Cultural Notes
貪官汚吏 is more than a political insult — within the Confucian view of government as a moral institution, it represents a direct inversion of virtuous rule, as in the *Analects* (2:1): "為政以德，譬如北辰，居其所而眾星共之" ("to govern by virtue is like the North Star: it stays in its place, and all the other stars pay homage to it"). When officials pursue private gain instead of public duty, this cosmic and moral harmony between ruler and ruled is understood to collapse, and the idiom becomes a sign of dynastic decline in traditional historiography. It functions as a moral archetype in Chinese literature, the foil against which celebrated upright officials (清官, *qīng guān*) such as Bao Zheng (包拯) and Hai Rui (海瑞) are held up as contrasting ideals. The idiom remains vivid and current: still widely used in journalism and political speech across the Sinophone world to denounce corruption, and its Sino-Korean form 탐관오리 (*tamgwan-ori*) is a fixed phrase in Korean history books and political rhetoric, especially describing corrupt local magistrates of the late Joseon period.

## Example Sentences
1. 除去貪官汚吏，還我清平世界。("Remove the corrupt officials, and return us to a world of purity and peace.")
2. 「一應貪官污吏，准許先斬後聞。」(元·無名氏《鴛鴦被》第四折)
3. 조선 말기의 탐관오리들이 민심을 잃게 했다。("The corrupt officials of late Joseon lost the people's trust.")
4. Tham quan ô lại làm dân oán trời than đất。("Greedy officials make the people curse Heaven and Earth.")

## Sentiment
Strongly negative, condemnatory. A direct moral and political insult with no ambiguity or irony.
