## Literal Meaning
- [貪 (char)](../characters/貪%20(char).md) — greedy
- [官](../characters/官.md) — officer, official
- [汚 (char)](../characters/汚%20(char).md) — dirty, corrupt
- [吏](../characters/吏.md) — clerk, functionary

Literally: "corrupt officials and filthy clerks."

## Extended Meaning
Greedy, dishonest bureaucrats; officials who abuse their power for personal gain. It encapsulates a perennial theme of Chinese political ethics — when virtue fails among those in power, corruption spreads through the whole realm.

## Source and Origin
Taiwan's Ministry of Education *Dictionary of Chinese Idioms* gives as the source (典源) the anonymous Yuan-dynasty drama **《鴛鴦被》** ("The Mandarin Duck Quilt"), Act 4, which is the earliest occurrence the Ministry documents (I did not search for earlier ones, and I checked the quotation against the Ministry's entry via a search summary, not the play's text):

> "一應貪官污吏，准許先斬後聞。"
> ("All corrupt officials [of this kind] — permission is granted to execute first and report afterward.")

The entry lists later documented uses in 《喻世明言》 vol. 39 and 《兒女英雄傳》 chapter 8. The two component terms are said to be older: 貪官 ("greedy official," one who enriches himself through bribes and extortion) and 汚吏/污吏 ("filthy clerk," a petty bureaucrat who sullies the administration of justice) circulated separately before being fixed into this compound. The idiom became especially prominent in Ming and Qing vernacular fiction and in memorials submitted by upright censors decrying moral decay in governance.

## Cultural Notes
貪官汚吏 is more than a political insult — within the Confucian view of government as a moral institution, it represents a direct inversion of virtuous rule, as in the *Analects* (2:1): "為政以德，譬如北辰，居其所而眾星共之" ("to govern by virtue is like the North Star: it stays in its place, and all the other stars pay homage to it"). When officials pursue private gain instead of public duty, this cosmic and moral harmony between ruler and ruled is understood to collapse, and the idiom becomes a sign of dynastic decline in traditional historiography. It functions as a moral archetype in Chinese literature, the foil against which celebrated upright officials (清官, *qīng guān*) such as Bao Zheng (包拯) and Hai Rui (海瑞) are held up as contrasting ideals. The idiom is used to denounce corruption (I did not check how widely), and the Sino-Korean 탐관오리 is the same phrase (I did not check how it is used in Korean history writing or how common it is).

## Example Sentences
1. 除去貪官汚吏，還我清平世界。("Remove the corrupt officials, and return us to a world of purity and peace.")
2. 「一應貪官污吏，准許先斬後聞。」(元·無名氏《鴛鴦被》第四折)
3. 조선 말기의 탐관오리들이 민심을 잃게 했다。("The corrupt officials of late Joseon lost the people's trust.")
4. Tham quan ô lại làm dân oán trời than đất。("Greedy officials make the people curse Heaven and Earth.")

## Sentiment
Strongly negative, condemnatory. A direct moral and political insult with no ambiguity or irony.
