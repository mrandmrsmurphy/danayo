## Literal Meaning
- [乾坤](../words/乾坤.md) — heaven and earth, the whole universe
- [一 (char)](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one, a single
- [擲](../characters/擲.md) — to throw, to cast

Literally: "heaven and earth in a single throw." The phrase's grandeur comes from juxtaposing 乾坤 (heaven and earth — the whole universe) with 一擲 (one throw), symbolizing total commitment or existential risk.

## Extended Meaning
To stake everything on one move; to risk it all on a single decisive action, whether in battle, politics, business, or a major life decision.

## Source and Origin
The phrase originates in the Tang-dynasty poet **韓愈** (Han Yu)'s poem **《過鴻溝》** ("Passing Honggou"), composed as he traveled through the site of the ancient Hong Canal (鴻溝), the boundary line where Liu Bang (the future Han emperor) and Xiang Yu (King of Chu) had agreed to divide the empire and end their long civil war around 203 BC:

> "龍疲虎困割川原，億萬蒼生性命存。誰勸君王回馬首，真成一擲賭乾坤。"
> ("Dragon exhausted, tiger spent, they divided the land by the river; a million common lives were thereby spared. Who was it urged the king to turn his horse around? — truly, heaven and earth were wagered on a single throw.")

The poem reflects on how Liu Bang, having agreed to the truce, was persuaded by his strategist Zhang Liang to break it and launch a surprise attack on the retreating Xiang Yu — an irreversible, all-or-nothing gamble that ultimately won him the empire. The four-character idiom 乾坤一擲 crystallized from the poem's closing line, "一擲賭乾坤" ("wagering heaven and earth on a single throw"), rearranged into its now-standard order.

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese usage the idiom suggests courage, decisiveness, and fate-defying audacity, though carrying a hint of recklessness alongside its heroism. It is often mentioned alongside related idioms of no-turning-back commitment, such as 破釜沈舟 ("break the cauldrons and sink the boats") and 背水一戰 ("fight with one's back to the river"). In Japanese, 乾坤一擲 (kenkon itteki) became a popular idiom in military rhetoric, meaning "a do-or-die gamble," notably during the Meiji and wartime eras. Korean 건곤일척 is a common 사자성어 used in political or sports commentary for a single, decisive gamble. Vietnamese kiền khôn nhất trịch (colloquially also rendered càn khôn nhất trịch) keeps the same dramatic sense of a heaven-and-earth wager.

## Example Sentences
1. 這一場戰役，是我軍的乾坤一擲。("This battle is our army's do-or-die gamble.")
2. 「誰勸君王回馬首，真成一擲賭乾坤。」(韓愈《過鴻溝》)
3. 彼は乾坤一擲の賭けに出た。("He staked everything on a single throw.")
4. 이번 경기는 건곤일척의 승부다。("This match is a decisive, all-or-nothing contest.")

## Sentiment
Heroic daring mixed with existential fatalism — echoes classical valor and total commitment, whether admired as courage or viewed with a hint of caution toward recklessness.
