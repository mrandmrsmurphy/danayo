[Misc. Chengyu](chengyu/Misc.%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [乾坤](../words/乾坤.md) — heaven and earth, the whole universe
- [一 (char)](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one, a single
- [擲](../characters/擲.md) — to throw, to cast

Literally: "heaven and earth in a single throw." The phrase's grandeur comes from juxtaposing 乾坤 (heaven and earth — the whole universe) with 一擲 (one throw), symbolizing total commitment or existential risk.

## Extended Meaning
To stake everything on one move; to risk it all on a single decisive action, whether in battle, politics, business, or a major life decision.

## Source and Origin
The phrase comes from the Tang-dynasty poet **韓愈** (Han Yu)'s poem **《過鴻溝》** ("Passing Honggou"), whose text I checked on Wikisource. The Honggou (鴻溝, "Hong Canal") was the boundary line where Liu Bang (the future Han emperor) and Xiang Yu (King of Chu) agreed to divide the empire and end their civil war around 203 BC.

> "龍疲虎困割川原，億萬蒼生性命存。誰勸君王回馬首？真成一擲賭乾坤。"
> ("Dragon exhausted, tiger spent, they divided the land by the river; a million common lives were thereby spared. Who was it urged the king to turn his horse around? — truly, heaven and earth were wagered on a single throw.")

The poem reflects on how Liu Bang, having agreed to the truce, was persuaded to break it and attack the retreating Xiang Yu, an irreversible all-or-nothing gamble that won him the empire. (The poem itself does not name who urged him; the persuaders are traditionally given as Zhang Liang and Chen Ping, a historical background I did not check in this pass.) The four-character idiom 乾坤一擲 appears to be drawn from the closing line 一擲賭乾坤 ("wagering heaven and earth on a single throw") and rearranged into its standard order. I did not trace when the four-character form first appears as a fixed phrase, nor did I check the circumstances in which Han Yu composed the poem.

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese usage the idiom suggests courage, decisiveness, and fate-defying audacity, though carrying a hint of recklessness alongside its heroism. It is often mentioned alongside related idioms of no-turning-back commitment, such as 破釜沈舟 ("break the cauldrons and sink the boats") and 背水一戰 ("fight with one's back to the river"). In Japanese, 乾坤一擲 (kenkon itteki) means "a do-or-die gamble," and Korean 건곤일척 and Vietnamese kiền khôn nhất trịch keep the same dramatic sense of a heaven-and-earth wager. I did not check the earlier claims about Meiji and wartime military rhetoric or about how common the idiom is in Korean political and sports commentary, so they are not repeated.

## Example Sentences
1. 這一場戰役，是我軍的乾坤一擲。("This battle is our army's do-or-die gamble.")
2. 「誰勸君王回馬首？真成一擲賭乾坤。」(韓愈《過鴻溝》)
3. 彼は乾坤一擲の賭けに出た。("He staked everything on a single throw.")
4. 이번 경기는 건곤일척의 승부다。("This match is a decisive, all-or-nothing contest.")

## Sentiment
Heroic daring mixed with existential fatalism — echoes classical valor and total commitment, whether admired as courage or viewed with a hint of caution toward recklessness.
