## Literal Meaning
- [因](../characters/因.md) — cause
- [果](../characters/果.md) — effect, fruit
- [報 (char)](../characters/報%20(char).md) — to repay, report
- [応 (char)](../characters/応%20(char).md) — to respond

Literally: "Cause and effect, repaid in response." [[因果]] ("cause and effect") pairs with [[報応]] ("retribution, response") to state the full cycle of karmic action and consequence.

## Extended Meaning
One's actions inevitably bring about corresponding results — good deeds are rewarded, bad deeds are punished, whether in this life or beyond it. A formal Buddhist doctrinal concept describing moral causation, now used broadly across East Asia (often close in force to the English "what goes around comes around") without necessarily invoking Buddhism explicitly.

## Source and Origin
Both halves of the phrase are attested independently in early Chinese Buddhist translation literature: [[因果]] renders the doctrine of cause and effect (Sanskrit *hetu-phala*) central to karma, and 報應/報応 (*vipāka*, karmic recompense) names the resulting consequence of action. The combined four-character form appears explicitly in **《大唐大慈恩寺三藏法師傳》卷七** (Biography of the Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Compassion Monastery, compiled by Huili 慧立 and Yancong 彦悰, recounting Xuanzang's life), describing Emperor Taizong's questioning of Xuanzang at Cuiwei Palace:
> 問因果報應，及西域先聖遺芳故跡，皆引經酬對。
> (He asked about karmic cause and effect, and about the traces left by the sages of the Western Regions; [Xuanzang] answered each by citing the sutras.)

The exact four-character compound was already fixed and in circulation by the mid-7th century, though the underlying doctrinal concept is far older, foundational to Buddhist karmic theory from its earliest Chinese translations onward.

## Standard Form
**因果報應** (traditional)
- 因果报应 — simplified form
- 因果応報 / 因果応报 — the reversed 応報 ordering, standard in Japanese and Korean (see Pronunciations below); this vault's title keeps the Mandarin-conventional 報応 ordering

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** yīn guǒ bào yìng
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** jan1 gwo2 bou3 jing3
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** いんがおうほう
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 인과응보 (in-gwa-eung-bo)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** nhân quả báo ứng

Japanese and Korean both read this compound in the reversed 応報/응보 order rather than literal 報応/보응 — matching the real, standard words 因果応報 (*inga ōhō*) and 인과응보 (*in-gwa-eung-bo*) — while Mandarin, Cantonese, and Vietnamese keep the literal 報応 character order. 応 here takes its "respond, requite" sense (Mandarin yìng, not yīng "ought"), the same branch already established at the word level in [[報応]].

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese, 因果報應 often carries a strong sense of moral retribution, close to "what goes around comes around," and remains current in both religious and everyday speech. In Japanese, 因果応報 is widely used in both religious and secular contexts, sometimes with a fatalistic tone. In Korean, 인과응보 similarly emphasizes that good and evil deeds bring corresponding consequences and is common in both Buddhist teaching and everyday moral commentary. Although rooted in Buddhism, the concept has been widely absorbed into general East Asian moral thinking, including Confucian-influenced ethics and popular belief, and needs no explicit religious framing to be understood.

## Example Sentences
1. 他相信因果報應，認為善有善報，惡有惡報。
2. He believes in karmic retribution — _yīn guǒ bào yìng_ — that good is rewarded and evil punished.
3. 因果応報という考え方は広く知られている。
4. 그는 인과응보를 믿는다.

## Sentiment
Solemn and moralizing rather than neutral; invoked as a warning or as vindication, asserting that consequences track the moral weight of one's actions.
