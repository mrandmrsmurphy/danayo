## Literal Meaning
- [対](characters/対.md) — toward, facing
- [牛 (char)](characters/牛%20(char).md) — cow, ox
- [弾](characters/弾.md) — to pluck, play (a stringed instrument)
- [琴 (char)](characters/琴%20(char).md) — qin (a stringed instrument)

Literally: "To play the qin toward a cow."

## Extended Meaning
對牛彈琴 describes speaking or performing for someone who cannot appreciate it — wasting subtlety or refinement on someone unable or unwilling to understand it. It can be humorous or disdainful in tone: when one's refined art or reasoning meets uncomprehending dullness. English equivalents include "casting pearls before swine," "preaching to the deaf," and "singing to a wall."

## Source and Origin
The idiom is first attested around the 2nd–3rd century CE in **《牟子理惑論》** (Mouzi's Treatise Resolving Doubts), an early Chinese Buddhist apologetic text:

> 「公明儀為牛彈清角之操，伏食如故，非牛不聞，不合其耳矣。轉為蚊虻之聲，孤犢之鳴，即掉尾奮耳。」
> "Gongming Yi played the refined *qingjiao* melody for a cow; it kept grazing as before — not because the cow could not hear, but because the sound did not suit its ear. When he switched to the sound of mosquitoes and gnats, or the cry of a lost calf, the cow immediately swished its tail and pricked up its ears."

Mouzi used this anecdote to argue for adapting one's mode of explanation to the audience — in his case, using familiar Confucian classics rather than Buddhist scripture alone when addressing skeptics unfamiliar with Buddhism. Later moralists and poets condensed the story into the fixed four-character idiom 對牛彈琴, and Buddhist writers more broadly adopted the image, comparing it to "explaining the Dharma to those without ears to hear" — a striking conceptual parallel to **Matthew 7:6**, "do not cast your pearls before swine," both expressing the futility of sharing subtle or sacred truths with the unreceptive.

## Standard Form
**對牛彈琴** (traditional) is the historically standard form.
- 对牛弹琴 — simplified Chinese form

## Variants
- 對牛鼓瑟 — "to play the harp to a cow"
- 對牛說理 — "to reason with a cow"
- 對牛吹笛 — "to play the flute to a cow"
- Korean also has a native vernacular equivalent, 소에게 거문고 타다 ("to play a zither to a cow")
- Vietnamese also has a native proverb with the same sense, đàn gảy tai trâu ("to pluck the lute into a buffalo's ear")

## Cultural Notes
Chinese usage describes futile communication, from humorous exasperation to outright disdain — 跟他談哲學，簡直是對牛彈琴 ("talking philosophy with him is like playing the qin to a cow"). Japanese 対牛弾琴 (taigyū dankin) is a recognized yojijukugo with identical meaning. Korean 대우탄금 (daeu-tangeum) and its vernacular equivalent 소에게 거문고 타다 are both used proverbially for futility in communication. Vietnamese đối ngưu đàn cầm carries the same sense in scholarly or poetic contexts, alongside the native proverb đàn gảy tai trâu.

## Example Sentences
1. 「公明儀為牛彈清角之操，伏食如故，非牛不聞，不合其耳矣。」(《牟子理惑論》)
2. 跟無知的人講藝術，無異於對牛彈琴。("Talking art with the ignorant is like playing the zither to a cow.")
3. 그에게 설명하는 건 소에게 거문고 타는 것과 같다. ("Explaining it to him is like playing a zither to a cow.")
4. Nói đạo lý với kẻ ngu si chẳng khác gì đối ngưu đàn cầm. ("To speak wisdom to a fool is like playing the lute to a cow.")

## Sentiment
Wry, sometimes disdainful, sometimes merely resigned. Expresses the melancholy of unappreciated eloquence — wisdom or beauty offered to someone unable to receive it.
