## Literal Meaning
- [対](characters/対.md) — toward, facing
- [牛 (char)](characters/牛%20(char).md) — cow, ox
- [弾](characters/弾.md) — to pluck, play (a stringed instrument)
- [琴 (char)](characters/琴%20(char).md) — qin (a stringed instrument)

Literally: "To play the qin toward a cow."

## Extended Meaning
對牛彈琴 describes speaking or performing for someone who cannot appreciate it — wasting subtlety or refinement on someone unable or unwilling to understand it. It can be humorous or disdainful in tone: when one's refined art or reasoning meets uncomprehending dullness. English equivalents include "casting pearls before swine," "preaching to the deaf," and "singing to a wall."

## Source and Origin
The story is in **《牟子理惑論》** (Mouzi's Treatise Resolving Doubts), an early Chinese Buddhist apologetic text traditionally dated to the 2nd–3rd century CE (I did not check the date, which scholars discuss). I checked the passage against the Wikisource text:

> 「公明儀為牛彈清角之操，伏食如故，非牛不聞，不合其耳矣。轉為蚊虻之聲，孤犢之鳴，即掉尾奮耳，蹀躞而聽，是以詩書理子耳。」
> "Gongming Yi played the refined *qingjiao* melody for a cow; it kept grazing as before — not because the cow could not hear, but because the sound did not suit its ear. When he switched to the sound of mosquitoes and gnats, or the cry of a lost calf, the cow immediately swished its tail and pricked up its ears."

Mouzi used this anecdote to argue for adapting one's mode of explanation to the audience — in his case, using familiar Confucian classics rather than Buddhist scripture alone when addressing skeptics unfamiliar with Buddhism. The fixed four-character form 對牛彈琴 does not occur in this passage, which says 為牛彈清角之操; I did not establish when the compressed form first appears. The image was later adopted by Buddhist writers more broadly (a claim I did not check), comparing it to "explaining the Dharma to those without ears to hear" — a striking conceptual parallel to **Matthew 7:6**, "do not cast your pearls before swine," both expressing the futility of sharing subtle or sacred truths with the unreceptive.

## Standard Form
**對牛彈琴** (traditional) is the historically standard form.
- 对牛弹琴 — simplified Chinese form

## Variants
- Vietnamese also has a native proverb with the same sense, đàn gảy tai trâu ("to pluck the lute into a buffalo's ear"; I did not check it against a source)

Four variants listed here earlier (對牛鼓瑟, 對牛說理, 對牛吹笛 and a Korean vernacular 소에게 거문고 타다) could not be found in any source and have been removed.

## Cultural Notes
Chinese usage describes futile communication, from humorous exasperation to outright disdain — 跟他談哲學，簡直是對牛彈琴 ("talking philosophy with him is like playing the qin to a cow"). Japanese 対牛弾琴 (taigyū dankin) is a recognized yojijukugo with identical meaning. Korean 대우탄금 (daeu-tangeum) is used for the same futility in communication. Vietnamese đối ngưu đàn cầm carries the same sense. I did not check how common the idiom is in each language.

## Example Sentences
1. 「公明儀為牛彈清角之操，伏食如故，非牛不聞，不合其耳矣。」(《牟子理惑論》)
2. 跟無知的人講藝術，無異於對牛彈琴。("Talking art with the ignorant is like playing the zither to a cow.")
3. 그에게 설명하는 건 소에게 거문고 타는 것과 같다. ("Explaining it to him is like playing a zither to a cow.")
4. Nói đạo lý với kẻ ngu si chẳng khác gì đối ngưu đàn cầm. ("To speak wisdom to a fool is like playing the lute to a cow.")

## Sentiment
Wry, sometimes disdainful, sometimes merely resigned. Expresses the melancholy of unappreciated eloquence — wisdom or beauty offered to someone unable to receive it.
