## Literal Meaning
- [骨 (char)](characters/骨%20(char).md) — bone
- [肉 (char)](characters/肉%20(char).md) — flesh
- [相](characters/相.md) — mutual, each other
- [連 (char)](characters/連%20(char).md) — connected, joined

Literally: "Bone and flesh are mutually connected."

## Extended Meaning
骨肉相連 expresses the natural, unbreakable bond of kinship — the idea that blood relatives are joined as intimately and inseparably as bone is joined to flesh in a single body. It is used affectionately and solemnly, never ironically: to describe the tie between parents and children, siblings, or extended family, and especially the pain or wrongness of that tie being severed by estrangement, war, or death. It carries no negative valence on its own — it is invoked as a positive, almost sacred description of family loyalty, and as a rebuke when that loyalty is betrayed.

## Source and Origin
**Genesis 2:23** (Old Testament), Adam's exclamation on seeing Eve, newly made from his rib:

> "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man." (Gen 2:23, ESV)

骨肉相連 is the fixed four-character crystallization of this verse used in East Asian Bible translation and commentary, rendering "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" as a single compact idiom rather than a literal clause-for-clause translation.

The crystallization was made easier by a pre-existing native Chinese tradition of pairing 骨 ("bone") and 肉 ("flesh") to mean "blood kin," attested well before any Biblical contact — e.g. 《左傳》: 「骨肉之親，不可棄也」 ("the ties of bone and flesh cannot be cast away"), and 《禮記·祭義》's description of family bond as 「合體同氣」 ("one body, one breath"). By the Han dynasty the 骨肉 pairing was already standard shorthand for kinship. The Biblical translators did not invent the underlying metaphor; they fit Genesis 2:23's imagery into an idiom mold that Chinese readers already possessed, which is why 骨肉相連 reads as naturally "Chinese" as it does "Biblical."

## Standard Form
**骨肉相連** (traditional 連) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 骨肉相连 — simplified Chinese form (连)

## Cultural Notes
Chinese usage draws on the long-standing native 骨肉 = "kin" metaphor (see Source and Origin above), so the idiom carries a double resonance for Chinese Christian readers: it is simultaneously a scriptural citation and an entirely native turn of phrase, which is likely why mission-era translations and sermons reached for it so readily.

Confucian ethics reinforces the same image from the secular side: 骨肉相連 aligns with the stress on filial piety (孝) and the sacredness of family unity, so the idiom functions comfortably in both religious and purely Confucian-inflected speech.

Japanese usage is rare as a fixed set phrase — the more common native expression for the same idea is 骨肉の情 ("the feeling of bone and flesh," i.e. ties of blood) — so こつにくそうれん is mostly encountered as a direct scripture citation rather than everyday idiom.

Korean and Vietnamese usage tracks the Chinese pattern closely: both languages recognize 골육상련/cốt nhục tương liên as expressing the same natural, unbreakable bond of family affection, used in both religious and secular registers.

## Example Sentences
1. 「這是我骨中的骨，肉中的肉。」(Gen 2:23, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 兄弟雖遠，骨肉相連。("Though brothers are far apart, they remain bone of bone and flesh of flesh.")
3. 비록 멀리 떨어져 있어도 골육상련이다. ("Even though they live far apart, they are bound as bone and flesh.")
4. No matter how the family had scattered across the world, they remained 骨肉相連 — bone of one bone, flesh of one flesh.

## Sentiment
Warm, solemn, affirming. Expresses affection, loyalty, and the natural inseparability of family — and, when invoked in contexts of estrangement or loss, the pain of that bond being strained or broken.
