## Literal Meaning
- [骨 (char)](characters/骨%20(char).md) — bone
- [肉 (char)](characters/肉%20(char).md) — flesh
- [相](characters/相.md) — mutual, each other
- [連 (char)](characters/連%20(char).md) — connected, joined

Literally: "Bone and flesh are mutually connected."

## Extended Meaning
骨肉相連 expresses the natural, unbreakable bond of kinship — the idea that blood relatives are joined as intimately and inseparably as bone is joined to flesh in a single body. It is used affectionately and solemnly, never ironically: to describe the tie between parents and children, siblings, or extended family, and especially the pain or wrongness of that tie being severed by estrangement, war, or death. It carries no negative valence on its own — it is invoked as a positive, almost sacred description of family loyalty, and as a rebuke when that loyalty is betrayed.

## Source and Origin
**Genesis 2:23** (Old Testament), Adam's exclamation on seeing Eve, newly made from his rib:

> "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man." (Gen 2:23, ESV)

The Chinese Union Version (和合本) renders the verse 「這是我骨中的骨，肉中的肉，可以稱他為女人，因為他是從男人身上取出來的。」 (checked on cnbible.com; the 現代標點和合本 and 聖經新譯本 give the same 「骨中的骨，肉中的肉」). The Chinese Bible's own phrase is therefore 骨中的骨，肉中的肉.

**The four-character form is older than the Dan'a'yo Biblical use.** 骨肉相連 is an existing Chinese idiom meaning "joined like bone and flesh," that is, closely related or inseparable. Checked on Wikisource, it occurs in the **《北齊書》** (*Book of Northern Qi*), in the biography of 楊愔, where the Prince of Changshan addresses the emperor:

> "常山王以磚叩頭，進而言曰：『臣與陛下骨肉相連。楊遵彥等欲擅朝權，威福自己，王公以還，皆重足屏氣。』"
> (The Prince of Changshan struck his head on the bricks and said: "I and Your Majesty are of one flesh and bone. Yang Zunyan and the others wish to seize the court's authority...")

Dictionary sources also cite 《管子·輕重丁》 for the related 骨肉相親 ("bone and flesh draw close"), which I did not check against the text. So 骨肉相連 was not coined from Genesis 2:23 and is not a Bible-translation crystallization; the earlier claim on this page that it was has been removed. What Dan'a'yo does is use this existing idiom, with its sense of kinship, for the Genesis meaning "bone of my bone and flesh of my flesh," which is why `origin` stays `Bible` for grouping purposes: that records the Dan'a'yo sense, not the earliest source of the four characters.

The earlier version also quoted 《左傳》 (「骨肉之親，不可棄也」) and 《禮記·祭義》 (「合體同氣」). I could not verify either quotation, so both are removed, along with the claim that the 骨肉 pairing was "standard shorthand by the Han dynasty."

## Standard Form
**骨肉相連** (traditional 連) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 骨肉相连 — simplified Chinese form (连)

## Cultural Notes
Because 骨肉 already means "kin" in Chinese, the idiom reads at once as a scriptural echo and as an entirely native turn of phrase, and it fits Confucian stress on family unity and filial piety (孝). The comparison with Japanese 骨肉の情 ("the feeling of bone and flesh") and the claims about Korean and Vietnamese usage in the earlier version were not checked, so no claim is made here about how common the idiom is in any particular country.

## Example Sentences
1. 「這是我骨中的骨，肉中的肉。」(Gen 2:23, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 兄弟雖遠，骨肉相連。("Though brothers are far apart, they remain bone of bone and flesh of flesh.")
3. 비록 멀리 떨어져 있어도 골육상련이다. ("Even though they live far apart, they are bound as bone and flesh.")
4. No matter how the family had scattered across the world, they remained 骨肉相連 — bone of one bone, flesh of one flesh.

## Sentiment
Warm, solemn, affirming. Expresses affection, loyalty, and the natural inseparability of family — and, when invoked in contexts of estrangement or loss, the pain of that bond being strained or broken.
