## Literal Meaning
- [哀](../characters/哀.md) — mournful, grieving, lamenting
- [鴻](../characters/鴻.md) — wild goose; swan goose
- [遍 (char)](../characters/遍%20(char).md) — everywhere, throughout, filling
- [野](../characters/野.md) — wilderness, open country, the fields

Literally: "Mournful geese throughout the wilderness" — the open land is filled with the cries of grieving, wandering birds.

## Extended Meaning
Wiktionary glosses the Chinese idiom "a land of wailing and despair; land swarming with disaster victims; disaster victims moaning everywhere" (literally "plaintive whine of geese"). Its Japanese entry says the compound 哀鴻 + 遍野 ("wailing wild geese" and "all across the fields") is metaphorically extended to refugees and defeated soldiers wandering the lands in misery.

## Source and Origin
**《詩經·小雅·鴻雁》** (*Classic of Poetry*, Minor Odes, "Wild Geese"):

> 鴻雁于飛，哀鳴嗷嗷。
> 維此哲人，謂我劬勞。
> 維彼愚人，謂我宣驕。
>
> "The wild geese fly away, crying out in sorrow.
> The wise men say I am exhausted with toil;
> The foolish men say I am being arrogantly proud."

The traditional Mao preface reads the poem as a praise-song for King Xuan of Zhou (《毛詩》序: 「鴻雁，美宣王也。萬民離散，不安其居，而能勞來還定安集之，至於矜寡，無不得其所焉」, "the people were scattered and had lost their homes, and he comforted and resettled them, down to the widowed and the bereft"). Later commentators read the speaker as an official who sees the plight of the refugees, so that the crying geese stand for the displaced people themselves. In either reading the wild geese, which migrate across vast distances in formation and call out in voices that carry over open country, are the image of people driven from their homes, and their cry, 嗷嗷 (*áo'áo*, sustained wailing), becomes the voice of that suffering.

The fixed four-character form 哀鴻遍野 does not appear in the 詩經. It condenses 哀鳴 ("mournful cries") from the poem into 哀鴻 ("mournful geese") and adds 遍野 ("throughout the wilderness") to extend the scene to a whole landscape of suffering. The four-character form itself appears to be modern: in the wikisource corpus it is found from the late Qing, in the Revive China Society manifestos of the 1890s (「饑饉交集，哀鴻遍野，民不聊生」), and then widely in the Republican period. I have not found an earlier attestation, so the poem is its source of imagery, not a text that contains the phrase.

## Cultural Notes
English Wiktionary has a Chinese entry (simplified 哀鸿遍野) and a Japanese entry (あいこうへんや), the latter noted as derived from the Chinese. Wiktionary quotes Chen Bijun's poem 《高陽台·中秋卻寄諸兒》 (1946–1949): 「姮娥不恨聽風雨，恨哀鴻遍野痍傷」.
