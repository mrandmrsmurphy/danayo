## Literal Meaning
- [鶏](../characters/鶏%20(char).md) — chicken
- [鳴](../characters/鳴%20(char).md) — cry, crow
- [狗](../characters/狗.md) — dog
- [盗](../characters/盗.md) — steal

Literally: "The crowing of a rooster and the stealing of a dog."

## Extended Meaning
People skilled only in petty tricks; tricksters or those who rely on low cunning to achieve goals. Usually negative, referring to deceitful or insignificant men — but occasionally used admiringly, for resourceful trickery that saves the day despite its low means.

## Source and Origin
The story is in the 《史記·孟嘗君列傳》 (Records of the Grand Historian, biography of Lord Mengchang), but the four-character phrase 雞鳴狗盜 itself is not in that chapter. I read the chapter on wikisource: it has 狗盜 and 鷄鳴 only as separate words. The four characters together are first found, as far as I could verify, in the Northern Song statesman 王安石's essay 《讀孟嘗君傳》.

**The story (《史記》).** Lord Mengchang of Qi went to Qin, where King Zhao imprisoned him and planned to kill him. Mengchang sent to the king's favorite concubine to ask for help, and she asked for his white fox-fur coat:

> 此時孟嘗君有一狐白裘，直千金，天下無雙，入秦獻之昭王，更無他裘。孟嘗君患之，遍問客，莫能對。最下坐有能為狗盜者，曰：「臣能得狐白裘。」乃夜為狗，以入秦宮臧中，取所獻狐白裘至，以獻秦王幸姬。
> "At that time Mengchang had one white fox-fur coat, worth a thousand in gold and without equal in the world, and he had already presented it to King Zhao, so he had no other. Mengchang was troubled and asked all his retainers, but none could answer. A man seated lowest, who could act the dog-thief, said, 'I can get the fox-fur coat.' So that night he went in as a dog, entered the storehouse of the Qin palace, took the coat he had presented, and gave it to the king's favorite."

She spoke for him and the king released him. Mengchang fled at once under a changed name, and reached the Hangu Pass at midnight. The Qin king soon regretted the release and sent men after him, but by the pass's rule gates opened only at cockcrow:

> 關法鷄鳴而出客，孟嘗君恐追至，客之居下坐者有能為鷄鳴，而鷄齊鳴，遂發傳出。
> "By the rule of the pass, travelers were let out at the rooster's crow. Mengchang feared pursuit, and one of the retainers seated lowest could imitate a rooster, and all the roosters crowed together, so the passes were opened and they got out."

The text adds that Mengchang had first listed these two among his guests, and the other retainers were ashamed of them, but in the Qin crisis it was these two who saved him, after which all the guests were convinced (「自是之後，客皆服」).

**The phrase (王安石).** 《讀孟嘗君傳》 turns the story against the praise of Mengchang: 「嗟乎！孟嘗君特雞鳴狗盜之雄耳，豈足以言得士？」, "Alas, Mengchang was merely the chief of the cock-crowers and dog-thieves; how can he be said to have won true talent?" Wang Anshi argues that a lord who could command Qi's strength should have gathered one real scholar, and that such low retainers at his gate are why real scholars did not come. So the idiom begins as a contemptuous judgment, and the page's "occasionally used admiringly" reading goes back to the 《史記》 story itself.

## Standard Form
**鷄鳴狗盜** / **雞鳴狗盜** (traditional, both forms of "chicken" attested) — **鸡鸣狗盗** (simplified).

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese, usually negative — deceitful or insignificant men — though sometimes used admiringly for resourceful trickery that saves the day. In Japanese, 鶏鳴狗盗 (keimei kutō) often appears in essays as a moral warning against being "petty in ability but proud in manner." In Korean, 계명구도 is a well-known 사자성어 (four-character idiom) for "petty skill," sometimes used self-deprecatingly. In Vietnamese, *kê minh cẩu đạo* similarly refers to crafty but small-minded people — "tricksters, not gentlemen."

## Example Sentences
1. 這些人不過是鷄鳴狗盜之徒，難成大事。
   "These people are mere tricksters — the sort who crow like roosters and steal like dogs; they cannot accomplish great things."
2. 彼は鶏鳴狗盗の徒にすぎない。
   "He's nothing more than a petty trickster."
3. 그는 계명구도의 재주밖에 없다.
   "He has only the petty talents of a rooster-crower and dog-thief."

## Sentiment
Petty cunning and lowly trickery — contemptible in moral worth, yet sometimes surprisingly useful. Originally mocking the base retainers of a nobleman, the phrase has evolved into a proverb about small talents, cunning minds, and unexpected utility.
