## Literal Meaning
- [種](characters/種.md) — to plant
- [瓜](characters/瓜.md) — melon
- [得](characters/得.md) — to get, obtain
- [瓜](characters/瓜.md) — melon

Literally: "Plant melons, get melons."

## Extended Meaning
種瓜得瓜 states that one reaps what one sows: outcomes follow reliably from their causes, and actions produce corresponding results. Unlike moral aphorisms that invoke Heaven, fate, or karmic retribution, 種瓜得瓜 grounds its logic in ordinary agricultural experience — observational rather than theological. It carries no explicit judgment of praise or blame; its force lies entirely in inevitability, with actions containing their outcomes implicitly and time simply revealing what was already set in motion. Used throughout the region to underscore responsibility and predictability, in ethical discussion, education, parenting, and everyday explanation of success or failure — the tone can be neutral, admonitory, or consolatory depending on context, but the underlying logic is always matter-of-fact.

## Source and Origin
Commonly traced to **《南史》卷六十四〈陸澄傳〉** (History of the Southern Dynasties, Biography of Lu Cheng), where the phrase functions as a plain moral explanation of consequences rather than a poetic flourish. The idiom's durability comes from its refusal to argue the point rhetorically — it simply points to the field and the harvest, and lets the observation stand on its own.

## Variants
- 種豆得豆 — the "bean" form, used interchangeably with the "melon" form and equally common
- 種信得信 — a moralized extension applied to faith/trust rather than literal planting

## Cultural Notes
種瓜得瓜 circulates as a genuinely everyday proverb across Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese, appearing in casual moral instruction, parenting, and general explanation of cause and effect — it needs no special religious or philosophical framing to be understood.

Japanese usage is comparatively rare and more literary: rather than a naturalized four-character on-yomi compound (yojijukugo), it is typically encountered as a kundoku (Japanese classical reading) paraphrase, 瓜を植えて瓜を得る, in written or aphoristic contexts. A speculative on-yomi reading, shuka tokka, sometimes circulates but is not a standardized or widely attested form — native Japanese speakers are more likely to reach for the functionally equivalent native proverb 蒔かぬ種は生えぬ ("seeds not sown do not sprout") for the same idea.

## Example Sentences
1. 種瓜得瓜，種豆得豆，此乃天理之常。("Plant melons and reap melons, plant beans and reap beans — this is the constant principle of natural law.")
2. 你若懈怠學業，將來必自食其果——種瓜得瓜。("If you neglect your studies, you will reap the consequences yourself — as you sow, so shall you reap.")
3. 종과득과라, 노력한 만큼 결과가 따른다. ("As you plant, so you reap — results follow effort.")
4. She'd cut every corner for years, and when the business collapsed, no one was surprised: 種瓜得瓜.

## Sentiment
Neutral to admonitory, depending on context; can also be consolatory. States the inevitability of consequence following cause, without moral flourish.
