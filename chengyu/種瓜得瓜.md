## Literal Meaning
- [種](characters/種.md) — to plant
- [瓜](characters/瓜.md) — melon
- [得](characters/得.md) — to get, obtain
- [瓜](characters/瓜.md) — melon

Literally: "Plant melons, get melons."

## Extended Meaning
種瓜得瓜 states that one reaps what one sows: outcomes follow reliably from their causes, and actions produce corresponding results. Its logic is grounded in ordinary agricultural experience, though its documented use in the 喻世明言 explicitly reads the image as cause (因) and effect (果). It carries no explicit judgment of praise or blame; its force lies entirely in inevitability, with actions containing their outcomes implicitly and time simply revealing what was already set in motion. Used throughout the region to underscore responsibility and predictability, in ethical discussion, education, parenting, and everyday explanation of success or failure — the tone can be neutral, admonitory, or consolatory depending on context, but the underlying logic is always matter-of-fact.

## Source and Origin
Taiwan's Ministry of Education *Dictionary of Chinese Idioms* documents the idiom with this usage:

**《喻世明言·卷二九·月明和尚度柳翠》** (Feng Menglong, Ming):
> "假如種瓜得瓜，種荳得荳，種是因，得是果。"
> (Suppose one plants melons and gets melons, plants beans and gets beans: planting is the cause, getting is the effect.)

The passage already ties the farming image to cause and effect (因果). I relied on the Ministry's entry and did not retrieve the novel's text itself. Another dictionary (Zdic) cites 《醒世恒言·施潤澤灘闕遇友》, also by Feng Menglong, with "種瓜得瓜，種李得李，乃自然之理" ("plant melons and get melons, plant plums and get plums — it is the natural order"), which I also did not check against the text.

Several dictionary and web sources trace the saying further back to the Buddhist 《涅槃經》 (Nirvana Sutra), in the form 種瓜得瓜，種李得李. I could not locate the passage or its fascicle, so I do not state that as fact: it is a claim to be checked, not a verified source.

**Corrected.** The earlier version of this page, and its `origin`, named 《南史》卷六十四〈陸澄傳〉. I checked 《南史》 volume 48, which holds 陸澄's biography: it does not contain 種瓜得瓜 or 種瓜, and the biography is not in volume 64, so that citation has been removed. The Ministry's usage is documented evidence, not necessarily the earliest occurrence; I did not establish the earliest.

## Variants
- 種豆得豆 — the "bean" form, attested together with the melon form (for example 種瓜得瓜，種豆得豆, and 種荳得荳 in the 喻世明言 passage above)
- 種瓜得瓜，種李得李 — the "plum" form seen in the 《醒世恒言》 citation above

A variant 種信得信 listed here earlier could not be found in any source and has been removed.

## Cultural Notes
種瓜得瓜 is an agricultural proverb of cause and effect that needs no special framing to be understood, and it fits the Buddhist idea of 因果 as well as plain farming logic. I did not check how common it is in each country.

In Japanese it is typically met as the kundoku paraphrase 瓜を植えて瓜を得る rather than as a naturalized on-yomi compound; a speaker looking for the same idea is more likely to reach for the native proverb 蒔かぬ種は生えぬ ("seeds not sown do not sprout").

## Example Sentences
1. 種瓜得瓜，種豆得豆，此乃天理之常。("Plant melons and reap melons, plant beans and reap beans — this is the constant principle of natural law.")
2. 你若懈怠學業，將來必自食其果——種瓜得瓜。("If you neglect your studies, you will reap the consequences yourself — as you sow, so shall you reap.")
3. 종과득과라, 노력한 만큼 결과가 따른다. ("As you plant, so you reap — results follow effort.")
4. She'd cut every corner for years, and when the business collapsed, no one was surprised: 種瓜得瓜.

## Sentiment
Neutral to admonitory, depending on context; can also be consolatory. States the inevitability of consequence following cause, without moral flourish.
