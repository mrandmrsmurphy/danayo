## Literal Meaning
- [暗](characters/暗%20(char).md) — secretly
- [送](characters/送%20(char).md) — to send
- [秋波](words/秋波.md) — autumn ripples (figuratively, a woman's clear, glancing eyes)

Literally: "to secretly send autumn ripples" — to covertly convey something through a glance of the eyes.

## Extended Meaning

To flirt, or more broadly to convey real feelings or intentions through a knowing look rather than words — eye contact, a glance, a subtle expression that means more than its surface appearance. Classically framed as a woman's glance toward a man, but living usage is not restricted by gender: either party in an exchange of "knowing looks" can be described as 暗送秋波. A secondary, harsher extended sense also exists in Mandarin — covertly currying favor or colluding with someone (献媚取寵，暗中勾結) — but the romantic/flirtatious sense is the dominant one across the CJKV sphere and the one this vault's [[Love]] domain page cites it for.

## Source and Origin

### Poetic precedent (10th century) — the image, not yet the fixed idiom
Southern Tang ruler-poet Li Yu (李煜) supplies the earliest close juxtaposition of 暗 and 秋波, in a love letter in verse to his sister-in-law, the "Small Zhou Consort" (小周后):

**李煜《菩薩蠻》**:
> 眼色暗相鉤，秋波橫欲流。
> (Our eyes secretly hooked together; her autumn-ripple gaze seemed almost to overflow.)

The two elements appear here in the same poem but in separate lines, not yet fused into a fixed four-character phrase — a lexical precedent, not the idiom's crystallization.

### Fixed four-character form (Ming period)
The earliest verbatim attestation of the fixed idiom is a folk song in Ming-dynasty vernacular-song collector Feng Menglong's (馮夢龍) **《掛枝兒·私部·私窺》**:

> 眉兒來，眼兒去，暗送秋波。
> (Eyebrows coming, eyes going — secretly sending autumn ripples.)

### Popularizing narrative (Ming period)
The idiom's most famous illustration comes from Luo Guanzhong's (羅貫中) **《三國演義》第八回** ("Romance of the Three Kingdoms," ch. 8), in the "Chain Stratagem" (連環計) episode: minister Wang Yun uses his adopted daughter Diaochan (貂蟬) to set the warlord Lü Bu (呂布) against his patron Dong Zhuo (董卓), in part by having Diaochan exchange meaningful glances with Lü Bu in Dong Zhuo's presence:

> 布欣喜無限，頻以目視貂蟬，貂蟬亦以秋波送情。
> (Lü Bu was overjoyed, repeatedly looking toward Diaochan, and Diaochan too conveyed her feelings through her autumn-ripple gaze.)

Note the novel's own text says 以秋波送情 rather than the fixed 暗送秋波 — the novel supplies the idiom's most famous illustrative scene, not its verbatim wording, which is why 《掛枝兒》 rather than 《三國演義》 is cited as the source in the frontmatter `origin` field.

## Cultural Notes

Live and current in Mandarin, where it can carry either the romantic sense or the harsher "covertly currying favor" sense depending on context — political commentary sometimes uses it of politicians or institutions colluding out of public view. Japanese あんそうしゅうは keeps the idiom in modern four-character-idiom (四字熟語) dictionaries essentially restricted to the romantic/flirtatious sense, a literary/formal register rather than everyday conversation. Korean 암송추파 is far less commonly used than the underlying 추파 (as in 추파를 던지다, "to cast a flirtatious glance") on its own — the bare noun-plus-verb phrase is the living colloquial form, while the fixed four-character idiom itself reads as literary/bookish.

## Example Sentences
1. 她時常對他暗送秋波，卻從未說出口。
2. She kept _àn sòng qiū bō_ across the room all evening, never saying a word.
3. 会議中，両社の代表はあんそうしゅうはを交わし、密かな合意をほのめかした。("During the meeting, the two companies' representatives traded knowing looks, hinting at a covert agreement" — the harsher "collusion" sense.)

## Sentiment
Mixed, register-dependent: playful and lightly suggestive in the romantic sense, faintly disapproving/cynical in the collusion sense. Never neutral — it always implies something is being communicated *other than* what's said aloud.
