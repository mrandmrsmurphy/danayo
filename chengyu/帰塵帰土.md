## Literal Meaning
- [帰](characters/帰.md) — return
- [塵](characters/塵.md) — dust
- [帰](characters/帰.md) — return
- [土 (char)](characters/土%20(char).md) — earth, soil

Literally: "Return to dust, return to earth."

## Extended Meaning
帰塵帰土 states the plain fact of human mortality: every body, regardless of wealth, status, or virtue in life, eventually decomposes back into the earth it came from. It functions as a memento mori — used in funeral contexts, eulogies, and reflective or philosophical writing to underscore the universality and inevitability of death, and often to argue (implicitly or explicitly) that worldly distinctions of rank and fortune are erased by it. Solemn rather than despairing; it can carry either a humbling, chastening tone or a consoling, leveling one, depending on context.

## Source and Origin
The phrase draws on two Old Testament passages. First, **Genesis 3:19**, God's words to Adam after the Fall:

> "By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return." (Gen 3:19, ESV)

And **Ecclesiastes 3:20**, extending the same image to all living things:

> "All go to one place. All are from the dust, and to dust all return." (Eccl 3:20, ESV)

The Chinese Union Version (和合本) renders Genesis 3:19 as 「你必汗流滿面才得糊口，直到你歸了土，因為你是從土而出的。你本是塵土，仍要歸於塵土。」 (checked on cnbible.com, which shows the same wording in the 現代標點和合本); the 聖經新譯本 reads 「…直到你歸回地土，因為你是從地土取出來的；你既然是塵土，就要歸回塵土。」 Both verses use 歸 with 塵 and 土, which is the material the four-character form arranges into the balanced pair 歸塵 / 歸土 ("return to dust / return to earth").

I found **no attestation of 帰塵帰土 (歸塵歸土, 归尘归土) as a fixed idiom** in Chinese, Japanese or Korean sources; a search for it found nothing. It appears to be a Dan'a'yo four-character rendering of the "dust to dust" idea, like the other Biblical chengyu on the grouping page. It is a counterpart of, not the source of, the English burial formula "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust" of the Book of Common Prayer, which itself draws on Genesis 3:19. The earlier claim on this page that it was the "East Asian Bible translation's crystallization" and "the direct source" of the English phrase was unsupported (and chronologically backwards) and has been removed.

## Standard Form
**帰塵帰土** (Japanese shinjitai 帰) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 歸塵歸土 — traditional Chinese form (歸)
- 归尘归土 — simplified Chinese form (归尘)

## Variants
Attested Chinese renderings of Genesis 3:19 are full sentences rather than a fixed idiom:
- 你本是塵土，仍要歸於塵土 — Chinese Union Version
- 你既然是塵土，就要歸回塵土 — 聖經新譯本

Three variants listed here earlier (由塵帰塵由土帰土, 塵帰塵土帰土, 本塵終塵) could not be found in any source and have been removed.

## Cultural Notes
The thought is the same one the English burial formula expresses at the graveside: that the body returns to the earth it was taken from. It also fits native Chinese reflection on impermanence, so it is intelligible beyond Christian circles. Because 帰塵帰土 is not attested as a fixed idiom, no claim is made here about how common it is in any particular country or in funeral liturgy.

## Example Sentences
1. 「你本是塵土，仍要歸於塵土。」(Gen 3:19, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 人生有限，終必帰塵帰土。("Human life is limited; ultimately we must return to dust, return to earth.")
3. 無論貧富貴賤，皆帰塵帰土，平等於死。("Regardless of poverty, wealth, nobility or baseness, all return to dust, return to earth — equal in death.")
4. At every funeral the priest repeated the old formula, 帰塵帰土, and for a moment rich and poor mourners alike fell silent.

## Sentiment
Solemn, mortality-focused, humbling. Used in contexts of death, funerals, or to emphasize human frailty and the equality of all before death.
