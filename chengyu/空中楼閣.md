## Literal Meaning
- [空](../characters/空%20(char).md) — empty
- [中](../characters/中.md) — midst, within
- [楼](../characters/楼.md) — watchtower, storied building
- [閣](../characters/閣.md) — pavilion, chamber

Literally: "A pavilion in the air."

## Extended Meaning
Something magnificent in imagination but lacking real foundation — an unreal plan, theory, or dream. Can be used philosophically (of the illusory nature of appearances generally) or practically (of one specific impractical scheme); always at least mildly critical, never a compliment.

## Source and Origin
The page previously said the phrase "appears first in Buddhist literature" in the 《大智度論》 (juan 27), with a quoted sentence. I could not locate that sentence: the text is not on wikisource, the CBETA search was unreachable from here, and the quotation reads as a paraphrase rather than a verified line. The attribution is therefore withdrawn until it can be checked against the sutra text.

The uses I could verify are Song-dynasty and run in the opposite direction from the modern sense:

- **《二程遺書》** (sayings of the Cheng brothers, juan 7) has 「邵堯夫猶空中樓閣」, "Shao Yaofu (Shao Yong) is like a pavilion in the air." It is praise: the 《朱子語類》 records the question 「問程子謂康節空中樓閣」 and the answer 「是四通八達」, "it means open on all sides, with free passage everywhere", and the 《二程粹言》 has 「堯夫襟懷放曠，如空中樓閣，四通八達也」. Here 空中樓閣 is a lofty, free-standing structure open to the world, not an unreal one.
- The 《通俗編》 notes that the image also relates to the mirage that the 《夢溪筆談》 describes at Dengzhou (登州四面臨海，春夏時遙見空際有城市樓台之狀，士人謂之海市, "Dengzhou is surrounded by sea, and in spring and summer one can see in the sky the shapes of cities and towers; the scholars call it the 海市, the sea-market mirage"), and adds that the phrase is now used of anything groundless and fabricated (今凡言行虛構者，世率取此譬之). This is a Qing compiler's note, a secondary report, not a text I read in the 《夢溪筆談》 itself.

So the figurative sense "baseless, unreal" developed later than the Song, from the mirage image and from the literal sense of a building hanging in the air. I have not established when. In Qing fiction and prose the negative sense is already common, as in the 《蜃樓志》 preface (「絕非空中樓閣也」, "by no means a castle in the air").

## Standard Form
**空中樓閣** (traditional) / **空中楼阁** (simplified) — both current.

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese, the idiom can be philosophical (the illusory nature of the world) or practical (an impractical scheme): 這計畫如空中樓閣，不可行 ("This plan is like a pavilion in the sky — unworkable"). In Japanese, 空中楼閣 (kūchū rōkaku) is used metaphorically for abstract theories with no grounding in fact. In Korean, 공중누각 carries the same sense — "an airy castle," impractical but often elegant in conception. In Vietnamese, *không trung lâu các* keeps both the literal and metaphorical sense, "grand but unreal."

In modern usage the idiom is a warning against idealism unmoored from structure; the page's earlier readings of it in Buddhist and Confucian terms are interpretive and not tied to a verified source. The idiom is also frequently paired with 鏡花水月 ("flowers in a mirror, moon in water") as a matched set of illusory-beauty metaphors — 鏡花水月 for beauty seen but untouchable, 空中樓閣 for grandeur imagined but ungrounded — though the two are distinct idioms, not variants of each other.

## Example Sentences
1. 沒有實際基礎的理論，不過是空中樓閣。
   "A theory without real basis is nothing but a pavilion in the air."
2. 彼の理想は空中楼閣にすぎない。
   "His ideals are no more than a castle in the air."
3. 그의 계획은 공중누각이었다.
   "His plan was a tower floating in the air."
4. Giấc mơ ấy chỉ là không trung lâu các mà thôi.
   "That dream was merely a pavilion in the sky."

## Sentiment
Lofty yet rootless; dazzling yet empty — a castle built upon air. Mildly to strongly critical, aimed at ideas or plans that impress in the abstract but cannot be realized.
