[Biblical Chengyu](chengyu/Biblical%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [家](characters/家.md) — house, household
- [分 (char)](characters/分%20(char).md) — divided, split
- [不 (char)](characters/不%20(char).md) — not
- [立 (char)](characters/立%20(char).md) — stand

Literally: "A house, divided, does not stand."

## Extended Meaning
家分不立 warns that any group bound together by shared purpose — a household, a nation, an organization, a church — will collapse if it turns against itself internally. The idiom is used prescriptively rather than merely descriptively: it is invoked to argue for unity and against factionalism, applied equally to politics, family relationships, business, and religious community. Always cautionary; never used to praise division.

## Source and Origin
The saying appears in near-identical form in all three Synoptic Gospels — **Matthew 12:25**, **Mark 3:25**, and **Luke 11:17** — spoken by Jesus in response to the accusation that he cast out demons by the power of Satan:

> "Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste, and no city or house divided against itself will stand." (Matt 12:25, ESV)

家分不立 is the East Asian Bible translation's crystallization of the "house divided" clause into fixed four-character form. The logical structure — subject (家, "house") + state (分, "divided") + consequence (不立, "cannot stand") — mirrors the terse cause-and-effect phrasing native to Chinese philosophical maxims, which is likely why the crystallized form reads naturally as a chengyu despite its New Testament origin.

## Variants
- 家若分争，必站不住 — a fuller, more explicitly conditional Classical Chinese rendering of the same verse ("if a house is divided against itself, it will surely be unable to stand")

## Cultural Notes
As with other Biblical chengyu built on a pre-existing native logical pattern (cf. [[剣生剣死]]), 家分不立's warning against internal division resonates independently with Confucian concern for household and social harmony, so the idiom functions as both a scriptural citation and a natively intelligible moral maxim in Chinese-language contexts.

Japanese and Korean usage is comparatively rarer and more narrowly confined to Christian sermons, Bible study, and explicitly religious writing, since neither language has an equally entrenched secular idiom built on exactly this image.

Vietnamese usage likewise sits within the country's Catholic and Protestant minority communities, where gia phân bất lập functions as a recognizable scriptural allusion rather than an everyday proverb.

## Example Sentences
1. 「凡一國自相分爭，必成為荒場；一城一家自相分爭，必站立不住。」(Matt 12:25, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 教會家分不立，當求合一之道。("If the church is divided against itself, it must seek the way of unity.")
3. 此國家分不立，終必敗亡。("This nation divided against itself will ultimately fall.")
4. The coalition's infighting proved the old warning true: 家分不立, and within a year it had collapsed entirely.

## Sentiment
Cautionary, admonitory. Used to warn against internal conflict and to argue for the necessity of unity, whether in a family, a church, a company, or a nation.
