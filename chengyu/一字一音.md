## Literal Meaning
- [一](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [字](../characters/字%20(char).md) — character
- [一](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [音](../characters/音.md) — sound

Literally: "one character, one sound."

## Extended Meaning
The principle of strict phonological determinacy: each character corresponds to a single, fixed pronunciation. In <ruby>単亜語<rt>ㄉㄚㄋ·ㄚ·⼄</rt></ruby>, a written form unambiguously determines its spoken form, eliminating the multiplicity of readings (multiple on-yomi, kun-yomi, or literary/colloquial doublets) found in the Chinese and Japanese traditions. The idiom asserts a WYSIWYG relationship between script and sound, prioritizing learnability, predictability, and mechanical clarity over historical layering.

## Source and Origin
Coined within Dan'a'yo itself (`origin: 単亜語`) as a slogan for the language's own orthographic design philosophy — see [[文法 - 03文字法]] for the grammar rule it names. Unlike Chinese characters, which routinely carry several unrelated readings depending on context, register, or borrowed use (and unlike Japanese kanji, which layer on-yomi over kun-yomi over compound-specific readings), Dan'a'yo assigns each character exactly one 注音/羅馬字 pronunciation, with no exceptions. 一字一音 is the idiom Dan'a'yo speakers use to name and defend that design choice explicitly, treating it as a deliberate simplification rather than an accident of history.

## Cultural Notes
Because this is a Dan'a'yo-internal coinage rather than an inherited Chinese or Japanese idiom, it carries no classical literary weight and is not attested outside Dan'a'yo usage. It functions as in-group technical vocabulary: a shorthand a learner or grammarian reaches for when explaining *why* Dan'a'yo reads the way it does, especially to someone coming from Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, Korean, or Vietnamese, all of which tolerate real polyphony in their own inherited script traditions. The tone is didactic and mildly programmatic rather than poetic.

## Example Sentences
1. 単亜語の表記法は一字一音の原則に基づく。("Dan'a'yo's orthography is based on the principle of one character, one sound.")
2. When a student asked why 行 has so many Mandarin readings but its Dan'a'yo reading never changes, the teacher answered simply: 一字一音.
3. Critics of the language sometimes call this rigid, but its defenders call it 一字一音 — the whole point.

## Sentiment
Neutral to mildly positive; a technical, normative slogan rather than an emotional expression. It frames orthography as an interface rather than an archive, treating the rejection of polyphony as a design virtue rather than a cultural loss.
