## Literal Meaning
- [一 (char)](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [気 (char)](../characters/気%20(char).md) — breath, vital energy
- [化](../characters/化.md) — to transform
- [三 (char)](../characters/三%20(char).md) — three
- [清](../characters/清.md) — pure, clear

Literally: "One breath of energy transforms into the Three Pure Ones."

## Extended Meaning
In Daoist thought, the claim that a single primal breath (気) can unfold into the three highest deities, the Three Pure Ones: [元始](../words/原始.md) Heavenly Venerable, [霊宝](../words/霊宝.md) Heavenly Venerable and [道徳](../words/道徳.md) Heavenly Venerable (see [天尊](../words/天尊.md)). More broadly, it names the way a single origin divides into a trinity while remaining one, and the way a master projects himself into several forms. It is used for Daoist cosmology and doctrine, for the novel's duel, and in a figurative sense for one source producing many forms.

## Source and Origin
The four-character form is in **《封神演義》**, the Ming novel traditionally credited to 許仲琳 (the zh.Wikisource text names 陳仲琳 as author). In chapter 77, titled 老子一炁化三清 in the Wikisource text, the sage [老子](../words/老子.md) fights Tongtian Jiaozhu (通天教主) in the Immortal-Slaying Formation. He pushes his fish-tail cap and three breaths rise from the top of his head and become the Three Pure Ones:

**《封神演義》第七十七回**:
> "把魚尾冠一推，只見頂上三道氣出，化為三清。"
> (He nudged his fish-tail cap, and three streams of breath rose from his crown and became the Three Pure Ones.)

The chapter opens with a poem whose first line is 一氣三清勢更奇. In chapter 78 the narrator explains that the Three Pure Ones formed from Laozi's one breath were only primal energy, and Laozi, as the breath is about to fade, composes a poem that ends with the idiom:

**《封神演義》第七十八回**:
> "先天而老後天生，借李成形得姓名。曾拜鴻鈞修道德，方知一氣化三清。"
> (Old before Heaven, yet born after it; he borrowed the Li family to take form and a name. He once studied the Way and its virtue under Hongjun, and so came to know how one breath becomes the Three Pure Ones.)

The idea behind it is older than the novel and is Daoist, since the Three Pure Ones were already the central deities of the religion; the novel gave it its set four-character shape. Checked on zh.Wikisource, 封神演義 卷077 and 卷078, on 2026-11-09; the pre-novel Daoist background is stated from general knowledge and was not checked against a primary text.

## Standard Form
**一気化三清** — 気 is the shinjitai form Dan'a'yo uses.
- 一氣化三清 — traditional; the form of the poem in chapter 78
- 一气化三清 — simplified Chinese
- 一炁化三清 — with Daoist 炁, as in the Wikisource chapter title
- 一無化三清 — with 無 for "the formless", the Daoist rendering of the primal state

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** yī qì huà sān qīng
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** jat1 hei3 faa3 saam1 cing1
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** いっきかさんせい
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 일기화삼청 (il-gi-hwa-sam-cheong)
- **Vietnamese (Hán–Việt):** nhất khí hóa tam thanh

The Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese forms are compositional readings of the five characters.

## Cultural Notes
Chinese: a Daoist and literary phrase, best known from the novel and from Daoist temples and teaching. Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese: known mostly through the novel and Daoist studies; none uses it as everyday speech. Because the Three Pure Ones are one doctrine of "one becoming three", it is sometimes compared with trinitarian ideas elsewhere.

## Example Sentences
1. 老子一氣化三清，通天教主不能識。
2. Laozi turned a single breath into the Three Pure Ones.
3. 道教說一気化三清，三清本是一体。

## Sentiment
Neutral to reverent; learned and religious, with a flavour of Daoist cosmology and of the novel's marvels.
