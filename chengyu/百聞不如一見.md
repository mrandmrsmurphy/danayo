## Literal Meaning
- [百 (char)](../characters/百%20(char).md) — hundred
- [聞 (char)](../characters/聞%20(char).md) — to hear
- [不 (char)](../characters/不%20(char).md) — not
- [如 (char)](../characters/如%20(char).md) — to be like, equal to
- [一 (char)](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [見 (char)](../characters/見%20(char).md) — to see

Literally: "hearing a hundred times is not equal to seeing once."

## Extended Meaning
Direct experience surpasses repeated reports; firsthand observation outweighs hearsay, no matter how many times a thing has been described secondhand. Across the region it is widely used in education, travel writing, journalism, and everyday speech to encourage experiential learning, personal visitation, or skepticism toward unverified claims; the tone is neutral and pragmatic.

## Source and Origin
The saying originates in **《漢書·趙充國傳》** (Book of Han, Biography of Zhao Chongguo), recounting an exchange between the aged general Zhao Chongguo (趙充國) and Emperor Xuan of Han. Asked, in his seventies, how many troops would be needed to deal with the Qiang tribes on the frontier, the general replied:

> "百聞不如一見。兵難隃度，臣願馳至金城，圖上方略。"
> ("Hearing a hundred times is not as good as seeing once. Military affairs are hard to judge from a distance; I wish to ride to Jincheng myself and draw up a plan on the spot.")

The emperor granted his request, and the maxim circulated widely thereafter as a general statement about the superiority of firsthand observation over distant reports.

## Cultural Notes
The structure contrasts quantitative accumulation with qualitative immediacy: 百聞 represents repeated testimony, secondhand knowledge, or accumulated reports, while 一見 represents a single direct encounter, and the comparative 不如 ("is not as good as") shifts authority decisively toward direct perception. Unlike purely moral aphorisms, this expression carries genuine epistemological weight, prioritizing empiricism over rumor and personal verification over inherited narrative — much of its durability owes to this practical orientation. The phrase also reflects the administrative world of early imperial China, where officials were routinely dispatched to inspect distant regions because reports could distort, exaggerate, or conceal the truth, and seeing for oneself was the only reliable corrective.

## Example Sentences
1. 百聞不如一見，你不如親自去看看。("Hearing about it a hundred times is not as good as seeing it once — you should go see it yourself.")
2. Photos never do the canyon justice — 百聞不如一見, as the saying goes.
3. 老將軍對曰：「百聞不如一見，兵難隃度。」(《漢書·趙充國傳》)
4. 旅行帶給人的最大收穫，正是百聞不如一見的真實體驗。("The greatest reward of travel is precisely the real experience that no amount of hearing can substitute for.")

## Sentiment
Neutral to positive, pragmatic and encouraging — values direct experience and firsthand verification over secondhand report.
