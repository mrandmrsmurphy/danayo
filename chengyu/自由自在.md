## Literal Meaning
- [自](../characters/自.md) — self
- [由 (char)](../characters/由%20(char).md) — from, by way of
- [自](../characters/自.md) — self
- [在 (char)](../characters/在%20(char).md) — to exist, to be present

Literally: "By oneself, existing in oneself" — [[自由]] ("proceeding from oneself," i.e. free, unconstrained) paired with [[自在]] ("existing in oneself," i.e. at ease, unhindered).

## Extended Meaning
Unrestricted; acting freely and naturally without constraint. In Chinese the phrase most often describes an easeful, unhurried state of life — leisure and lack of external constraint. In Japanese and Korean usage it commonly shifts toward describing skillful mastery — the ability to handle a tool, technique, or situation with complete, effortless control. The range runs from spiritual or existential freedom to technical fluency, depending on context.

## Source and Origin
The idiom is first attested as a fixed four-character phrase in **《景德傳燈錄》卷二十三** (*Jingde-Era Record of the Transmission of the Lamp*, compiled 1004 CE by the Chan monk Daoyuan 釋道原), in a dialogue exchange:
> 問：「牛頭未見四祖時如何？」師曰：「自由自在。」曰：「見後如何？」師曰：「自由自在。」
> (Someone asked: "How was Niutou before he met the Fourth Patriarch?" The master said: "Free and at ease." "And after?" The master said: "Free and at ease.")

The exchange makes a Chan point: the master gives the identical answer both times, suggesting that Niutou's fundamentally free, unhindered nature was never something attained through meeting a teacher — it was always already so. The compound draws on two already-current two-character Buddhist terms, [[自由]] ("free, at liberty," lit. "proceeding from oneself") and [[自在]] ("unhindered, sovereign," Sanskrit *īśvara*/*svatantra* in translation contexts, describing a state of spiritual freedom unbound by attachment). The phrase later broadened well beyond its Chan origins — appearing in vernacular fiction such as *Journey to the West* ("只知日食三餐，夜眠一宿，無事牽縈，自由自在") — to describe effortless ease or mastery in any domain, physical, artistic, or mental.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** zì yóu zì zài
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** zi6 jau4 zi6 zoi6
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** じゆうじざい
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 자유자재 (ja-yu-ja-jae)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** tự do tự tại

All five readings are fully compositional, matching each constituent character's own standard reading; Japanese 自由 (jiyū) uses じ (JI), the secondary of 自's two on'yomi, matching the real, common Japanese word for "freedom."

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese, 自由自在 often describes a state of ease, naturalness, and lack of constraint, sometimes with a relaxed or leisurely tone — a comfortable life free of external pressure. In Japanese, the idiom frequently shifts toward complete control or mastery, as in handling a skill or situation freely and fluently — closer to "with total command" than "at leisure." In Korean, 자유자재 likewise often emphasizes skillful control, the ability to manipulate something freely at will. The phrase therefore ranges from spiritual or existential freedom to technical mastery depending on context and language.

## Example Sentences
1. 他過著自由自在的生活。
2. He lives a free and unrestrained life — _zì yóu zì zài_.
3. 彼はその技術を自由自在に操る。
4. 그는 도구를 자유자재로 다룬다.

## Sentiment
Positive and unburdened; evokes ease, freedom from constraint, and (in Japanese/Korean usage especially) effortless mastery.
