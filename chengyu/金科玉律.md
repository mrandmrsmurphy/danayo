## Literal Meaning
- [金 (char)](characters/金%20(char).md) — gold
- [科](characters/科.md) — statute, category
- [玉 (char)](characters/玉%20(char).md) — jade
- [律](characters/律.md) — law, statute

Literally: "Golden statutes and jade laws."

## Extended Meaning
金科玉律 names supreme and inviolable principles — sacred moral or legal standards that must never be broken. "Gold" and "jade" symbolize purity, incorruptibility, and eternal value; over time the phrase expanded beyond the legal sphere into moral and philosophical use, naming the highest standards by which conduct is measured. Used to praise a principle or maxim as sacred and unchangeable, though it can carry a note of rigid dogmatism when applied to a rule that is followed too unquestioningly.

## Source and Origin
The phrase dates to the Han dynasty, when 金科 ("golden statutes") and 玉律 ("jade laws") referred to the highest codes of law or morality, metaphorically written on precious materials to signify their sanctity. Its earliest textual appearance (in the variant form 金科玉條) is in **揚雄's 《劇秦美新》**:

> 「懿律嘉量，金科玉條，神卦靈兆，古文畢發，煥炳照曜，靡不宣臻。」
> "Fine statutes and excellent measures, golden codes and jade regulations, divine hexagrams and numinous omens, ancient writings brought fully to light — all shone forth radiantly, leaving nothing unrevealed."

Over the centuries the phrase generalized from its original sense of codified law under a specific dynasty into a general term for any supreme, unbreakable principle — legal, moral, or personal.

## Standard Form
**金科玉律** is stable across traditional and simplified script.

## Variants
- 金科玉條 / 金科玉条 — "golden statutes and jade regulations," an older and slightly broader form, and the form in which the phrase is first attested
- 鐵律如山 — "iron law like a mountain," similar in tone but more rigidly legal

## Cultural Notes
Chinese usage praises a principle or maxim as sacred and unchangeable — 這句話成了我們的金科玉律 ("this saying became our inviolable rule"). Japanese 金科玉律 (kinka gyokuritsu) is a set phrase meaning "an unbreakable rule," sometimes carrying a note of rigid dogmatism if overused. Korean 금과옥률 likewise refers to inviolable doctrine or law. Vietnamese kim khoa ngọc luật appears in Confucian writing to denote celestial or moral law.

The idiom also became the natural Chinese term for the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12):

> 「所以無論何事，你們願意人怎樣待你們，你們也要怎樣待人，因為這就是律法和先知的道理。」
> "Therefore, whatever you want others to do to you, do also to them."

Mission-era translators and preachers often used 金科玉律 to render or describe this verse, sometimes calling it 主所立之金科玉律 ("the Lord's golden rule"). In Christian Chinese usage today, 金科玉律 can therefore mean either the moral principle par excellence (e.g. love your neighbor as yourself) or any unchanging divine commandment — a phrase bridging ancient Chinese reverence for moral order and Biblical reverence for divine command.

## Example Sentences
1. 「懿律嘉量，金科玉條，神卦靈兆，古文畢發，煥炳照曜，靡不宣臻。」(揚雄《劇秦美新》)
2. 誠實守信，是我們公司的金科玉律。("Honesty and trustworthiness are our inviolable rules.")
3. 그 말은 우리에게 금과옥률로 여겨진다. ("Those words are regarded by us as a golden and jade law.")
4. "Yêu người như chính mình" là kim khoa ngọc luật của Đạo Cơ-đốc. ("'Love your neighbor as yourself' is the Golden Rule of Christianity.")

## Sentiment
Reverent, absolute. An inviolable moral law, as precious and pure as gold and jade — the ideal of a law written not just on tablets, but on the human heart.
