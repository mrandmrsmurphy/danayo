## Literal Meaning
- [重 (char)](characters/重%20(char).md) — to value, weight
- [文](characters/文.md) — culture, civil affairs
- [軽 (char)](characters/軽%20(char).md) — to belittle, light
- [武 (char)](characters/武%20(char).md) — military, martial

Literally: "To value the civil and belittle the martial."

## Extended Meaning
重文軽武 describes prioritizing culture, education, and moral governance over military strength and warfare — esteeming scholars more than soldiers. It carries a double edge: positively (Confucian reading), it expresses the conviction that civilization, learning, and moral leadership bring lasting peace rather than brute force; negatively (Legalist or modern reading), it names a policy failure — overvaluing culture at the expense of national defense, leaving a state vulnerable. Used in historiography to characterize dynasties, and in modern political and essay writing to critique societies that invest too heavily in soft power, education, or bureaucracy while neglecting defense.

## Source and Origin
The exact four-character phrase is not very early; it crystallizes in Tang–Song historiography, though the underlying idea is older. Early background ideas include **《史記·平準書》**'s contrast of "農戰之道" (the way of agriculture and war) and **《漢書·食貨志》**'s discussion of rulers alternating emphasis between "文治" and "武功" — but the fixed four-character phrase does not appear in Han texts.

In Tang–Song commentaries the wording begins to appear directly, as in **《新唐書·兵志》**:

> 「國家自古重文輕武，武人常被抑。」
> "Since ancient times the state has esteemed civil over military, and soldiers are often restrained."

This formula became common in Song historiography, reflecting the dynasty's own tendencies — under Song rule, civil-service scholars held real power while generals were often distrusted after earlier military coups, and success in the civil service examinations (科舉) became the primary path to honor and office. By Ming and Qing dynastic histories, 重文輕武 was firmly fixed as a standard idiom used to critique dynasties, especially the Song, for overvaluing culture and under-preparing militarily.

The broader idea draws on Confucian political philosophy, which emphasized moral order, education, and ritual (禮樂) as the foundation of a stable state — cf. **《尚書·武成》**'s account of King Wu of Zhou conquering by virtue rather than cruelty, and Confucius's own statement in **《論語·子路》** that the gentleman rules through civil virtue.

## Variants
- 崇文抑武 — "to exalt culture and suppress the military"
- 尚文輕武 — "to admire learning and disdain arms"
- 以文治國 — "to rule the state through culture/civil virtue" (related in spirit, not a strict variant)

## Cultural Notes
Historians most often invoke 重文輕武 to describe the Song dynasty specifically, frequently blaming the policy for the Song's military weakness against northern invaders — while the Ming dynasty, by contrast, sometimes celebrated the same underlying value as evidence of China's refined moral order after Mongol rule. In modern usage across the region, the phrase extends to any society or institution accused of investing too much in soft power, education, or bureaucracy while neglecting defense or practical strength.

The tension the idiom names has cross-cultural parallels: the ancient Greek debate between the philosopher and the warrior in Plato and Aristotle, the Biblical contrast between the triumph of the "word" and the sword ("Not by might, nor by power, but by My Spirit," Zechariah 4:6), and the modern English maxim "the pen is mightier than the sword."

## Example Sentences
1. 「國家自古重文輕武，武人常被抑。」(《新唐書·兵志》)
2. 宋代重文輕武，文臣掌權，武將受抑。("The Song dynasty valued the civil and belittled the military; scholars held power while generals were restrained.")
3. 조선은 유교적 이념에 따라 중문경무의 전통을 세웠다. ("Joseon established a Confucian tradition of valuing the civil and downplaying the military.")
4. Critics argued the ministry had become 重文軽武 — brilliant at policy papers, hopeless at actually defending anything.

## Sentiment
Double-edged. Admiring of civil virtue and moral leadership at its best; a cautionary label for institutional weakness and vulnerability at its worst.
