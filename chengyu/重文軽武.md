## Literal Meaning
- [重 (char)](characters/重%20(char).md) — to value, weight
- [文](characters/文.md) — culture, civil affairs
- [軽 (char)](characters/軽%20(char).md) — to belittle, light
- [武 (char)](characters/武%20(char).md) — military, martial

Literally: "To value the civil and belittle the martial."

## Extended Meaning
重文軽武 describes prioritizing culture, education, and moral governance over military strength and warfare — esteeming scholars more than soldiers. It carries a double edge: positively (Confucian reading), it expresses the conviction that civilization, learning, and moral leadership bring lasting peace rather than brute force; negatively (Legalist or modern reading), it names a policy failure — overvaluing culture at the expense of national defense, leaving a state vulnerable. Used in historical writing to characterize dynasties (above all the Song), and in modern political and essay writing to critique societies that invest too heavily in soft power, education, or bureaucracy while neglecting defense.

## Source and Origin
The earliest occurrence of the exact four characters that I could verify is in Ming vernacular fiction, **凌濛初《二刻拍案驚奇》卷十七** (〈同窗友認假作真　女秀才移花接木〉, 1632). A character advises against submitting a military officer's own defence, because the censor who impeached him is a civil official:

> 「而今重文輕武，老伯是按院題的，若武職官出名自辨，他們不容起來，反致激怒，弄壞了事。」
> (Nowadays the civil is honoured and the military slighted; your father was impeached by the censor, and if a military officer comes forward to defend himself they will not tolerate it, and it will only provoke them and spoil the matter.)

A second early use is in **吳敬梓《儒林外史》第四十六回** (〈三山門賢人餞別　五河縣勢利薰心〉), where Wu Zhengzi jokes about clerks knowing the pasture and water of the frontier better than the officials who write about it:

> 「若說是司官的學問，怪不的朝廷重文輕武；若說是書辦的考核，可見這大部的則例是移動不得的了。」
> (If it is the officials' learning, no wonder the court honours the civil and slights the military; if it is the clerks' calculation, then the Board's regulations clearly cannot be changed.)

The *idea* is much older, and Song policy is the usual historical referent: after the early-Song fear of generals, civil officials and the examination system took precedence over military men. But the four-character phrase is not found in the Song-era texts I checked. In particular, the phrase and the line 「國家自古重文輕武，武人常被抑」 that an earlier version of this page attributed to 《新唐書·兵志》 are **not in that chapter** (checked against the text of 新唐書 卷五十); that attribution was wrong and has been removed. I have not searched Song or earlier texts exhaustively, so an earlier occurrence may exist.

## Variants
- 崇文抑武 — "to exalt culture and suppress the military"
- 尚文輕武 — "to admire learning and disdain arms"
- 以文治國 — "to rule the state through culture/civil virtue" (related in spirit, not a strict variant)

## Cultural Notes
Historians most often invoke 重文輕武 to describe the Song dynasty specifically, frequently blaming the policy for the Song's military weakness against northern invaders. In modern usage across the region, the phrase extends to any society or institution accused of investing too much in soft power, education, or bureaucracy while neglecting defense or practical strength.

The tension the idiom names has cross-cultural parallels: the ancient Greek debate between the philosopher and the warrior in Plato and Aristotle, the Biblical contrast between the triumph of the "word" and the sword ("Not by might, nor by power, but by My Spirit," Zechariah 4:6), and the modern English maxim "the pen is mightier than the sword."

## Example Sentences
1. 「而今重文輕武，老伯是按院題的。」(《二刻拍案驚奇》卷十七)
2. 宋代重文輕武，文臣掌權，武將受抑。("The Song dynasty valued the civil and belittled the military; scholars held power while generals were restrained.")
3. 조선은 유교적 이념에 따라 중문경무의 전통을 세웠다. ("Joseon established a Confucian tradition of valuing the civil and downplaying the military.")
4. Critics argued the ministry had become 重文軽武 — brilliant at policy papers, hopeless at actually defending anything.

## Sentiment
Double-edged. Admiring of civil virtue and moral leadership at its best; a cautionary label for institutional weakness and vulnerability at its worst.
