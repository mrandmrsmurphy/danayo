## Literal Meaning
- [朝 (char)](../characters/朝%20(char).md) — morning
- [三 (char)](../characters/三%20(char).md) — three
- [暮](../characters/暮.md) — evening, dusk
- [四 (char)](../characters/四%20(char).md) — four

Literally: "Three in the morning, four in the evening."

## Extended Meaning
Describes changing the presentation or wording of something while its underlying substance stays exactly the same — manipulating people with superficial rearrangement rather than real change, or, by extension, simple fickleness and inconsistency (frequently changing one's mind, plans, or policy). The core sense is always that a distinction being reacted to is not a real distinction at all.

## Source and Origin
**《莊子·齊物論》** ("Discussion on Making All Things Equal," Zhuangzi, c. 4th century BCE):
> 狙公賦芧，曰：「朝三而暮四。」眾狙皆怒。曰：「然則朝四而暮三。」眾狙皆悅。名實未虧而喜怒為用，亦因是也。
> (The monkey-keeper was distributing acorns, and said, "Three in the morning and four in the evening." All the monkeys were furious. He said, "Very well then — four in the morning and three in the evening." All the monkeys were delighted. The name and the substance had not changed at all, yet [the monkeys'] anger and delight worked all the same — this too follows from [failing to see] that things are one.)

The keeper feeds his monkeys the same seven acorns per day regardless of the split — the monkeys are only reacting to how the numbers are framed, not to any real difference in outcome. Zhuangzi uses this parable within his broader argument in 齊物論 that apparent distinctions people react emotionally to are often illusory, a matter of framing rather than substance. Note that 芧 (the item distributed) specifically denotes acorns, not chestnuts (栗) as English summaries sometimes loosely render it.

## Standard Form
**朝三暮四**
- 朝四暮三 — the reversed-order form the monkeys preferred within the story itself, also attested as a standalone variant with the identical idiomatic meaning
- 暮四朝三 — a further reordering, less common but attested

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** zhāo sān mù sì
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** ziu1 saam1 mou6 sei3
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** ちょうさんぼし
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 조삼모사 (jo-sam-mo-sa)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** triêu tam mộ tứ

朝 is a genuine heteronym in Mandarin (zhāo "morning" vs. cháo "dynasty, imperial court"), Cantonese (ziu1 "morning" vs. ciu4 "dynasty, court"), and Vietnamese (triêu "morning" vs. triều "court, dynasty") alike — three languages independently preserving the same historical sense-split. This idiom uses the "morning" reading throughout (zhāo/ziu1/triêu), not the "court" reading.

## Cultural Notes
Two related but distinguishable senses circulate across the sphere: (1) manipulating people through superficial rewording while the underlying reality is unchanged — the sense closest to Zhuangzi's own point — and (2) simple capriciousness or fickleness, changing one's mind or plans frequently. Chinese usage leans toward sense (1), especially in political and rhetorical criticism; Korean usage, per some references, extends toward criticizing the easily-deceived monkeys themselves rather than only the deceiver, occasionally framing 조삼모사 as describing gullibility as much as manipulation. Japanese usage centers on sense (2), describing inconsistent plans or policies.

## Example Sentences
1. 他的政策總是朝三暮四，換湯不換藥，讓人難以信任。
2. The company's "new" pricing plan was pure _zhāo sān mù sì_ — same total cost, different monthly breakdown.
3. そんな朝三暮四のやり方では信頼を失う。
4. Investors saw through the _triêu tam mộ tứ_ restructuring immediately — nothing had actually changed.

## Sentiment
Negative to mildly critical; describes manipulation, deception through wordplay, or fickleness. Not a compliment in any of the five languages.
