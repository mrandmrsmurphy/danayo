## Literal Meaning
- [言 (char)](../characters/言%20(char).md) — word, speech
- [行 (char)](../characters/行%20(char).md) — conduct, action
- [一 (char)](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [致 (char)](../characters/致%20(char).md) — to arrive at, to reach

Literally: "Words and actions arrive at one [and the same]." I.e., words and actions are in agreement.

## Extended Meaning
To practice what one says; consistency between speech and conduct. A person to whom this idiom applies keeps their word — their actions match their stated intentions and beliefs, with no gap between promise and performance. Strongly positive, and commonly invoked as a standard of personal integrity and trustworthiness.

## Source and Origin
The earliest occurrence of the exact four-character form that I could verify is in Song dynastic anecdote literature in which Liu Anshi (劉安世, styled 元城) recounts how Sima Guang (司馬光) worked on sincerity. It is in **邵博《邵氏聞見後錄》卷二十** (Southern Song; read at [維基文庫〈邵氏聞見後錄/卷二十〉](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/邵氏聞見後錄/卷二十)), where the text reads:

> 吾初甚易之，退而自隱括日之所行與所言，相掣肘矛盾者多矣，力行七年而後成，自茲言行一致，表裏相應，遇事坦然有余地矣。
> ("At first I thought it very easy; but withdrawing and tallying up what I had done and said each day, I found many contradictions between them. Only after seven years of diligent practice did it come to fruition; from then on my words and actions were in accord, inner and outer corresponding to each other — I met every affair with ease, with room to spare.")

The speaker in the passage is Sima Guang describing his own practice, as Liu Anshi recounts it. I did not check the 《元城語録》 or the 《仕學規範》.

I did not check 趙善璙《自警編·誠實》; the 邵氏聞見後錄 line is checked above. I did not look for an earlier occurrence.

The underlying principle is considerably older, rooted in Confucian ethics going back to the *Analects* (《論語》), where the ideal 君子 ("gentleman") is repeatedly urged to let action, not word, lead:
> 子曰：「君子欲訥於言而敏於行。」（《論語·里仁》4.24, read at [維基文庫〈論語/里仁第四〉](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/論語/里仁第四)）
> (The gentleman wishes to be slow in speech but quick in action.)

The exact four-character idiom 言行一致 appears in the later Song-dynasty text above; it distills the older Confucian principle — that moral integrity requires alignment between what one says and what one does — into a fixed, citable form.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** yán xíng yī zhì
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** jin4 hang4 jat1 zi3
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** げんこういっち
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 언행일치 (eon-haeng-il-chi)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** ngôn hành nhất trí

All five readings are fully compositional, matching each constituent character's own standard reading; Japanese げんこういっち shows the expected sokuon assimilation of いち (ICHI, 一's primary on'yomi) before 致's ち-initial reading, the same pattern seen in [[一致]], [[一体]], and [[一斉]].

## Cultural Notes
In Chinese, 言行一致 is a common moral expectation, especially in evaluating trustworthiness and character. In Japanese, the phrase is often used in educational and ethical contexts, emphasizing credibility and consistency. In Korean, 언행일치 similarly refers to a person whose actions match their words, a key measure of integrity. Related but distinct expressions include 言行相符 ("words and actions correspond") and 知行合一 ("knowledge and action are unified," associated with Wang Yangming) — close in spirit but not orthographic variants of this idiom.

## Example Sentences
*All four examples are constructed, not quotations.*

1. 他一向言行一致，因此深受信任。
2. He consistently practices what he says — _yán xíng yī zhì_ — and is therefore deeply trusted.
3. 言行一致の人は信用される。
4. 그는 언행일치의 모범으로 여겨진다.

## Sentiment
Strongly positive; a standard of personal virtue, trustworthiness, and integrity.
