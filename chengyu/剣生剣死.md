[Biblical Chengyu](chengyu/Biblical%20Chengyu.md)

## Literal Meaning
- [剣 (char)](characters/剣%20(char).md) — sword
- [生](characters/生.md) — live
- [死](characters/死.md) — die

Literally: "Sword, live; sword, die" — by the sword one lives, by the sword one dies.

## Extended Meaning
The idiom states a principle of reciprocal, karmic violence: one who makes the sword — force, aggression, coercion — the instrument of one's life will in turn be destroyed by that same instrument. It is not a literal prediction so much as a moral warning, used to describe warlords, tyrants, gang violence, arms races, or any cycle of retaliation. Always negative or cautionary in valence; never used approvingly. It appears in political commentary, historical narrative, and religious/ethical teaching against vengeance and militarism.

## Source and Origin
**Matthew 26:52** (Gospel of Matthew), set at Jesus's arrest in the Garden of Gethsemane. When one of his companions (identified as Peter in John 18:10) drew a sword and struck the high priest's servant, Jesus rebuked him:

> "Then Jesus said to him, 'Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword.'" (Matt 26:52, ESV)

Greek: *πάντες γὰρ οἱ λαβόντες μάχαιραν ἐν μαχαίρῃ ἀπολοῦνται* — "for all who take up the sword will perish by the sword."

The saying condenses an older Biblical theme (cf. Genesis 9:6, Revelation 13:10) that violence returns upon the violent. The fixed four-character form 剣生剣死 is not a translation of the Greek verse word-for-word but a crystallization made in East Asian Bible translation and commentary, shaped to the four-character idiom mold native to the region (compare the native karmic-retribution idiom 自業自得) even though its source is the New Testament rather than a classical Chinese text.

## Standard Form
**剣生剣死** (Japanese shinjitai 剣) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 劍生劍死 — traditional Chinese form (劍)
- 剑生剑死 — simplified Chinese form (剑)

## Variants
Beyond simple script variation, different East Asian Bible translation traditions render the verse with distinct four-character (or longer) phrasings rather than a single fixed idiom:
- 取剣死剣 (take-sword die-sword) — emphasizes the act of drawing the sword as the cause
- 用剣必亡剣 (use-sword must-perish-sword) — a fuller, more explicitly causal rendering
- 以剣生者以剣死 (by-sword live [one who], by-sword die) — a longer classical-register rendering, closer in word order to the Greek/English original

## Cultural Notes
Chinese-language usage of 剣生剣死 draws strength from a pre-existing native concept of karmic retribution (因果報應, 自業自得), so the idiom reads as immediately intelligible even to non-Christian audiences — it slots into an already-familiar moral logic rather than importing a foreign one.

Japanese usage is comparatively narrower: Japan's small Christian population means けんせいけんし is mostly encountered in sermons, Bible study, and explicitly Christian writing rather than general secular idiom, unlike native yojijukugo.

Korean usage sits between the two: Korea's much larger Christian population means 검생검사 is more likely to appear in sermons, Christian media, and religious education, though it remains a scripture-citation phrase rather than an everyday proverb the way native Korean four-character idioms are.

Vietnamese usage is similarly confined to the country's Catholic and Protestant minority communities, where kiếm sinh kiếm tử functions as a recognizable scriptural allusion rather than mainstream idiom.

## Example Sentences
1. 「凡動刀的，必死在刀下。」(Matt 26:52, 和合本 Chinese Union Version)
2. 暴君剣生剣死，終遭反噬。("The tyrant lived by the sword and died by the sword, ultimately suffering reversal.")
3. 基督教人勿剣生剣死，当以和平待人。("Christ teaches people not to live by the sword and die by the sword, but to treat others with peace.")
4. Every warlord who built his rule on the blade eventually learned the truth of 剣生剣死 the hard way.

## Sentiment
Cautionary, karmic. Used to warn against violence, express the inevitability of retribution, or describe the self-destructive nature of aggression.
