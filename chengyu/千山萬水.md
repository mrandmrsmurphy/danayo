## Literal Meaning
- [千](../characters/千.md) — thousand
- [山 (char)](../characters/山%20(char).md) — mountain(s)
- [万](../characters/万.md) — ten-thousand
- [水 (char)](../characters/水%20(char).md) — water(s)

Literally: "A thousand mountains and ten thousand waters." 千 and 万 are used hyperbolically here — indicating great number, not literal counts.

## Extended Meaning
Describes a great, often difficult distance to travel, or by extension any long and arduous journey or undertaking beset with obstacles. It frequently carries an emotional dimension in poetry: mountains and rivers standing between separated friends, lovers, or exiled officials and the people/places they long for, so the phrase combines literal geographic vastness with a sense of longing or hardship. Register is poetic-literary rather than archaic — it remains current in speeches, song lyrics, and everyday writing to mean simply "a very long and difficult way."

## Source and Origin
**宋之問《至端州驛見杜五審言沈三佺期閻五朝隱王二無競題壁慨然成詠》** ("At Duanzhou Relay Station, Seeing the Wall Inscriptions of Du Shenyan, Shen Quanqi, Yan Chaoyin, and Wang Wujing, I Was Moved to Compose This," early 8th century):
> 豈意南中岐路多，千山萬水分郷縣。
> (Who would have thought the forking roads of the south were so many — a thousand mountains and ten thousand waters dividing county from county.)

Song Zhiwen (宋之問, c. 656–712) wrote this poem while exiled to the far south (Lingnan) under the Tang dynasty, having come across wall inscriptions left by four fellow banished officials at a relay station in Duanzhou. Reflecting on how the mountainous, river-cut terrain of the south kept them scattered and unable to reunite, he used 千山萬水 to capture both the literal ruggedness of the landscape and the ache of prolonged separation. The four-character sequence 千山萬水 already appears verbatim in this original line — this is a direct quotation crystallized into a fixed idiom, not a later compression.

A related but distinct use appears in a later Tang verse by 張喬 ("山水千重隔") — sometimes cited alongside this idiom for its similar mountains-and-waters-of-separation imagery — but the idiom's actual textual origin is Song Zhiwen's poem above, not Zhang Qiao's.

## Standard Form
**千山萬水** (traditional form, matching the original poem) — the standard citation form for this idiom
- 万水千山 / 萬水千山 — a common word-order inversion, equally current and listed as a near-synonym in most dictionaries rather than a mere variant
- The frontmatter here cites the character 万 (not 萬) for Dan'a'yo purposes, matching this vault's own established distinction between 万 (the ordinary numeral) and 萬 (its formal/anti-forgery register form, akin to 七/漆) — both share the identical Dan'a'yo reading, so this is a citation-register choice, not a pronunciation difference.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** qiān shān wàn shuǐ
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** cin1 saan1 maan6 seoi2
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** せんざんばんすい
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 천산만수 (cheon-san-man-su)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** thiên sơn vạn thủy

Japanese せんざんばんすい shows real, lexicalized rendaku (連濁) on the second and fourth morphemes — さん→ざん and まん→ばん — rather than the bare compositional せんさんまんすい; this voicing is fixed in the idiom's standard dictionary reading, not optional.

## Cultural Notes
Common across all five languages in written and literary registers — travel writing, song lyrics, memoirs of hardship, and formal speech about perseverance. It is not typically used for a short or easy trip; the implication of real difficulty and scale is part of the idiom's meaning, distinguishing it from a plain statement of distance. Mandarin and Japanese both treat it as a live, frequently used four-character idiom; Cantonese, Korean, and Vietnamese usage leans more literary/written than conversational.

## Example Sentences
1. 為了尋找失散多年的家人，他跋涉千山萬水，終於在異國重逢。
2. Their pilgrimage took them through _qiān shān wàn shuǐ_ before they reached the shrine.
3. 千山万水も厭わず、彼は恋人のもとへ向かった。
4. No matter how many _thiên sơn vạn thủy_ separate us, I will find my way back to you.

## Sentiment
Neutral to positive; evokes admiration for perseverance and endurance rather than complaint. Frequently paired with expressions of determination, longing, or eventual reunion.
