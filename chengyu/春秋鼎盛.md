## Literal Meaning
- [春 (char)](../characters/春%20(char).md) — spring
- [秋 (char)](../characters/秋%20(char).md) — autumn
- [鼎 (char)](../characters/鼎%20(char).md) — the bronze tripod cauldron; here, "just, right at" (see Source and Origin)
- [盛](../characters/盛.md) — flourishing; full; at its peak

Literally: "Spring and autumn just at their fullness" — one's years are at their height and one's powers at their peak.

## Extended Meaning

Used to describe a person in the prime of life: mature enough to have accumulated experience and standing, yet still vigorous enough to act with full force. The idiom implies the optimal point between the rawness of youth and the decline of old age.

In its original setting it described a reigning emperor in the vigor of his years. In modern Chinese the idiom is applied more broadly to anyone in their 30s–50s at the peak of their career, health, or influence.

## Source and Origin

The phrase comes from **賈誼** (*Jiǎ Yì*, 200–168 BC), the Western Han scholar and court academician under **漢文帝** (Emperor Wen of Han), in his memorial known as the 《治安策》 (*Policy for Peace and Order*), preserved in **《漢書·賈誼傳》** (Book of Han, ch. 48):

> 「天子春秋鼎盛，行義未過，德澤有加焉，猶尚如是，況莫大諸侯，權力且十此者乎！」
> ("The Son of Heaven is in the full vigor of his years; his conduct has been without fault and his kindness has only grown, and still it is thus — how much more so with the great feudal lords, whose power is ten times as great!")

Jia Yi's argument is a warning, not a flattery: even with the emperor in his prime and governing well, the feudal kings are already plotting and rebelling, so the danger will only grow when those kings come of age. The words 春秋鼎盛 here simply describe the emperor as young and vigorous.

**春秋** ("spring and autumn") is a classical metonym for *years* or *age*, and also names the chronicle 《春秋》. For **鼎盛**, the Han commentator 應劭 glosses 鼎 as 方 ("just, at the moment of"), so 鼎盛 means "right at the height of flourishing". The popular reading of 鼎 as the sovereign's bronze cauldron (the Nine Tripods of Yu) is a later picture, not the original sense of the word here. The four-character form 春秋鼎盛 appears already in the original sentence and later became a set phrase.

## Cultural Notes

The register is formal and elevated: 春秋鼎盛 belongs to speeches, formal letters, and historical or literary writing, and is not casual conversation. It has no negative edge; to call someone 春秋鼎盛 is to compliment them as being at their best.

In modern Chinese, the expression has moved into educated everyday usage, applied to anyone in their prime without the specifically royal context. It is also used slightly ironically or playfully when someone in middle age reflects on their remaining vitality.

Japanese しゅんじゅうていせい is used in classical and literary contexts; Korean 춘추정성 appears in historical and formal writing. Vietnamese *xuân thu đỉnh thịnh* is the Sino-Vietnamese rendering; native Vietnamese tends to express the concept through phrases like *đang ở độ sung sức nhất* rather than the Hán-Việt compound.

## Example Sentences
1. 陛下春秋鼎盛，宜勉勵勤政，以安天下。("Your Majesty is in the prime of life; you should diligently govern to bring peace to all under heaven.")
2. 他年方四十，春秋鼎盛，正是大展宏圖的好時機。("At only forty, he is in the prime of life — the perfect moment to realise great ambitions.")
3. The minister addressed the new king: he was 春秋鼎盛, and the realm had never stood in greater need of his full strength.

## Sentiment

Positive; respectful and honorific.
Admiring rather than critical; it states that someone is at their best without implying any further obligation.
