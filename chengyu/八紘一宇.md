## Literal Meaning
- [八紘](../words/八紘.md) — the eight cords, the eight extremities of the world
- [一 (char)](characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [宇](characters/宇.md) — roof, eaves; the space enclosed beneath a roof

Literally: "the eight cords, one roof."

## Extended Meaning
The idea that the world's four quarters and eight directions are united under a single sovereign or order — that the whole world may be gathered beneath one roof. In its original literary setting the phrase reflects cosmological kingship rather than modern nationalism, articulating a universalizing political theology common to East Asian imperial thought. In the twentieth century, however, it was revived as an explicit ideological slogan of Japanese imperial expansionism, and that later usage now dominates the phrase's connotation for most modern readers.

## Source and Origin
The imagery of 八紘 ("eight cords," the eight extremities of the world) appears far earlier than the modern slogan, in *Liezi* (《列子》), "Tang Wen" (湯問) chapter, describing the bottomless valley 歸墟 into which all the world's waters flow:

> "渤海之東不知幾億萬里，有大壑焉，實惟無底之谷，其下無底，名曰歸墟。**八紘** 九野之水，天漢之流，莫不注之，而無增無減焉。"
> ("East of the Bohai Sea, one knows not how many myriads of miles, there is a great chasm — truly a bottomless valley, without bottom, named Guixu. The waters of the eight cords and nine fields, and the flow of the Milky Way, all pour into it, yet it neither increases nor decreases.")

The specific four-character form's proximate source is the **《日本書紀》** (Nihon Shoki, 720 AD), in the account of Emperor Jinmu's foundational edict establishing his capital at Kashihara:

> "兼六合以開都，掩八紘而為宇。"
> ("Uniting the six directions to found the capital, covering the eight cords to make them one roof.")

六合 ("the six directions") refers to the four cardinal points plus above and below; 八紘 extends this spatial imagery outward to the eight extremities of the world. However, the exact four-character idiom 八紘一宇 itself is a modern coinage: it was created in 1903 by the Nichiren Buddhist nationalist thinker Tanaka Chigaku (田中智学), who compressed the Nihon Shoki phrase into this fixed slogan as a statement of Japan's world-unifying mission. The slogan was subsequently adopted as official state ideology during the Shōwa era's expansionist period, taking on explicitly imperial and geopolitical meaning far removed from its classical narrative origin — a later usage that now strongly colors contemporary perception of the phrase.

## Cultural Notes
Today 八紘一宇 appears primarily in historical discussion, especially in studies of Japanese political thought and wartime ideology; it is not used casually in everyday speech, and its tone is formal and historically fraught. In Japan the phrase remains closely associated with wartime propaganda and is generally avoided or used only with explicit historical framing in mainstream discourse. Outside Japan, in Mandarin, Cantonese, Korean, and Vietnamese, the phrase is essentially a specialist historical term encountered in the context of studying Japanese imperialism rather than a living idiom in any of those languages.

## Example Sentences
1. 田中智学於1903年創「八紘一宇」一語，後為軍國主義所用。("Tanaka Chigaku coined the phrase 'hakkō ichiu' in 1903; it was later appropriated by militarist ideology.")
2. Historians studying wartime Japan cannot avoid the loaded slogan *hakkō ichiu* — "the whole world under one roof."
3. 八紘一宇曾被用作對外擴張的口號，今日多見於歴史研究。("Hakkō ichiu was once used as a slogan for external expansion; today it appears mainly in historical research.")
4. The phrase's shift from cosmological metaphor to imperial slogan makes 八紘一宇 a cautionary case study in how classical language can be repurposed for modern ideology.

## Sentiment
Historically fraught and largely negative in modern connotation, owing to its use as wartime imperial propaganda; formal and academic in register, essentially absent from casual speech.
