## Literal Meaning
- [東](../characters/東.md) — east
- [奔](../characters/奔.md) — run hurriedly
- [西](../characters/西.md) — west
- [走 (char)](../characters/走%20(char).md) — run, go

Literally: "Run east, run west" — the paired-direction construction (east/west, or elsewhere north/south) is a common classical Chinese device for expressing motion in many directions at once, rather than any specific eastward or westward trip.

## Extended Meaning
Describes someone constantly on the move, rushing from place to place to handle many tasks, obligations, or urgent matters — busy travel or effort, often with a tone of hardship, diligence, or being pulled in many directions at once. Applies equally to a literal journey (a messenger, an official, a traveler) or a figurative one (someone juggling many responsibilities).

## Source and Origin
**魏初《沁園春·留別張周卿韻》** (Wei Chu 魏初, 1232–1292, Yuan dynasty official and poet; a *ci*-poem in the "Qinyuanchun" pattern, written on parting from his friend Zhang Zhouqing):
> 甚年來行役，交情契闊，東奔西走，水送山迎。遙望神州，故人千里，何事今年共此行。
> (These past years of official travel — friendships grown distant, running east and west, seen off by rivers and greeted by mountains. Gazing afar toward the homeland, old friends a thousand miles away — what brings us, this year, to share this same journey?)

Wei Chu uses 東奔西走 here to describe the constant travel demanded by his official duties, separating him from friends and home. The phrase later became a standard fixed idiom for busy, ceaseless movement in general, and appears in later vernacular fiction as well — notably in 《西遊記》(*Journey to the West*), chapter 35: "趕得東奔西走，出路無門" ("chased about running east and west, with no way out").

## Standard Form
**東奔西走**
- 东奔西走 — simplified form
- 東奔西跑 — a common variant substituting 跑 ("run, dash") for 走, equally current
- Occasionally paired in parallel with **南奔北走** ("run south and north"), extending the same paired-direction pattern

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** dōng bēn xī zǒu
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** dung1 ban1 sai1 zau2
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** とうほんせいそう
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 동분서주 (dong-bun-seo-ju)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** đông bôn tây tẩu

All five readings are fully compositional, matching each constituent character's own standard reading exactly.

## Cultural Notes
Used across all five languages to describe busy, obligatory travel or effort — most often sympathetically (hard work, dutiful hustle) rather than critically. Common in describing officials, businesspeople, parents managing many errands, or anyone whose responsibilities pull them in many directions. Korean usage in particular often carries a tone of diligence and conscientious effort rather than complaint.

## Example Sentences
1. 為了辦成這件事，他東奔西走了好幾天，終於找到解決辦法。
2. He'd been _dōng bēn xī zǒu_ for a week straight, chasing down permits and signatures.
3. 彼は仕事のために東奔西走している。
4. She spent the whole campaign _đông bôn tây tẩu_, rallying support district by district.

## Sentiment
Neutral to positive; conveys diligent, hardworking effort under pressure of many obligations, rather than complaint or criticism.
