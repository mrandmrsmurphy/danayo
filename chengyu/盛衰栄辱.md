## Literal Meaning
- [盛](characters/盛.md) — flourishing, prosperous
- [衰](characters/衰.md) — decline, wane
- [栄](characters/栄.md) — glory, honor
- [辱](characters/辱.md) — disgrace, humiliation

Literally: "Prosperity, decline, glory, disgrace."

## Extended Meaning
盛衰栄辱 names the alternating ups and downs of fortune: the inevitable rise and fall of things, and the cyclical nature of success and failure, honor and shame. It is used as a sober, cyclical view of existence — reminding rulers, scholars, and ordinary people alike that fortune is fleeting but virtue endures. Applied to individuals, dynasties, businesses, or nations, always with a reflective or moralizing tone rather than as neutral description.

## Source and Origin
The dualism of 盛衰 (rise/decline) and 榮辱 (honor/disgrace) as a pair of opposites is old and diffuse across Chinese classical thought rather than traceable to a single coining text. A frequently cited line expressing the same equanimity toward gain and loss, glory and disgrace, comes from **范仲淹's 《岳陽樓記》** (1046 AD):

> 「不以物喜，不以己悲。」
> "Not exulting in external gain, nor grieving over personal loss."

By the Han–Tang period, four-character compounds built from this dualism (盛衰榮辱 and its variants) were well established as moral reflections on the impermanence of worldly fortune.

## Standard Form
**盛衰栄辱** (Japanese shinjitai 栄) is the Dan'a'yo standard form.
- 盛衰榮辱 — traditional Chinese form (榮)
- 盛衰荣辱 — simplified Chinese form (荣)

## Variants
- 榮枯盛衰 — "flourishing and withering, rise and fall"
- 興亡盛衰 — "rise and decline of states"
- 榮辱不驚 — "unmoved by honor or disgrace"

## Cultural Notes
In Confucian thought, the phrase teaches moderation and detachment — the gentleman (君子) maintains virtue through all circumstances regardless of fortune's turns. In Daoist philosophy, it reflects the natural cycle whereby anything that reaches fullness (盛) turns toward decline (衰), echoing 物極必反 ("when things reach their extreme, they reverse"). In Buddhist usage, it reflects impermanence (無常): glory and disgrace, rise and fall, are all transient.

Chinese historians used the phrase to describe dynastic cycles; Japanese samurai and Zen monks used it to express 無常 (*mujō*), the transience of fame and success; Korean and Vietnamese scholars quoted it as a reminder of humility and detachment.

## Example Sentences
1. 「不以物喜，不以己悲。」(范仲淹《岳陽樓記》)
2. 人生有盛衰榮辱，唯德者常安。("In life there is rise and fall, honor and disgrace; only the virtuous remain at peace.")
3. 세상일에는 성쇠영욕이 있기 마련이다. ("In worldly affairs, prosperity and disgrace are inevitable.")
4. The old general had seen every kind of 盛衰栄辱 in his long career and had stopped being surprised by either.

## Sentiment
Sober, reflective, cyclical. Glory and shame, rise and fall — all things have their season; fortune is fleeting, but virtue endures.
