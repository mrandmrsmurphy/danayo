## Literal Meaning
- [弱 (char)](../characters/弱%20(char).md) — weak
- [肉 (char)](../characters/肉%20(char).md) — flesh, meat
- [強 (char)](../characters/強%20(char).md) — strong
- [食 (char)](../characters/食%20(char).md) — to eat

Literally: "The weak are meat, the strong eat [it]."

## Extended Meaning
The strong prey on the weak; survival of the fittest; a ruthless competitive order in which advantage alone, not fairness or merit, determines outcomes. Close in force to the English idiom "law of the jungle." Used of nature, business, geopolitics, and any arena where the powerful dominate the powerless — the register can be flatly descriptive (a neutral observation about how a market or ecosystem works) or pointedly critical (condemning a system for tolerating or rewarding exploitation).

## Source and Origin
The imagery traces to **韓愈《送浮屠文暢師序》** ("Preface Seeing Off the Monk Wenchang," Tang dynasty), in a passage contrasting the animal world with human civilization:
> 夫鳥俛而啄，仰而四顧；夫獸深居而簡出：懼物之為己害也，猶且不脫焉。弱之肉，強之食，今我與文暢安居而暇食，優遊以生死，與禽獸異者，寧可不知其所自耶？
> (Birds peck at the ground, then raise their heads to look about; beasts stay hidden and rarely emerge — fearing harm from other creatures, yet unable to escape it. The weak become flesh, the strong its food. Now Wenchang and I live at ease and eat at leisure, passing through life and death unhurried — how could we not know what sets us apart from the birds and beasts?)

Han Yu's point is Confucian: ritual propriety and moral principle, not raw predation, are what distinguish human society from the animal world. His phrasing **弱之肉，強之食** ("the weak's flesh, the strong's food") uses grammatical particles 之 not present in the modern fixed form; the crystallized four-character idiom **弱肉強食** emerged later, and gained especially wide currency in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in China and Japan, where it was used to describe international power politics and served as a ready rendering for Social Darwinist ideas of "survival of the fittest" entering East Asia from the West.

All five readings are fully compositional, matching each constituent character's own standard reading.

## Cultural Notes
In modern Chinese, 弱肉強食 commonly describes harsh competition, whether in nature, business, or geopolitics. In Japanese, the idiom became especially prominent in Meiji-era discourse, often invoked in discussions of national strength and global rivalry as a near-equivalent of Western Social Darwinism. In Korean, 약육강식 similarly describes a brutal competitive environment, sometimes with critical or cautionary overtones. Across all three, the phrase can be either descriptive (acknowledging a harsh reality) or condemnatory (criticizing a system for permitting it) depending on context.

## Example Sentences
1. 在這個行業中，常常是弱肉強食。
2. In this industry, it is often a case of _ruò ròu qiáng shí_ — the strong preying on the weak.
3. 自然界は弱肉強食の世界だ。
4. 그 사회는 약육강식의 논리가 지배한다.

## Sentiment
Harsh and often critical, though sometimes merely descriptive; evokes a ruthless order governed by power rather than fairness — the East Asian counterpart to the English "law of the jungle."
