## Literal Meaning
- [欲](../characters/欲.md) — desire
- [求](../characters/求.md) — to seek
- [満 (char)](../characters/満%20(char).md) — full, satisfied
- [不 (char)](../characters/不%20(char).md) — not

Literally: "desires sought, [but] not satisfied."

## Extended Meaning
Frustration; dissatisfaction; the state of yearning without fulfillment. In casual Japanese usage the term (often abbreviated 欲不満) can narrow specifically to sexual frustration, but its core sense across the region is the broader psychological condition of unmet desire — romantic, ambitious, or existential, not merely sexual or material.

## Source and Origin
欲求不満 is a modern compound built from classical roots rather than a phrase inherited whole from an ancient text. [欲](../characters/欲.md) ("desire") is a central concern throughout the *Dao De Jing* and Buddhist sutras, most often named as an obstacle to peace; chapter 37 of the *Dao De Jing* teaches that stilling desire (不欲) brings the world to rest of its own accord ("不欲以靜，天下將自定"). The related four-character phrase 無欲則剛 ("without desire, one becomes unyielding") is popularly but incorrectly attributed to the *Dao De Jing* itself — its clearest historical attestation is instead the Qing-dynasty official Lin Zexu's (林則徐) self-motivating 1839 couplet, "海納百川，有容乃大；壁立千仞，無欲則剛" ("the sea admits a hundred rivers, and so becomes vast; a cliff stands a thousand yards sheer, for it desires nothing"), the ultimate source of the phrase itself being older and disputed.

The full expression 欲求不満 gained its modern prominence specifically in Japanese psychology and sociology of the late Meiji through early Shōwa period (c. 1900–1930), where it served as the standard translation for the Western psychological concept of "frustrated desire" or frustration. From Japanese it spread into modern Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese discourse as both a clinical/psychological and everyday moral term.

## Cultural Notes
欲求不満 names one of the deepest paradoxes of human life — the endless hunger of the mind and senses, and the suffering that follows when craving outpaces reality. In Confucian ethics, desire (欲) is not evil in itself but must be governed by propriety (禮) and measured by righteousness (義); excess desire leads to disorder, both personal and social. In Daoist philosophy the highest wisdom is 少私寡欲 ("few private desires"), since desire is held to disturb the heart's balance and cloud perception of the Dao. In Buddhist thought the concept is nearly synonymous with *dukkha* (suffering): the Second Noble Truth teaches that suffering arises from *taṇhā* (craving, 渴愛), with liberation coming through detachment. In modern psychology and art the idiom expresses emotional or existential frustration more broadly — the restless gap between wanting and being, whether romantic yearning, unfulfilled ambition, or the emptiness of consumer desire.

## Example Sentences
1. 人若被欲求不滿所困，終日不得安寧。("One who is trapped by unfulfilled desire can never find peace.")
2. 欲求不満が続くと、心が荒んでしまう。("When frustration continues, the heart becomes desolate.")
3. 그는 욕구불만으로 괴로워했다。("He suffered from frustrated desire.")
4. Dục cầu bất mãn là nguồn gốc của khổ đau。("Unfulfilled desire is the source of suffering.")

## Sentiment
Restless, dissatisfied, mildly melancholic. Names a psychological or existential condition rather than expressing outright anger.
