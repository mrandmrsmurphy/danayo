## Literal Meaning
- [三 (char)](../characters/三%20(char).md) — three
- [綱](../characters/綱.md) — guiding cord; bond
- [五 (char)](../characters/五%20(char).md) — five
- [常](../characters/常.md) — constant

Literally: "the three bonds and the five constants."

## Extended Meaning
三綱五常 names the complete framework of imperial Confucian ethics: [[三綱]], the Three Bonds that structure hierarchical relationships (ruler–subject, father–son, husband–wife, each pairing assigning the first party a dominant role the second must obey), and [[五常]], the Five Constant Virtues that should govern personal conduct (仁 benevolence, 義 righteousness, 禮 propriety, 智 wisdom, 信 trustworthiness). Used together, the phrase stands for the entire traditional moral-political order — invoked either with reverence, as the bedrock of civilized society, or critically, as shorthand for rigid patriarchal hierarchy, depending on the speaker's stance toward Confucian tradition.

## Source and Origin
The term 三綱 itself first appears in Western Han scholar Dong Zhongshu's (董仲舒, c. 179–104 BC) *Chunqiu Fanlu* (《春秋繁露·基義》), which states:

> "王道之三綱，可求於天。"
> ("The three bonds of the kingly way may be sought in Heaven.")

grounding the hierarchy of ruler/subject, father/son, and husband/wife in yin-yang cosmology (the dominant party corresponding to yang, the subordinate to yin). However, Dong Zhongshu uses the term only in passing and does not define it systematically. The authoritative definition comes a century later from the Eastern Han **《白虎通義·三綱六紀》**, the record of the official White Tiger Hall conference (白虎觀會議) convened by Emperor Zhang in 79 AD to settle Confucian orthodoxy:

> "三綱者何？謂君臣、父子、夫婦也...君為臣綱，父為子綱，夫為妻綱。"
> ("What are the Three Bonds? They are ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife... the ruler is the bond for the subject, the father is the bond for the son, the husband is the bond for the wife.")

五常 (仁義禮智信), the Five Constant Virtues, are older individually — each traceable to core Confucian and Mencian texts — but were formally paired with 三綱 into the combined doctrine by this same Han-dynasty synthesis. The fixed four-character compound 三綱五常 crystallized later as shorthand for the whole paired doctrine, and became the standard term used throughout imperial Chinese history for the complete Confucian social-ethical order.

## Cultural Notes
In Mandarin and Cantonese contexts the phrase is now almost always historical or critical in register — invoked in discussions of Confucian tradition, gender roles, or as a byword for outdated patriarchal hierarchy in modern critique, rather than as a live prescriptive standard. Japanese さんこうごじょう and Korean 삼강오상 carry the same historical-Confucian register, encountered mainly in scholarly, historical, or classical-education contexts (Korean 서당 and 한문 education in particular still teaches the Five Constants as core ethical vocabulary). Vietnamese tam cương ngũ thường likewise survives as the standard term in discussions of traditional Confucian ethics in Vietnamese scholarship, paired exactly as in the source Chinese formula.

## Example Sentences
1. 舊社會強調三綱五常，女性地位低下。
2. Traditional society emphasized *sāngāngwǔcháng*, leaving women with a subordinate status.
3. 儒家倫理的核心可以用三綱五常四個字概括。
4. The core of Confucian ethics can be summed up in the four characters *sāngāngwǔcháng*.

## Sentiment
Mixed to negative in contemporary usage; historically neutral-to-reverent as a description of orthodox Confucian order, but now most often invoked critically as shorthand for rigid, patriarchal hierarchy.
