[Dan'a'yo Chengyu](chengyu/Dan'a'yo%20Chengyu.md)
>[!Tip] The guiding maxim for translation and loanword practice in [[文法 - 06語用]].

## Literal Meaning
- [義](characters/義.md) — meaning, righteousness
- [重 (char)](characters/重%20(char).md) — heavy, weighty, to value
- [於 (char)](characters/於%20(char).md) — than (comparative particle)
- [音](characters/音.md) — sound

Literally: "Meaning outweighs sound."

## Extended Meaning
義重於音 states that, in cases of tension, semantic force takes priority over phonetic resemblance. It applies especially where a translator is tempted by phonetic mimicry, rhetorical symmetry, alliteration or metrical echo, or preservation of a proper name purely by sound — the idiom asserts that semantic integrity governs such choices. This is not a general hostility to form: it is opposition to form specifically when form distorts sense. It implies a particular view of language — words are vehicles, meaning is the cargo, and if the vessel must change to preserve the cargo, so be it.

Practically, this means: when a pun cannot survive transfer, the argument should be preserved and the pun annotated rather than forced; when meter obscures logic, logical clarity should be privileged; when phonetic imitation would mislead readers about conceptual categories, it should be abandoned; and in theological or philosophical translation, conceptual accuracy governs stylistic charm rather than the reverse.

## Source and Origin
義重於音 is a Dan'a'yo-coined idiom (単亜語), the guiding maxim for translation and loanword practice in the language (see [[文法 - 06語用]]). It rejects the fragile practice of phonetic transcription, which varies across regions and shifts over time, in favor of semantic clarity, and enshrines Dan'a'yo's translation policy: semantic transparency ensures stability and mutual comprehension, whereas phonetic imitation risks confusion and obsolescence. One named sub-principle flows from this maxim: [[義以立名]] ("names should be established by meaning"), holding that names should be established by meaning, conveying an identity or quality that endures across languages, rather than being fixed to any single language's sound system.

The maxim also takes a side in a long-standing tension in translation history between sound-based and sense-based transfer — paralleling the Western debate between "word-for-word" and "sense-for-sense" translation, and the historical struggle among Buddhist translators over whether to transliterate Sanskrit terms or translate their meaning. 義重於音 sides clearly with interpretive clarity. It also bears directly on Dan'a'yo's other translation-discipline motto, [[信達雅化]]: of Yan Fu's three virtues, 信 (faithfulness) aligns naturally with 義重於音, 達 (intelligibility) supports it since clarity usually requires privileging sense, and 雅 (elegance) is the virtue most constrained by it, since elegance often leans on sound. Where 信達雅化 names the discipline as a whole, 義重於音 supplies the ranking principle for when its component virtues conflict.

## Cultural Notes
義重於音 is grammar-policy language rather than an idiom in general circulation, cited specifically when explaining why Dan'a'yo coins new vocabulary by meaning rather than by transliterating foreign sounds — unlike, for instance, how many modern Sinitic and Japanese loanwords are built phonetically from English or other source languages. It stands alongside the language's other founding mottos ([[合漢再決]], [[東亜自通]], [[覧昭和決]], [[毎字明意]], [[朝鮮正音]], [[文言現代]], [[百家共承]], [[文体並存]]) as part of the same family of design-principle statements.

## Example Sentences
1. 単亜語は義重於音の原則に従い、新語を音訳ではなく意訳で作る。("Dan'a'yo follows the principle of 義重於音, coining new words by meaning rather than by phonetic transcription.")
2. When a proper name would only confuse readers if borrowed by sound, 義重於音 says: translate the meaning instead, and let the name carry sense across every language it crosses.
3. 이 언어의 조어 원칙은 의중어음에 근거한다. ("This language's word-coining principle is based on 의중어음.")

## Sentiment
Principled, discerning, unsentimental about sound. States a clear priority for resolving conflicts between fidelity to meaning and fidelity to form.
