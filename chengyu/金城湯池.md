## Literal Meaning
- [金 (char)](../characters/金%20(char).md) — metal, gold
- [城](../characters/城.md) — city wall
- [湯 (char)](../characters/湯%20(char).md) — hot/boiling water
- [池 (char)](../characters/池%20(char).md) — moat, pond

Literally: "golden walls, a moat of boiling water."

## Extended Meaning
An impregnable defense; a position so strongly fortified that it cannot be attacked successfully. It describes a condition where attack itself becomes irrational because the defensive advantage is so overwhelming — praise not merely of strength, but of a defense so complete that assault is pointless.

## Source and Origin
From **《漢書·蒯伍江息夫傳·蒯通》**, recounting how the persuasive strategist Kuai Tong (蒯通), at the end of the Qin dynasty, argued that if the cities of the empire fortified themselves properly, none could be taken by force:

> "皆為金城湯池，不可攻也。"
> ("All would become cities of metal walls and boiling moats — impossible to attack.")

Kuai Tong used the image to persuade the commander of Fanyang (范陽) to surrender peacefully rather than risk destruction, arguing that other cities, seeing Fanyang's fate, would otherwise fortify themselves into such impregnable strongholds that pacifying the realm would become far harder.

## Cultural Notes
The two images reinforce each other: 城 (a city wall) made of gold suggests perfect strength and incorruptibility, while 湯池 describes a moat filled with boiling water, an impossible barrier to cross. Together they form an exaggerated but vivid description of total defensive security. Unlike many chengyu drawn from moral commentary, 金城湯池 belongs to the vocabulary of military and political analysis, emphasizing strategic advantage created by geography, fortification, or preparation rather than courage or numbers. The phrase also reflects a broader pattern in classical Chinese rhetoric: pairing two images of the same quality to intensify a single concept rather than to diversify it — both halves independently express invulnerability. Across the region today it is used metaphorically for any strongly protected position, whether physical, political, or institutional, appearing in historical writing, political commentary, and sometimes business or organizational language to describe systems extremely difficult to penetrate or overturn.

## Example Sentences
1. 這座城池防守嚴密，堪稱金城湯池。("This city is so tightly defended it could be called an impregnable fortress.")
2. Their cybersecurity system was a veritable 金城湯池 — no breach in over a decade.
3. この会社の財務基盤は金城湯池だ。("This company's financial foundation is rock-solid and impenetrable.")
4. 敵軍見城防堅固如金城湯池，遂不敢輕舉妄動。("Seeing the defenses as solid as an impregnable fortress, the enemy army dared not act rashly.")

## Sentiment
Admiring, emphatic. Praises overwhelming strength or security, sometimes bordering on hyperbole.
