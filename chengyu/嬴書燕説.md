## Literal Meaning
- [嬴](../characters/嬴.md) — Ying, the capital of Chu (written 郢 in Chinese; 郢 is an alias of 嬴 in Dan'a'yo)
- [書](../characters/書.md) — letter; writing
- [燕](../characters/燕.md) — Yan, a northern state
- [説](../characters/説.md) — to explain; an explanation

Literally: "A letter from Ying, explained in Yan."

## Extended Meaning
To read a meaning into a text that its writer never intended: a forced, far-fetched or mistaken interpretation. In the story the mistaken reading happened to do good, so the idiom can carry a faint irony: the reading is wrong, but it works. It is mostly used of scholars and commentators who strain a passage to fit a theory.

## Source and Origin
The idiom comes from **《韓非子·外儲說左上》** (Han Feizi, 'Outer Collection of Sayings, Left, Part 1'). A man of Ying wrote a letter to the chief minister of Yan at night, by a dim lamp, and told the candle-holder to raise the light. He wrote "raise the candle" into the letter by mistake. The minister of Yan read it as advice, and the king was delighted:

**《韓非子·外儲說左上》** (compiled 3rd c. BC):
> "郢人有遺燕相國書者，夜書，火不明，因謂持燭者曰：『舉燭。』云而過書『舉燭』。舉燭，非書意也。燕相受書而說之，曰：『舉燭者，尚明也；尚明也者，舉賢而任之。』燕相白王，王大說，國以治。治則治矣，非書意也。今世舉學者多似此類。"
> (A man of Ying sent a letter to the chief minister of Yan. Writing at night, the light was dim, so he told the candle-holder, "Raise the candle," and wrote "raise the candle" into the letter by mistake. "Raise the candle" was not what the letter meant. The minister of Yan received it and was pleased with it, saying, "To raise the candle is to prize brightness; to prize brightness is to raise the worthy and employ them." The minister told the king, the king was greatly pleased, and the state was well governed. Well governed it was, but that was not what the letter meant. Many scholars of today are like this.)

The four-character form 郢書燕說 is the later shortening of the story, and the closing line shows how the text itself applies it to scholars. Checked on zh.Wikisource, 韓非子 外儲說左上, on 2026-11-09: the passage is as above, and the page is marked fully proofread.

## Standard Form
**嬴書燕説** — Dan'a'yo's own spelling. The first character is 嬴 in place of 郢 (郢 is kept as an alias of 嬴, with the same derived sound), and the last is 説, the Japanese form of 說.
- 郢書燕說 — the traditional Chinese spelling
- 郢書燕説 — with 説
- 郢书燕说 — simplified Chinese
- 嬴書燕說 — Dan'a'yo's 嬴 with 說

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** yǐng shū yān shuō
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** jing5 syu1 jin3 syut3
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** えいしょえんせつ
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 영서연설 (yeong-seo-yeon-seol)
- **Vietnamese (Hán–Việt):** dĩnh thư yên thuyết

The Dan'a'yo sound of the first character is ⼶ㄫ (the sound of 嬴 and 盈), written from the derived sound of 郢, and is not tied to its tone. The Mandarin, Cantonese and Vietnamese forms above are those of the actual name 郢; the Japanese and Korean forms are the usual ones.

## Cultural Notes
Chinese: a well-known literary idiom, used in essays and in discussion of how texts are read. Japanese (えいしょえんせつ), Korean (영서연설) and Vietnamese (dĩnh thư yên thuyết) know it in the same learned register. It pairs with the idiom 牽強附會, which also describes forcing a meaning on a text.

## Example Sentences
1. 燕相受書而說之。
2. His reading of the poem is pure _yǐng shū yān shuō_: the author never meant any of it.
3. 他把這段話解成這樣，簡直是郢書燕說。

## Sentiment
Mildly negative; learned and ironic, a criticism of forced interpretation.
