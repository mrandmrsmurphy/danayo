## Literal Meaning
- [一](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [長](../characters/長%20(char).md) — long
- [一](../characters/一%20(char).md) — one
- [短](../characters/短%20(char).md) — short

Literally: "One long, one short."

## Extended Meaning
Wiktionary gives two senses, by language. The Japanese entry 一長一短 (いっちょういったん) is "advantages and disadvantages, pros and cons". The Chinese entry glosses the idiom "talking endlessly; long-winded". The figurative "pros and cons" sense is the one Dan'a'yo uses as the base meaning of this page, and it is attested in Japanese.

## Source and Origin
I read the 《史記》 biography of Li Si (卷八七) on wikisource and the phrase does not occur in it.

The earliest use of the four characters that I could verify is literal. The 《說文解字》 entry for 冊 (bamboo-slip book) describes the graph as depicting slips bound together: 「冊，符命也。諸侯進受於王也。象其札一長一短，中有二編之形」, "the character shows the slips, one long and one short, with two cords binding them in the middle." Here 一長一短 simply describes slips of two lengths.

The thought that everything has both strengths and weaknesses is old and is expressed in different words, most famously in the 《楚辭·卜居》, where the diviner declines to answer: 「夫尺有所短，寸有所長；物有所不足，智有所不明」, "a foot has its shortness and an inch its length; things have what they lack and wisdom has what it does not see." The figurative four-character 一長一短, meaning "each has pros and cons", is the modern idiom built on this idea. I have not established its first figurative attestation.

## Standard Form
**一長一短** (traditional) / **一长一短** (simplified) — both current.

## Cultural Notes
English Wiktionary gives 一長一短 the Japanese sense "advantages and disadvantages, pros and cons" and the Chinese sense "talking endlessly; long-winded". It lists 三長兩短 as a related Chinese entry.

## Example Sentences
1. どの方法にも一長一短があるから、状況に応じて選ぶべきだ。
   "Every method has its strengths and weaknesses, so you should choose according to the situation."

## Sentiment
Neutral; it weighs good points against bad ones.
