## Literal Meaning
- [天 (char)](characters/天%20(char).md) — heaven, natural
- [真 (char)](characters/真%20(char).md) — genuine, true
- 乱 — (see aliases: cross-linguistic readings throughout this page reflect 爛/烂, "brilliant, blooming," per the idiom's real pronunciation and its own listed variant forms)
- [漫](characters/漫.md) — free-flowing, unrestrained

Literally: "Heaven's truth, bright and blooming."

## Extended Meaning
天真爛漫 describes something pure, innocent, natural, and unaffected — especially the simplicity and candor of a child or a person of guileless character. 爛漫 (lànmàn) originally meant "luxuriant, brilliant, blossoming in profusion," so the compound reads as "innocence that shines forth like blooming flowers." Used across the region to describe pure-hearted, straightforward people, especially children or artists — genuine rather than naïve.

## Source and Origin
Taiwan's Ministry of Education *Dictionary of Chinese Idioms* gives as the source (語本) the Southern Song painter Gong Kai's (龔開) poem 〈高馬小兒圖〉, as quoted in Yuan Wu Shidao's 《吳禮部詩話》:

> 「此兒此馬俱可憐，馬方三齒兒未冠。天真爛熳好容儀，楚楚衣裝無不宜。」
> ("This boy and this horse are both endearing: the horse is only three years old and the boy not yet capped. His artless, radiant bearing is fine, and his neat clothes suit him in every way.")

The line has 爛熳, a variant of 爛漫. The same entry lists later documented uses: Song Yue Ke's 《寶真齋法書贊》 vol. 5 (「蓋其天真爛漫，妙入神品」, of the calligrapher Zhang Xu), Ming Hu Yinglin's 《詩藪》 (of Su Shi), 《聊齋志異》 vol. 12 and 《三俠五義》 chapter 76. I relied on the Ministry's entry and did not check these against the texts or find an earlier use.

The philosophical background of 天真 is the Daoist idea of Heaven-given genuineness. **《莊子·漁父》**, checked on Wikisource, says:

> 「真者，精誠之至也。…真者，所以受於天也，自然不可易也。」
> ("Genuineness is the utmost of sincerity... Genuineness is what one receives from Heaven; it is natural and cannot be altered.")

This passage does not contain the compound 天真爛漫, so it is the idea behind 天真, not the source of the idiom.

**Corrected.** The earlier version of this page gave the 《莊子·漁父》 as the origin and said the idiom "dates to Tang–Song dynasty poetry." The documented source I found is Song, so `origin` is now the Gong Kai poem.

## Standard Form
**天真爛漫** (traditional 爛) is the historically standard form, with 天真烂漫 (simplified) and archaic variants 天眞爛漫/天眞爛熳 also attested.

## Related Expressions
Related in sense (though distinct idioms) are 純真無邪 ("pure and without evil"), 童心未泯 ("childlike heart not yet faded"), 光風霽月 ("bright wind and clear moon," i.e. clear and noble character), and 返璞歸真 ("return to uncarved simplicity").

## Cultural Notes
In Daoist thought, 天真爛漫 names naturalness (自然) uncorrupted by artificial striving — the childlike spontaneity praised by Zhuangzi. In Confucian usage it suggests sincerity and moral simplicity before experience complicates virtue. In Buddhist usage it evokes the original purity of mind before delusion. In modern usage across all four daughter languages it simply describes childlike candor and a bright, open personality: Japanese 天真爛漫 (tenshin ranman) is common in both literature and daily speech; Korean 천진난만 (cheonjin nanman) carries the same connotation; Vietnamese thiên chân lạn mạn is used in literary contexts to mean "innocent, naturally beautiful."

## Example Sentences
1. 「真者，所以受於天也，自然不可易也。」(《莊子·漁父》)
2. 她的笑容天真爛漫，像春天的花一樣明亮。("Her smile was pure and radiant, like flowers in spring.")
3. 그 아이는 천진난만하게 웃었다. ("The child laughed with innocent joy.")
4. Nụ cười của cô ấy thiên chân lạn mạn như hoa nở mùa xuân. ("Her smile was innocent and radiant like spring blossoms.")

## Sentiment
Warm, admiring, luminous. Innocence that shines like flowers in full bloom — the radiance of an uncorrupted heart.
