## Literal Meaning
- [公 (char)](../characters/公%20(char).md) — public, impartial
- [明 (char)](../characters/明%20(char).md) — bright, clear
- [正 (char)](../characters/正%20(char).md) — upright, correct
- [大 (char)](../characters/大%20(char).md) — great, magnanimous

Literally: "Public, bright, upright, and great" — built from two existing two-character compounds, [[公明]] ("fair and impartial") and [[正大]] ("upright and dignified"), joined into a single four-character idiom.

## Extended Meaning
Describes conduct, decisions, or character that are fair, transparent, and free of hidden motives or favoritism — the opposite of scheming, secretive, or self-interested behavior. It is almost always positive or approving, used to praise a person's integrity or to describe a process (a competition, a judgment, an administrative decision) as legitimately impartial. Common as a personal motto (座右の銘) in Japanese, reflecting its aspirational, virtue-naming character.

## Source and Origin
**陳確《翠薄山房帖》** (Chen Que 陳確, 1604–1677, early Qing dynasty scholar of Zhejiang):
> 若一本于公明正大之心，則豈直如此而已。
> (If one's foundation is a heart that is fair and upright, then how could it stop at merely this?)

Chen Que was known for resisting the prevailing Neo-Confucian orthodoxy of his era in favor of practical, independent ethical reasoning; this line reflects his emphasis on personal integrity as the root of right action. The four-character sequence 公明正大 already appears verbatim in this passage, built by combining two already-existing two-character compounds, [[公明]] and [[正大]], rather than coining new vocabulary — but the fixed, citable four-character idiom traces to this specific line.

## Pronunciations
- **Mandarin (Pinyin):** gōng míng zhèng dà
- **Cantonese (Jyutping):** gung1 ming4 zing3 daai6
- **Japanese (on-yomi, hiragana):** こうめいせいだい
- **Korean (Sino-Korean):** 공명정대 (gong-myeong-jeong-dae)
- **Vietnamese (Hán-Việt):** công minh chính đại

Japanese こうめいせいだい voices the final morpheme (大 TAI→DAI) rather than the bare compositional こうめいせいたい — this voicing is fixed in the idiom's standard dictionary reading.

## Cultural Notes
A commonly confused near-doublet exists in Chinese usage: 公正明大 (characters in the wrong order) is a frequent misspelling that Japanese and Chinese reference sources alike specifically warn against — the correct order pairs 公明 and 正大 as two established compounds, not 公正 and 明大 (which is not an established compound). In Japanese business and self-improvement writing, 公明正大 is a favorite phrase for describing fair, transparent institutional conduct (a referee, a judge, a company's decision-making) as well as personal integrity as a stated ideal. Korean usage leans similarly toward praising judges, administrators, and officials for impartial conduct. A closely related but distinct idiom is 光明正大 ("bright and upright"), overlapping heavily in sense but built from a different first compound (光明 "brightness" rather than 公明 "fairness").

## Example Sentences
1. 他以公明正大的態度處理這件事情，贏得了眾人的信任。
2. The tribunal's ruling was praised as thoroughly _gōng míng zhèng dà_.
3. 彼は公明正大な人物として知られている。
4. The judge's decision was seen as _công minh chính đại_, free of any favoritism.

## Sentiment
Strongly positive; a term of praise for fairness, transparency, and moral uprightness. Frequently used aspirationally, as a stated personal or institutional ideal.
