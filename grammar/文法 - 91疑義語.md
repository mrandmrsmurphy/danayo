## Conflicted Words (<ruby>疑義語<rt>ㄧㄜㄧ⼄</rt></ruby>)

Some compounds are built from perfectly legal characters, yet mean different — sometimes opposite — things in different source languages. Unlike a forbidden character ([[文法 - 98違法字]]), a word cannot be made unwritable: anyone can put the characters together. So this page is **a warning list, not a ban list**. It records words where a speaker may be understood to mean something very different from what they intended, says how Dan'a'yo resolves the conflict (if it does), and names a safer alternative.

## Categories

- **Fiat** — The word is usable, but Dan'a'yo imposes one sense by decree (often the mechanical gluing-together of the components, or one source language's sense), because the source languages disagree. Users should still expect some listeners to hear a source-language sense.
- **Split** — The word exists in Dan'a'yo with its divergent senses recorded side by side. Context must disambiguate, and users should expect to be misheard.
- **Avoid** — The word is usable in principle, but the conflict is structural (an auto-antonym or a sense reversal). Prefer a substitute.
- **Not created** — Dan'a'yo has no page for the word, because its pedigree is too thin or too conflicted. A substitute is given.

## Entries

#### Fiat and Avoid

##### 愛人
- Category: Fiat
- Mainland Mandarin àirén is the neutral, standard word for "spouse", not a dating partner. Taiwan and Hong Kong Mandarin lean toward "lover", sometimes illicit. Korean 애인 and Vietnamese người yêu mean a neutral boyfriend or girlfriend. Modern Japanese あいじん means "mistress; the other party in an affair" (a post-WWII narrowing). Calling someone's spouse 愛人 in Japanese would wrongly imply an affair.
- Resolution: a fiat sense of "lover, partner" (love + person). It may be illicit and is probably sexual today.
- Safer: [愛女](../words/愛女.md) "girlfriend" and [愛男](../words/愛男.md) "boyfriend", coined by fiat to sidestep the ambiguity.

##### 丈夫
- Category: Fiat
- Mandarin zhàngfu and Cantonese zoeng6 fu1 mean "husband". Japanese じょうぶ means "robust, sturdy, healthy". Korean uses the native 남편 for "husband"; Sino-Korean 장부 means "a great, manly man". Vietnamese uses the native chồng.
- Resolution: the page's gloss is only "husband", so in practice the Chinese sense is imposed. The Japanese sense is kept as the real reading and noted as a false friend.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 汽車
- Category: Fiat
- Mandarin qìchē is the everyday word for "car". Japanese きしゃ is the older, literal "steam train, locomotive".
- Resolution: a deliberate choice of the Japanese sense ("steam train, locomotive"), the same pattern as 汽水. The Mandarin "car" sense is not imported.
- Safer: [自動車](../words/自動車.md) for "automobile", [列車](../words/列車.md) for general "train".

##### 汽水
- Category: Fiat
- Japanese きすい and Korean 기수 are the hydrology term "brackish water". Mandarin qìshuǐ and Cantonese hei3 seoi2 are everyday "soda pop; soft drink". Vietnamese was not researched.
- Resolution: the page deliberately adopts "brackish water" and notes the Mandarin "soda" sense, so it is the most exposed to misunderstanding in Chinese.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 炭酸 compound for soda. Keep 汽水 for brackish water.

##### 客気
- Category: Avoid
- A genuine Sinosphere auto-antonym. Mandarin 客气, Cantonese haak3 hei3 and Vietnamese khách khí mean "polite, reserved, standing on ceremony". Japanese かっき and Korean 객기 mean "reckless bravado, impetuosity". 客 ("guest, outsider") + 気 ("disposition") names an unstable position that the Sinosphere resolved in two opposite directions.
- Resolution: none. It cannot be formally forbidden, so this is guidance.
- Safer: a disambiguated prefix form, not yet coined — 拘客氣 (the restraint pole) or 浮客氣 (the bravado pole).

#### Split

##### 新聞
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "news", the informational content, and use 报纸 / bou3 zi2 for "newspaper". Japanese しんぶん and Korean 신문 mean "newspaper", the physical periodical. Japanese uses ニュース for "news".
- Resolution: the page documents both senses and does not force agreement. Its gloss lists "news" (zh, yue) first and "newspaper" (ja, ko) second.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 勉強
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese keep the original sense "to force oneself; reluctant, strained, inadequate". Vietnamese miễn cưỡng keeps "reluctant, forced". Japanese べんきょう means "to study", with "give a discount" as a secondary sense.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "to study", "to force oneself" and "reluctant" side by side. The page does not choose between them.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 大家
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "everyone; everybody" (a pronoun). Japanese おおや means "landlord" (its other reading たいか means "master, authority"). Korean 대가 means "master; expert". Vietnamese đại gia is slang for "tycoon". Vietnamese says "everyone" with the native mọi người.
- Resolution: the gloss fixes the headword to "everyone" while the other senses are recorded as real attested words.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 点心
- Category: Split
- Mandarin, Cantonese and Japanese mean "snack; dim sum; light dishes". Vietnamese điểm tâm means "light meal", often breakfast. Korean 점심 means "lunch", one of the most common everyday Korean words.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "snack; dim sum; light dishes" first and "lunch" (Korean) second. A high-stakes false friend at mealtimes.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 方便
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "convenient, suitable", and the word is also a euphemism for relieving oneself. Korean 방편 and Japanese ほうべん mean "expedient means, a makeshift solution", the Buddhist upāya. Vietnamese phương tiện means "means; tool; vehicle".
- Resolution: the gloss has three entries, one per sense cluster, leading with "convenient".
- Safer: none documented.

##### 先生
- Category: Split
- Modern Mandarin xiānsheng chiefly means "Mr., sir", a polite title for any man. "Teacher" is archaic there (老师 is used instead). Japanese せんせい and Korean 선생 mainly mean "teacher". Vietnamese tiên sinh is an honorific covering teacher, respected elder or expert.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "Mr.", "sir", "teacher", leading with the Mandarin sense.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 文章
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "essay, article", never a grammatical "sentence". Japanese ぶんしょう mainly means "writing, composition", with "sentence" secondary. Korean 문장 is specifically the grammar term "sentence". Vietnamese văn chương leans toward "literature".
- Resolution: the gloss is "essay", "article", following the Mandarin and Japanese sense.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 時候
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "time, moment, when" (什么时候, 有时候), one of the most common HSK-1 words. Japanese じこう is "season, time of year" in a formal register (時候の挨拶). Vietnamese uses thời gian for "time".
- Resolution: both senses are listed, leading with "time; moment; when".
- Safer: [時間](../words/時間.md) for "time".

##### 喧嘩
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "commotion, noise, hubbub". Japanese けんか has narrowed to "quarrel, fight" between people. Vietnamese huyên hoa is classical or literary with the general "noisy" sense.
- Resolution: the gloss has only "commotion"; the Japanese narrowing is noted in the Notes. Split is an inference, since the page does not call it a conflict outright.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 大人
- Category: Split
- Japanese おとな is the everyday "adult". Mandarin 大人 is compositional "adult" but in colloquial use often an address for officials in historical contexts (成人 is plainer). Korean 대인 is formal or literary (어른 or 성인 in everyday speech). Vietnamese đại nhân is mainly an archaic honorific ("lord, Your Excellency"), with "adult" only a secondary classical sense; modern Vietnamese says người lớn.
- Resolution: the gloss is "adult", "grown up". The Vietnamese divergence is flagged on the page.
- Safer: 成人 (zh), 어른 or 성인 (ko), người lớn (vi); none is linked to a vault page.

##### 東西
- Category: Split
- A tone-based heteronym in Mandarin: neutral-tone dōngxi is "thing, stuff" (买东西 "to shop"), full-tone dōngxī is "east and west". Japanese とうざい, Korean 동서 and Cantonese dung1 sai1 have only the literal "east and west" sense; their words for "thing" are 物/もの, 것 and 嘢/je5.
- Resolution: the gloss lists both senses, "thing; object; stuff" first.
- Safer: 物/もの, 것, 嘢 (none linked).

##### 入口
- Category: Split
- Mandarin rùkǒu, Cantonese jap6 hau2, Japanese いりぐち and Korean 입구 all mean "entrance". Vietnamese nhập khẩu is the same compound but has drifted to "import" in trade. The everyday Vietnamese word for "entrance" is lối vào or cửa vào.
- Resolution: the page keeps "entrance" and discloses the Vietnamese divergence.
- Safer: lối vào, cửa vào (none linked).

##### 走 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin zǒu is "walk, go on foot" (also "leave", "leak", euphemistically "die"); running is only literary or dialectal. Cantonese zau2 is "leave, flee". Japanese 走る and Korean 주 mean "run". The Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese senses come from general knowledge, not a fetched entry.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "run" (the Japanese and Korean sense) and does not mention the Mandarin "walk". This is a serious clash for a basic verb.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 湯 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin tāng and Cantonese tong1 mean "soup, broth" (also herbal decoction). Japanese ゆ means "hot water; bath water" (銭湯 "bathhouse"). Korean 탕 is a stew or soup, also in bath words. The herbal sense in Vietnamese was not checked.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "hot water" (the Japanese sense).
- Safer: none documented. [熱](../words/熱.md) and [粥](../words/粥.md) exist but do not replace "soup".

##### 娘 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin niáng is colloquially "mother" (also "wife of", as in 新娘). Japanese musume is "daughter; girl". Cantonese noeng4 is "woman, mother" (thinly attested). The Korean and Vietnamese senses were not shown. A mother/daughter reversal is the core conflict.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "young woman", which is neutral between the two.
- Safer: [母親](../words/母親.md) "mother", [少女](../words/少女.md) "girl".

##### 告訴 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin gàosù and Cantonese gou3 sou3 mean "tell, inform", with "file a complaint" as a legal sense. Japanese こくそ and Korean 고소 mean only "accuse, sue", never "tell". Vietnamese was not covered.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is the legal sense only ("accuse", "charge", "file a lawsuit"), so the everyday Mandarin "tell" is unrepresented.
- Safer: [通知](../words/通知.md) for "tell". For "sue": none documented.

##### 約束
- Category: Split
- Mandarin yuēshù and Cantonese joek3 cuk1 mean "restrain, constrain, bind". Japanese やくそく and Korean 약속 mean "promise; appointment". Vietnamese is attested as "restrain". Mandarin uses other words for "to promise".
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "promise".
- Safer: [誓約](../words/誓約.md) "oath, pledge", [約会](../words/約会.md) for an appointment; both partial.

##### 小人 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin xiǎorén and Cantonese siu2 jan4 mean "scoundrel, petty person" (with archaic "I" and "child" senses). Japanese しょうじん shares the "petty person" sense, but 小人 also reads こびと "dwarf" and しょうにん "child" on fare signs. The risk is a neutral Japanese reading being heard as an insult. Korean and Vietnamese were not verified.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is the pejorative sense.
- Safer: [人](../words/人.md) for neutral use. "Dwarf" and "child": none documented.

##### 床
- Category: Split
- Mandarin chuáng and Cantonese cong4 mean "bed" (also frame, chassis, riverbed). Vietnamese sàng and Korean 상 are attested as "bed" (Korean 상 also means a low platform or table). Japanese 床 is ゆか "floor" (and とこ "bed, alcove" in compounds).
- Resolution: the vault gloss is the Chinese "bed" sense.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 工作
- Category: Split
- Mandarin gōngzuò and Cantonese gung1 zok3 mean "work, job". Japanese こうさく is "handicraft, construction" and also "scheming". Korean 공작 is "maneuvering, covert operation". Vietnamese công tác is "task, duty, mission". Saying "my 工作" in Japanese or Korean would sound like a craft or a plot, not a job.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "work; be employed".
- Safer: [作業](../words/作業.md) "work, task".

##### 便宜
- Category: Split
- Mandarin piányi and Cantonese pin4 yi4 mean "cheap" (also "advantage"). Japanese べんぎ and Korean 편의 mean "convenience, expediency, facilities". Vietnamese tiện nghi is "amenities". Mandarin biànyí is the "convenient" reading, so Mandarin itself holds both.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "cheap" (the Mandarin piányi reading).
- Safer: [廉価](../words/廉価.md), [安価](../words/安価.md) for "cheap".

##### 地方
- Category: Split (mild)
- Mandarin dìfang is "place, spot", dìfāng "local, regional". Cantonese is similar. Japanese ちほう and Korean 지방 mean "region, the provinces, outside the capital". The risk is "this place" (Mandarin) heard as "this region" in Japanese or Korean.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "region; territory".
- Safer: [地域](../words/地域.md) for "region"; [所](../words/所.md) or [処](../words/処.md) or [土地](../words/土地.md) for "a place".

##### 人間
- Category: Split
- Mandarin rénjiān, Cantonese jan4 gaan1 and Vietnamese nhân gian mean "the human world, the world of mortals". Japanese にんげん and Korean 인간 mean "human being, person" (Japanese also "character"). A Japanese "good 人間" is a good person, which a Chinese reader would hear as a realm.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is the Chinese and Vietnamese sense.
- Safer: [人](../words/人.md), [人類](../words/人類.md) for humans; [世間](../words/世間.md) for "world".

##### 同志
- Category: Split
- Mandarin, Cantonese (tung4 zi3), Japanese どうし, Korean 동지 and Vietnamese đồng chí all mean "comrade" or "like-minded person". In Chinese-speaking communities (Hong Kong from the 1980s, then Taiwan and the mainland) 同志 also means "gay person". A Japanese, Korean or Vietnamese speaker addressing a "comrade" could be heard by a Chinese speaker as outing someone.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "comrade" and omits the slang.
- Safer: [同伴](../words/同伴.md) "companion", [同僚](../words/同僚.md) "colleague".

##### 看病 †
- Category: Split
- Japanese かんびょう and Korean 간병 mean "to nurse or tend a sick person". Mandarin kànbìng (Cantonese hon3 beng6) means "to see a doctor" (patient) or "to treat a patient" (doctor). A Mandarin speaker may hear a clinic visit where a Japanese speaker means home care.
- Resolution: the page has a dual gloss, but its notes call the compound "clean, directly parallel", which overstates the match. It should carry a caution callout.
- Safer: [看](../words/看.md) + [護](../words/護.md) *(護 page not checked)*, or [診](../words/診.md) for "examine a patient".

##### 馬鹿
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin 马鹿 mǎlù and Cantonese maa5 luk6 mean "horse and deer; red deer". Japanese ばか means "fool", written with ateji characters chosen for sound (probably from Sanskrit *moha*). Korean and Vietnamese have no native reading for the compound: Korean 빠가 is a loan of the Japanese sound, and the native Korean for red deer is 말사슴. The "red deer" sense has pedigree in only two of the five languages.
- Resolution: no page. The Japanese "fool" sense is an ateji with no Sino-reading pedigree.
- Safer: [愚](../words/愚.md) "foolish".

##### 手紙
- Category: Not created
- Japanese てがみ means "letter". Mandarin 手紙 shǒuzhǐ is a dialectal word for "toilet paper", an unrelated false friend. Korean and Vietnamese don't use it for "letter".
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [書簡](../words/書簡.md).

##### 老婆 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin lǎopo and Cantonese lou5 po4 are colloquial "wife" (also internet-slang "waifu"). Japanese ろうば is "old woman". Korean 노파 and Vietnamese lão bà are also "old woman" but were not verified. Calling someone's wife an "old woman" reads as an insult elsewhere.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [妻](../words/妻.md) "wife".

##### 大丈夫
- Category: Not created
- Japanese だいじょうぶ means "all right; okay; unharmed". Korean 대장부 and Vietnamese đại trượng phu mean "a real man; a man of character", as does classical Chinese. The Japanese "okay" sense has been borrowed back into Chinese as internet and ACG slang.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [安全](../words/安全.md) "safe".

##### 迷惑
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin míhuò is "confused; to confuse". Korean 미혹 is "delusion". Vietnamese mê hoặc is "to bewitch". Japanese めいわく is "nuisance; trouble caused to others".
- Resolution: no page; [迷](../words/迷.md) and [惑](../words/惑.md) exist as characters.
- Safer: [困難](../words/困難.md), a partial fit. A better match for "nuisance" would be an uncoined 麻煩, so this is a judgment call.

##### 結構
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin, Korean and Vietnamese use it as the noun "structure". Japanese けっこう also means "splendid; fine", "quite; fairly", and "no thank you". Only Japanese uses it as an evaluation or adverb.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [構造](../words/構造.md) "structure"; [頗](../words/頗.md) or [十分](../words/十分.md) for "quite".

##### 愚痴 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin yúchī is "stupid; foolish". Japanese ぐち is a noun meaning "complaint; grumbling". Korean and Vietnamese were not verified.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [不平](../words/不平.md) "grievance" (gloss not read), [愚](../words/愚.md) for "foolish".

##### 工夫
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin gōngfu is "time and effort; acquired skill; free time". Japanese くふう is "a device; ingenuity" (and こうふ "labourer"). Korean 공부 is "study".
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [努力](../words/努力.md) for effort; for ingenuity, an uncoined 巧思.

##### 情人 †
- Category: Not created
- Mainland Mandarin qíngrén is typically the partner in an extramarital affair, except in 情人節 (Valentine's Day). Other Mandarin usage and Japanese じょうじん are more neutral: "sweetheart; lover". Vietnamese tình nhân is "lover". No Korean sense was found.
- Resolution: no page. Compare the fiat resolution of [愛人](../words/愛人.md).
- Safer: [恋](../words/恋.md) "love".

##### 下手
- Category: Not created
- Japanese へた is "unskilled; poor at" (したて, しもて mean "lower part; stage left"). Mandarin xiàshǒu is a verb meaning "to set about; take action (even to commit a crime)", or a noun "next player; assistant". They are nearly opposites: "poor at" versus "start doing".
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [拙劣](../words/拙劣.md) "clumsy"; for "start", an uncoined 著手.

##### 天井 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese てんじょう is "ceiling" (also "the top price"). Mandarin tiānjǐng is a "courtyard atrium; patio" or "skylight", nearly the opposite: an opening rather than a cover.
- Resolution: no page. Do not use [天花](../words/天花.md) for "ceiling", since that page is glossed "snowflake".
- Safer: *(uncoined)* 天板, or describe it with [屋](../words/屋.md).

##### 丁寧 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese ていねい is "polite; courteous; careful". Chinese 丁寧 is a variant of 叮嚀 dīngníng, "to exhort repeatedly" (also a bell-shaped battle instrument). The Japanese sense does not exist in Chinese. Korean and Vietnamese were not checked.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [鄭重](../words/鄭重.md) "courteous" (gloss not read), or [注意](../words/注意.md) "careful".

##### 放心 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin fàngxīn is "to be at ease; stop worrying". Japanese ほうしん is "in a trance; absent-minded". Korean 방심 is "carelessness, letting one's guard down". The Mandarin and Korean senses are near-opposites: "all is well" against "you were careless". Vietnamese was not verified.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [安心](../words/安心.md) "peace of mind".

##### 暗算 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin ànsuàn is "to plot against; to scheme", noun "a trap". Japanese あんざん is "mental arithmetic" (Taiwanese Hokkien shares it, so the Chinese sense is not uniform). Korean and Vietnamese were not checked.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* 心算 for mental arithmetic, 陰謀 for a plot.

##### 検討 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese 検討 and Korean 검토 mean "consideration; to examine", a neutral business word. Mandarin jiǎntǎo means "to examine; review" and also "to criticise oneself", a common mainland sense. The conflict is partial. Vietnamese and Cantonese were not checked.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 考 compound such as 考慮; check for an existing page first.

##### 得意 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin déyì is "successful" but often pejorative: "smug". Cantonese adds "cute; interesting". Japanese とくい is "skilled; one's strong suit", also "regular customer". Korean 득의 is "satisfaction, pride". Calling someone 得意 reads as "skilled" in Japanese and "smug" in Mandarin.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 巧 or 上手 compound.

##### 小姐 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin xiǎojiě and Cantonese síu2 jé2 mean "Miss; young lady", and are also dated slang for "prostitute", which made the term risky on the mainland. Korean 소저 is archaic "young lady". Vietnamese tiểu thư is believed to mean "young lady; rich man's daughter" but was not verified. Japanese does not use it as a living word.
- Resolution: no page. Avoid it as an address term.
- Safer: *(uncoined)*. [姉](../words/姉.md) is only for an elder sister.

##### 先輩
- Category: Not created
- Japanese せんぱい and Korean 선배 mean "senior in the same school or workplace", also an address term. Chinese 先辈 xiānbèi is "older generation; predecessor; ancestor", about the dead or the past, not a current senior peer. A real but moderate mismatch.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)*; a compound of 前 and 輩 could be coined (check that [輩](../words/輩.md) and [前](../words/前.md) exist).

##### 経理 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese けいり is "accounting; bookkeeping", a department, not a rank. Mandarin jīnglǐ is "manager; director". Korean 경리 (accounting) and Vietnamese kinh lý (manager) are remembered but unconfirmed. A message to "the 経理" reaches the accountant in Japan and the boss in China.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 会計 compound for accountant; 管理 or a [長](../words/長.md) compound for manager.

##### 出世 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese しゅっせ and Korean 출세 mean "success in life; rising in rank". Mandarin chūshì is "to be born; to come into the world", and in Buddhism "to transcend worldliness". Vietnamese was not checked.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [成](../words/成.md) in a compound, or 成功 (check for a page).

##### 百姓
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin bǎixìng (literally "hundred surnames"), Korean 백성 and Vietnamese bách tính mean "the common people". Japanese ひゃくしょう is historically "peasant; farmer" (百姓一揆 "peasant uprising"). The conflict is "populace in general" against "farmer".
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 民 compound such as 庶民; check for an existing page first.

##### 心中 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin xīnzhōng is "in one's heart; in one's mind". Japanese しんじゅう is "lovers' double suicide", while しんちゅう is "one's innermost thoughts". The しんじゅう reading is the dangerous one for a Japanese reader. Korean and Vietnamese were not verified.
- Resolution: no page. [心](../words/心.md) and [中](../words/中.md) exist on their own.
- Safer: [心](../words/心.md) alone; for "in the heart", [心](../words/心.md) with 內.

##### 人参 †
- Category: Not created
- Japanese にんじん is "carrot", with ginseng as the older or medicinal sense (朝鮮人参 needs a qualifier). Mandarin rénshēn, Korean 인삼 and Vietnamese nhân sâm mean "ginseng". Japanese is from Wiktionary; the others are from general knowledge.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* separate compounds for "carrot" and "ginseng" (for example 高麗參 for ginseng).

##### 麻雀 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin máquè is "sparrow". Japanese まあじゃん (麻雀) is "mahjong" (the bird is 雀 すずめ). 麻雀 was also the original Cantonese, Hakka and Southern Min name for mahjong, while Standard Mandarin writes 麻將. Korean and Vietnamese were not verified.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: [鳥](../words/鳥.md) compounds for the animal; 麻將 for the game.

##### 飯店 †
- Category: Not created
- Mandarin fàndiàn is "hotel" in Taiwan (Hong Kong uses 酒店) and also "restaurant". Japanese はんてん and Korean 반점 mean "Chinese restaurant". A traveller booking a 飯店 gets a bed in Mandarin and a meal in Japanese or Korean. Vietnamese was not verified.
- Resolution: no page.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* 旅館 for hotel, 食堂 for restaurant; check for existing pages first.

## Adding an entry

A word belongs here when its source-language senses differ enough that a speaker of one could be badly misunderstood by a speaker of another. Mere differences in nuance or register do not qualify; those stay in the word page's Notes. Put the per-language breakdown on the word page itself (as a `>[!caution]` callout if the word is usable), then add a row to the Master List and a short entry here. Mark anything not checked against a dictionary with †.
