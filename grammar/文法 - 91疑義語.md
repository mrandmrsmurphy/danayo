## Conflicted Words (<ruby>疑義語<rt>ㄧㄜㄧ⼄</rt></ruby>)

Some compounds are built from perfectly legal characters, yet mean different — sometimes opposite — things in different source languages. Unlike a forbidden character ([[文法 - 98違法字]]), a word cannot be made unwritable: anyone can put the characters together. So this page is **a warning list, not a ban list**. It records words where a speaker may be understood to mean something very different from what they intended, says how Dan'a'yo resolves the conflict (if it does), and names a safer alternative.

## Categories

- **Fiat** — The word is usable, but Dan'a'yo imposes one sense by decree (often the mechanical gluing-together of the components, or one source language's sense), because the source languages disagree. Users should still expect some listeners to hear a source-language sense.
- **Split** — The word exists in Dan'a'yo with its divergent senses recorded side by side. Context must disambiguate, and users should expect to be misheard.
- **Avoid** — The word is usable in principle, but the conflict is structural (an auto-antonym or a sense reversal). Prefer a substitute.
- **Not created** — Dan'a'yo has no page for the word, because its pedigree is too thin or too conflicted. A substitute is given.

## Entries

#### Fiat and Avoid

##### 愛人
- Category: Fiat
- Mainland Mandarin àirén is the neutral, standard word for "spouse", not a dating partner. Taiwan and Hong Kong Mandarin lean toward "lover", sometimes illicit. Korean 애인 and Vietnamese người yêu mean a neutral boyfriend or girlfriend. Modern Japanese あいじん means "mistress; the other party in an affair" (a post-WWII narrowing). Calling someone's spouse 愛人 in Japanese would wrongly imply an affair.
- Resolution: a fiat sense of "lover, partner" (love + person). It may be illicit and is probably sexual today.
- Safer: [愛女](../words/愛女.md) "girlfriend" and [愛男](../words/愛男.md) "boyfriend", coined by fiat to sidestep the ambiguity.

##### 丈夫
- Category: Fiat
- Mandarin zhàngfu and Cantonese zoeng6 fu1 mean "husband". Japanese じょうぶ means "robust, sturdy, healthy". Korean uses the native 남편 for "husband"; Sino-Korean 장부 means "a great, manly man". Vietnamese uses the native chồng.
- Resolution: the page's gloss is only "husband", so in practice the Chinese sense is imposed. The Japanese sense is kept as the real reading and noted as a false friend.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 汽車
- Category: Fiat
- Mandarin qìchē is the everyday word for "car". Japanese きしゃ is the older, literal "steam train, locomotive".
- Resolution: a deliberate choice of the Japanese sense ("steam train, locomotive"), the same pattern as 汽水. The Mandarin "car" sense is not imported.
- Safer: [自動車](../words/自動車.md) for "automobile", [列車](../words/列車.md) for general "train".

##### 汽水
- Category: Fiat
- Japanese きすい and Korean 기수 are the hydrology term "brackish water". Mandarin qìshuǐ and Cantonese hei3 seoi2 are everyday "soda pop; soft drink". Vietnamese was not researched.
- Resolution: the page deliberately adopts "brackish water" and notes the Mandarin "soda" sense, so it is the most exposed to misunderstanding in Chinese.
- Safer: *(uncoined)* a 炭酸 compound for soda. Keep 汽水 for brackish water.

##### 客気
- Category: Avoid
- A genuine Sinosphere auto-antonym. Mandarin 客气, Cantonese haak3 hei3 and Vietnamese khách khí mean "polite, reserved, standing on ceremony". Japanese かっき and Korean 객기 mean "reckless bravado, impetuosity". 客 ("guest, outsider") + 気 ("disposition") names an unstable position that the Sinosphere resolved in two opposite directions.
- Resolution: none. It cannot be formally forbidden, so this is guidance.
- Safer: a disambiguated prefix form, not yet coined — 拘客氣 (the restraint pole) or 浮客氣 (the bravado pole).

##### 初心
- Category: Avoid
- Japanese しょしん is the "beginner's state of mind" and, by extension, a beginner (初心者 is the usual noun; 初心忘るべからず "never forget your beginner's mind"). Mandarin chūxīn and Cantonese co1 sam1 mean "original intention, one's first aspiration" (不忘初心, "don't forget why you started"). Korean 초심 and Vietnamese sơ tâm lean toward the Chinese sense, "original resolve".
- Resolution: none. The page records both senses, but the conflict is structural, and a Chinese or Japanese reader will each take it the other way.
- Safer: [新手](../words/新手.md) "beginner, novice"; [新人](../words/新人.md) "newcomer, rookie" and [学習者](../words/学習者.md) "learner" also exist.

#### Split

##### 新聞
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "news", the informational content, and use 报纸 / bou3 zi2 for "newspaper". Japanese しんぶん and Korean 신문 mean "newspaper", the physical periodical. Japanese uses ニュース for "news".
- Resolution: the page documents both senses and does not force agreement. Its gloss lists "news" (zh, yue) first and "newspaper" (ja, ko) second.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 勉強
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese keep the original sense "to force oneself; reluctant, strained, inadequate". Vietnamese miễn cưỡng keeps "reluctant, forced". Japanese べんきょう means "to study", with "give a discount" as a secondary sense.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "to study", "to force oneself" and "reluctant" side by side. The page does not choose between them.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 大家
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "everyone; everybody" (a pronoun). Japanese おおや means "landlord" (its other reading たいか means "master, authority"). Korean 대가 means "master; expert". Vietnamese đại gia is slang for "tycoon". Vietnamese says "everyone" with the native mọi người.
- Resolution: the gloss fixes the headword to "everyone" while the other senses are recorded as real attested words.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 点心
- Category: Split
- Mandarin, Cantonese and Japanese mean "snack; dim sum; light dishes". Vietnamese điểm tâm means "light meal", often breakfast. Korean 점심 means "lunch", one of the most common everyday Korean words.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "snack; dim sum; light dishes" first and "lunch" (Korean) second. A high-stakes false friend at mealtimes.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 方便
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "convenient, suitable", and the word is also a euphemism for relieving oneself. Korean 방편 and Japanese ほうべん mean "expedient means, a makeshift solution", the Buddhist upāya. Vietnamese phương tiện means "means; tool; vehicle".
- Resolution: the gloss has three entries, one per sense cluster, leading with "convenient".
- Safer: none documented.

##### 先生
- Category: Split
- Modern Mandarin xiānsheng chiefly means "Mr., sir", a polite title for any man. "Teacher" is archaic there (老师 is used instead). Japanese せんせい and Korean 선생 mainly mean "teacher". Vietnamese tiên sinh is an honorific covering teacher, respected elder or expert.
- Resolution: the gloss lists "Mr.", "sir", "teacher", leading with the Mandarin sense.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 文章
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "essay, article", never a grammatical "sentence". Japanese ぶんしょう mainly means "writing, composition", with "sentence" secondary. Korean 문장 is specifically the grammar term "sentence". Vietnamese văn chương leans toward "literature".
- Resolution: the gloss is "essay", "article", following the Mandarin and Japanese sense.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 時候
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "time, moment, when" (什么时候, 有时候), one of the most common HSK-1 words. Japanese じこう is "season, time of year" in a formal register (時候の挨拶). Vietnamese uses thời gian for "time".
- Resolution: both senses are listed, leading with "time; moment; when".
- Safer: [時間](../words/時間.md) for "time".

##### 喧嘩
- Category: Split
- Mandarin and Cantonese mean "commotion, noise, hubbub". Japanese けんか has narrowed to "quarrel, fight" between people. Vietnamese huyên hoa is classical or literary with the general "noisy" sense.
- Resolution: the gloss has only "commotion"; the Japanese narrowing is noted in the Notes. Split is an inference, since the page does not call it a conflict outright.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 大人
- Category: Split
- Japanese おとな is the everyday "adult". Mandarin 大人 is compositional "adult" but in colloquial use often an address for officials in historical contexts (成人 is plainer). Korean 대인 is formal or literary (어른 or 성인 in everyday speech). Vietnamese đại nhân is mainly an archaic honorific ("lord, Your Excellency"), with "adult" only a secondary classical sense; modern Vietnamese says người lớn.
- Resolution: the gloss is "adult", "grown up". The Vietnamese divergence is flagged on the page.
- Safer: 成人 (zh), 어른 or 성인 (ko), người lớn (vi); none is linked to a vault page.

##### 東西
- Category: Split
- A tone-based heteronym in Mandarin: neutral-tone dōngxi is "thing, stuff" (买东西 "to shop"), full-tone dōngxī is "east and west". Japanese とうざい, Korean 동서 and Cantonese dung1 sai1 have only the literal "east and west" sense; their words for "thing" are 物/もの, 것 and 嘢/je5.
- Resolution: the gloss lists both senses, "thing; object; stuff" first.
- Safer: 物/もの, 것, 嘢 (none linked).

##### 入口
- Category: Split
- Mandarin rùkǒu, Cantonese jap6 hau2, Japanese いりぐち and Korean 입구 all mean "entrance". Vietnamese nhập khẩu is the same compound but has drifted to "import" in trade. The everyday Vietnamese word for "entrance" is lối vào or cửa vào.
- Resolution: the page keeps "entrance" and discloses the Vietnamese divergence.
- Safer: lối vào, cửa vào (none linked).

##### 走 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin zǒu is "walk, go on foot" (also "leave", "leak", euphemistically "die"); running is only literary or dialectal. Cantonese zau2 is "leave, flee". Japanese 走る and Korean 주 mean "run". The Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese senses come from general knowledge, not a fetched entry.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "run" (the Japanese and Korean sense) and does not mention the Mandarin "walk". This is a serious clash for a basic verb.
- Safer: none documented.

##### 湯 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin tāng and Cantonese tong1 mean "soup, broth" (also herbal decoction). Japanese ゆ means "hot water; bath water" (銭湯 "bathhouse"). Korean 탕 is a stew or soup, also in bath words. The herbal sense in Vietnamese was not checked.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "hot water" (the Japanese sense).
- Safer: none documented. [熱](../words/熱.md) and [粥](../words/粥.md) exist but do not replace "soup".

##### 娘 †
- Category: Split
- Mandarin niáng is colloquially "mother" (also "wife of", as in 新娘). Japanese musume is "daughter; girl". Cantonese noeng4 is "woman, mother" (thinly attested). The Korean and Vietnamese senses were not shown. A mother/daughter reversal is the core conflict.
- Resolution: the vault gloss is "young woman", which is neutral between the two.

## Adding an entry

A word belongs here when its source-language senses differ enough that a speaker of one could be badly misunderstood by a speaker of another. Mere differences in nuance or register do not qualify; those stay in the word page's Notes. Put the per-language breakdown on the word page itself (as a `>[!caution]` callout if the word is usable), then add a row to the Master List and a short entry here. Mark anything not checked against a dictionary with †.
